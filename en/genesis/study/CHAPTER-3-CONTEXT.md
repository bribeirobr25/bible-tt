## A. Hebrew Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. The *arum/arom* wordplay — the hinge between chapters
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis 2:25: *arom* (naked). Genesis 3:1: *arum* (crafty/shrewd). Same consonants, different vocalization. This is the deliberate sonic link between the two chapters — the TT flags it in both locations. The wordplay is untranslatable in EN/PT/DE; the note system makes it visible.

### A2. The serpent uses "Elohim" — never "YHWH Elohim"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The narrator consistently uses *YHWH Elohim* throughout Gen 2–3 (vv. 1, 8, 9, 13, 14, 21, 22, 23). But in dialogue, the serpent (vv. 1, 5) and the woman (v. 3) use only *Elohim*. The human in dialogue uses no divine name at all (vv. 10, 12). The TT preserves this distinction because it renders YHWH consonantally rather than collapsing to "the LORD God." The narrative significance is POSSIBLE but undetermined: the serpent/woman may speak outside the YHWH-covenant register.

### A3. The woman adds "touch" — and softens the death clause
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Original command (2:16–17): prohibition is eating; consequence is "dying you shall die" (infinitive absolute). In 3:3, the woman adds "you shall not touch it" and changes the death clause to "lest you die" (*pen temutun* — a consequence clause weaker than the original emphatic). The TT preserves the structural difference between 2:17 and 3:3 so readers can see the shift.

### A4. Negated infinitive absolute — the serpent's maximum-force contradiction
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis 3:4: *lo mot temutun* — "not dying you shall die." This directly negates 2:17's *mot tamut* ("dying you shall die") at maximum grammatical force, using the same emphatic construction. The TT preserves both structures so the inversion is visible. Traditional smooth renderings ("you shall not surely die") blur the grammatical parallel.

### A5. "Like God/gods" — genuinely ambiguous
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis 3:5 and 3:22 both use *ke-elohim*. Since *Elohim* is grammatically plural but used for the singular God throughout Genesis, the ambiguity is genuinely active here: "like God" (one) or "like gods/divine beings" (plural). The TT preserves both via slash in all three languages.

### A6. *qol* — voice or sound?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis 3:8: "they heard the *qol* of YHWH Elohim walking." Hebrew *qol* ranges from "voice" to "sound" to "thunder." The *qol* of someone *walking* is more naturally a sound than a voice. But in 3:10, the human says "your *qol* I heard" — there, "voice" is more natural. The TT uses "voice" consistently (Rule 1) with a note at 3:8 flagging the broader range.

### A7. Only the serpent and the ground are cursed — not the humans
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

*Arur* (cursed) is applied to the serpent (3:14) and the ground (3:17). The human and the woman are addressed, warned, and given consequences — but they are never called *arur*. Popular tradition blurs this distinction; the TT preserves it.

### A8. YHWH Elohim confirms the serpent's prediction
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis 3:5 (serpent): "you shall be like God/gods, knowing good and evil." Genesis 3:22 (YHWH Elohim): "the human has become like one of us to know good and evil." The text allows the serpent's prediction to stand as partially accurate at the narrative level. The TT note at 3:22 flags this resonance without resolving it.

### A9. The broken sentence — aposiopesis at 3:22
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis 3:22: "and now, lest he stretch out his hand and take also from the tree of the life, and eat, and live forever…" The sentence breaks off unfinished. The TT preserves this with an ellipsis. The label "aposiopesis" (deliberate speaker trailing-off) is more precise than "anacoluthon" (syntactic irregularity).

### A10. "Her seed" — grammatically unusual
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis 3:15: *zar'ah* — "her seed." Attribution of *zera* (seed/offspring) to a female line is rare in the Hebrew Bible. The observation is philological. The TT notes flag this as unusual without extending to later Christian readings (virgin-birth foreshadowing) that are post-biblical.

### A11. Eyes opened — serpent's prediction fulfilled but content differs
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Serpent predicts (3:5): "your eyes shall be opened, and you shall be like God/gods, knowing good and evil." Fulfilled at 3:7: "the eyes of both of them were opened, and they knew that they were naked." The prediction is **structurally accurate** (eyes opened) but **experientially different** — they gain not divine power but awareness of nakedness/vulnerability. The serpent's half-truth is that the mechanics are correct while the experience is not what was implied. The text lets this tension stand.

### A12. Command distortion — the woman's reformulation
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Original command (2:16–17): "from every tree of the garden eating you shall eat. But from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, you shall not eat from it, for in the day of your eating from it dying you shall die." The woman's version (3:2–3): adds "you shall not touch it" and softens the consequence from "dying you shall die" (infinitive absolute) to "lest you die" (consequence clause). The text records this distortion without evaluating it — whether it is forgetfulness, deliberate revision, or theological hedging is left to the reader.

### A13. Curse as cosmic state vs. consequence as individual penalty
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

*Arur* (cursed) is a **cosmic placement** — the serpent is placed into a cursed state among creatures (3:14); the ground is placed into a cursed state because of the human (3:17). The human and woman receive **consequences** (toil, pain, mortality, displacement) but are never called *arur*. This distinction between curse (structural/cosmic) and consequence (personal/experiential) is textually clear but popularly blurred. The TT preserves it.

### A14. Mortality as intrinsic — "dust you ARE"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis 3:19: "dust you *are*, and to dust you shall return." The verb is **present tense** — not "dust you shall become." The text frames mortality as an **intrinsic condition stated**, not a punishment newly imposed. The dust-frame opened at 2:7 ("formed the human, dust from the ground") is closed here: the human was always dust; now that fact is articulated. Whether mortality existed before the eating or only became operative afterward is a later theological question the text does not resolve.

### A15. "Where are you?" — the one-word divine question
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis 3:9: **אַיֶּכָּה** (*ayyekkah*) — a single Hebrew word constituting YHWH Elohim's first question to the human. The most famous one-word question in Genesis. It is rhetorical rather than informational (the narrator assumes divine knowledge of the human's location). The TT preserves it literally ("Where are you?") with a note flagging the rhetorical dimension. This question inaugurates the interrogation sequence (3:9–13) that assigns responsibility without imposing punishment — the curses follow separately (3:14–19).

### A15b. God asks before humans ask — the pattern of divine inquiry
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The first questions in the Bible are all divine:
1. Gen 3:9 — "Where are you?" (*ayyekkah*)
2. Gen 3:11 — "Who told you that you are naked?"
3. Gen 3:13 — "What is this you have done?"
4. Gen 4:6 — "Why does it burn to you?"
5. Gen 4:9 — "Where is your brother?"

The first **human** question is Cain's response at Gen 4:9b: "Am I my brother's keeper?" — and it is evasive. God initiates inquiry; the human's first question is a deflection of responsibility.

The pattern: divine questions seek truth and accountability ("Where? Who? What? Why?"). The first human question avoids both.

### A16. "Wind of the day" — not "cool of the day"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis 3:8: *leruach hayom* — literally "at/to the wind of the day." Traditional translations render "in the cool of the day" (interpreting *ruach* as "breeze" and inferring evening). The TT renders literally per Rule 12 (no false precision). The Hebrew says only "wind of the day" — whether this is morning, afternoon, evening, or a specific meteorological moment is not stated. The note flags the traditional interpretation as one POSSIBLE reading without adopting it.

### A17. Naming of Chava — wordplay with "living"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis 3:20: "And the human called the name of his woman **Chava**, for she was mother of all the **living**." The name חַוָּה (*Chavvah*) resembles the root חַי (*chay*, living). The TT transliterates *Chava* (not "Eve" — which comes via Greek *Εὔα* and Latin *Eva*, losing the Hebrew sound-connection). This is the first point in the narrative where the woman receives a personal name; until now she has been *ishah* (woman). The naming happens after the expulsion sentences — she is individualized at the threshold of exile.

### A17b. The word "sin" is absent from the entire Eden narrative
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The Hebrew word for sin — **חַטָּאת** (*chatat*) — does not appear anywhere in Genesis 2:4–3:24. The serpent, the eating, the consequences, the curses, the expulsion — all happen without the text ever calling what occurred a "sin." The word first enters the Bible at Genesis 4:7, personified ("sin crouches at the door"), aimed at Cain — not at Adam or Eve.

This means: the entire theological framework of "the Fall" and "original sin" is constructed on a narrative that **does not use the word**. The text describes disobedience, consequence, exile, and broken relationship — but it does not label these with the term *chatat*. Whether this absence is significant or incidental is for the reader to assess. The text is what it is.

### A18. East as direction of displacement
**[TEXTUAL / POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE]**

Genesis 3:24: cherubim placed "at the east of the garden of Eden." In biblical geography, east often functions as the direction of **exile and displacement**: Cain goes "east of Eden" (4:16); the tower-builders migrate "from the east" (11:2); Lot chooses the eastern plain (13:11). Whether "east" carries symbolic weight here (direction away from divine presence) or is simply geographic is POSSIBLE but unresolved.

## B. Ancient Near Eastern Parallels

### B1. Serpent iconography in ANE
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

Serpents appear widely in ANE literature and art:
- **Gilgamesh Epic** (Tablet XI): a serpent steals the plant of eternal youth from Gilgamesh — loss of immortality through a serpent, parallel to Genesis 3
- **Egyptian tradition:** serpents as both protective (uraeus) and threatening (Apophis)
- **Mesopotamian art:** serpents associated with wisdom, healing, and underworld

Genesis 3 shares the serpent-as-agent-of-lost-immortality motif with Gilgamesh but diverges: in Genesis the serpent speaks and deceives; in Gilgamesh it simply steals.

**Source:** Charlesworth, J.H., *The Good and Evil Serpent*, 2010.

### B2. Loss of innocence / expulsion from paradise
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

- **Enkidu's civilizing** (Gilgamesh Epic, Tablet I): Enkidu, a wild man, gains knowledge through sexual encounter, loses his harmony with animals, and enters civilization. Structural parallel to the human gaining knowledge, losing garden access, and entering labored existence.
- **Adapa myth:** Adapa is offered immortality by the gods but refuses (or is tricked into refusing) — another "near miss" of eternal life.

These parallels suggest a shared ANE motif of humans approaching but not attaining divine status/immortality. Genesis 3 belongs to this conversation but is not reducible to any single parallel.

**Source:** Moran, W.L., "The Creation of Man in Atrahasis I 192–248," *BASOR* 200 (1970): 48–56.

### B3. The tree of life in Mesopotamian art
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

Stylized "sacred trees" appear throughout Mesopotamian art, often flanked by guardian figures (cf. Genesis 3:24 cherubim). These are attested in Assyrian palace reliefs, cylinder seals, and temple decorations. Whether they represent a "tree of life" concept parallel to Genesis is debated; the visual parallel is undeniable.

**Source:** Parpola, S., "The Assyrian Tree of Life," *JNES* 52 (1993): 161–208.

## C. Historical and Archaeological Context

### C1. Cherubim as guardian figures
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

*Keruvim* (cherubim) are composite guardian figures — not the winged children of later Christian art. Archaeologically, they correspond to ANE guardian creatures:
- Assyrian *lamassu*: human-headed winged bulls/lions flanking palace entrances
- Egyptian sphinxes: lion-bodied guardians
- Ivory cherubim from Samaria and Megiddo (Iron Age Israel)

Genesis 3:24 places cherubim east of Eden to "guard the way of the tree of the life" — a guardian function consistent with ANE architectural tradition.

**Source:** Mettinger, T.N.D., "Cherubim," *DDD* (Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible), 1999, pp. 189–192.

### C2. Clothing and covering in ancient culture
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Genesis 3:7 (fig-leaf coverings by humans) → 3:21 (skin garments by YHWH Elohim). In ANE culture, clothing symbolized social status and civilization. The transition from self-made minimal covering to divinely provided full garments may signal the transition from garden-state to civilized existence.

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. *nachash* (נָחָשׁ) — serpent and divination
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The consonantal root נ-ח-שׁ (*n-ch-sh*) connects to both "serpent" (*nachash*, noun) and "divination/enchantment" (*nichesh*, verb — cf. Gen 44:5, Num 23:23). Whether the text intends a resonance between the serpent-creature and the practice of divination is POSSIBLE but unconfirmed. The TT translates "serpent" (direct meaning) and does not transliterate *nachash* (it doesn't cross the Rule 4 threshold).

### D2. *shuf* (שׁוּף) — a genuinely rare verb
**[TEXTUAL — UNCERTAIN]**

Appears only 3 times in the Hebrew Bible (Gen 3:15, Job 9:17, Ps 139:11). Meaning ranges from "bruise" to "crush" to "strike" to "snap at." The TT uses "bruise/strike" with a slash in all three languages. Crucially, the SAME verb form is used for both actions (head and heel) — traditional translations that differentiate ("crush the head / bruise the heel") are importing interpretation.

### D3. *teshuqah* (תְּשׁוּקָה) — three occurrences, three contexts
**[TEXTUAL — UNCERTAIN]**

Only 3 occurrences in the Hebrew Bible:
1. Genesis 3:16 — woman's *teshuqah* toward her man
2. Genesis 4:7 — sin's *teshuqah* toward Cain
3. Song of Songs 7:11 — the beloved's *teshuqah* toward the speaker

Meaning ranges from "desire" to "turning/orientation/impulse." The Gen 4:7 parallel (sin's *teshuqah* "for you, but you must rule over it") colors the Gen 3:16 reading but does not resolve it. The TT preserves both options via "desire/turning" slash.

### D4. *itsabon* (עִצָּבוֹן) — same root for woman and man
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The same root (ע-צ-ב) appears in both 3:16 (woman's toil/pain in childbearing) and 3:17 (human's toil in working the ground). The TT preserves this echo by using the same English root "toil" in both verses. Traditional translations often split this ("pain" for the woman, "toil" for the man), which erases a deliberate Hebrew parallel.

## E. Scientific Correspondence and Non-Correspondence

### E1. Mortality — process or event?
**[SCIENTIFIC COMPARISON — for awareness]**

Genesis 2:17 warns "dying you shall die." Genesis 3:19 states "dust you are and to dust you shall return." Modern biology understands death as a process inherent to cellular life (telomere shortening, entropy). The text presents mortality as a *condition* stated after the eating, not necessarily as an instantaneous event. Whether this means "mortality activated" or "spiritual death" or "process begun" is a theological question the Hebrew does not resolve (the TT note at 2:17 lists four POSSIBLE interpretations).

### E2. Pain in childbirth — biological or narrative?
**[SCIENTIFIC COMPARISON — for awareness]**

Genesis 3:16: "in toil you shall bear sons." Human childbirth is objectively more difficult than most mammals' (due to bipedal pelvic structure vs. neonatal head size). Whether the text "explains" this biological reality or uses it narratively is the reader's question, not the text's answer.

## F. Later Reception in Other Traditions

### F1. Jewish reception — no "Fall" doctrine
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

Rabbinic Judaism does not develop a "Fall" or "original sin" doctrine from Genesis 3. Instead:
- The *yetzer hara* (evil inclination) is understood as part of human nature, not as a consequence of Adam's sin transmitted to all humans
- Genesis 3 is read as a narrative of human maturation and consequence, not cosmic corruption
- Responsibility is individual, not inherited

This is one of the sharpest Jewish-Christian divergences on the same text.

**Source:** Levenson, J.D., *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*, 1993, ch. 8.

### F2. Christian reception — "the Fall" and original sin
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

- **Paul** (Romans 5:12–21): "through one man sin entered the world" — Genesis 3 read as the origin of universal human sinfulness
- **Augustine** (4th–5th c.): develops the doctrine of *original sin* — all humanity inherits Adam's guilt through biological descent
- **Irenaeus** (2nd c.): reads Gen 3:15 as *protoevangelium* (first gospel) — "he shall bruise your head" interpreted as Christ defeating Satan

These readings are **LATER RECEPTION** — they are theologically significant but post-biblical. The Hebrew text of Genesis 3 does not contain the words "fall," "sin" (the noun first appears at Gen 4:7), "Satan," or "original sin." The TT front matter explicitly excludes this vocabulary from the translation.

### F3. Islamic reception — Adam repents, no original sin
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

Quran (Surah 2:35–39, 7:19–25, 20:115–123): Adam and his wife eat from the forbidden tree, are expelled from the garden, but Adam repents and is forgiven. No doctrine of inherited sin. The serpent (*Iblis*/Satan in Quran) is explicitly identified as a jinn — a departure from the Genesis text where the serpent is "a living thing of the field."

**Source:** Tottoli, R., *Biblical Prophets in the Qur'an and Muslim Literature*, 2002.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. The serpent's question — grammatically ambiguous
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis 3:1: *af ki amar elohim* — the opening words of the serpent's question are syntactically difficult. Three readings are POSSIBLE: (1) interrogative "Has God really said...?"; (2) incredulous "So God has said...!"; (3) conditional "Even though God said..." The TT renders "Indeed, has God said" and notes the ambiguity.

### G2. No "paradise" in the Hebrew
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The Hebrew word *pardes* (garden/park, from Persian) does not appear in Genesis 1–3. The text says *gan eden* (garden of Eden). "Paradise" enters via Greek *paradeisos* (LXX translation of *gan*) and becomes standard in Western tradition. The TT preserves "garden of Eden" without importing "paradise."

### G3. The man was "with her"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis 3:6: the woman "gave also to her man *with her* (*immah*)." The phrase explicitly places the man at the scene. The TT preserves this. The note states the text says "with her" without specifying further about degree of presence, awareness, or participation — interpretation remains open.

### G4. Who made the first clothes?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

3:7: humans make *chagorot* (loin-coverings/girdles) from fig leaves. 3:21: YHWH Elohim makes *kotnot or* (garments of skin). The upgrade from partial self-covering to full divine clothing is textually present. The text does not say from which animal the skin comes, or whether any animal died. Rule 12 — no false precision.

### G5. "Like one of us" — echoing 1:26
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis 3:22: "like one of us" (*ke'achad mimmennu*) echoes 1:26: "let us make a human." Both use divine plural. The TT notes list the same three options at both locations (royal plural, divine council, literary plural) without privileging any.

### G6. Psychology — shame, fear, and blame as consciousness sequence
**[TEXTUAL / STRONG INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**

Genesis 3:7–13 presents a psychological sequence the text names but does not explain:
1. **Awareness:** "they knew that they were naked" (v.7) — self-consciousness
2. **Covering:** they sew fig leaves (v.7) — shame response
3. **Hiding:** "hid themselves from the face of YHWH Elohim" (v.8) — avoidance
4. **Fear:** "I was afraid, for I am naked" (v.10) — explicit fear
5. **Blame:** the human blames the woman and (implicitly) God; the woman blames the serpent (vv. 12–13)

The text presents this as a sequence of **consequences of knowledge** — not a theological system, but a narrative observation of how awareness of exposure produces shame, which produces hiding, which produces fear, which produces deflection. The TT preserves the sequence without interpreting it.
