## A. Greek Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. "In beginning was the word" — *archē* without "the"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 1:1a: Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος (*en archē ēn ho logos*). The noun ἀρχή (*archē*, "beginning") appears without the word "the" — literally "in beginning," not "in *the* beginning." This mirrors the Hebrew of Gen 1:1, where בְּרֵאשִׁית (*bereshit*) likewise lacks "the" ("in beginning," not "in *the* beginning"). Whether the missing article in John signals a qualitative sense ("in a state of beginning"), an unspecified time reference, or a deliberate echo of Genesis 1:1 is debated. Most English translations insert "the" for naturalness, obscuring the Greek construction. The TT preserves the form without "the." The parallel between the two openings is verbal, not merely thematic: both texts begin with the same prepositional structure pointing to an unspecified temporal origin.

### A2. The Logos Prologue structure — chiastic or linear?
**[TEXTUAL — PROBABLE]**

The Prologue (1:1-18) has been analyzed as following a mirror structure (chiasm — where the second half reverses the order of the first) by several scholars. One widely discussed arrangement (proposed by the scholars Culpepper and Staley) identifies the center at v.12b-13 (the children of God born not of blood) with concentric rings expanding outward: vv.1-2 parallel vv.17-18 (the Word with God / the only-born at God's side); vv.3-5 parallel vv.14-16 (creation and light / incarnation and glory); vv.6-8 parallel vv.15 (Yochanan's witness). Others read the Prologue as a straightforward linear descent — from preexistence (vv.1-2), through creation (vv.3-5), to incarnation (vv.14-16), to testimony (vv.17-18). The TT does not impose either structural reading on the text but notes both as exegetical possibilities.

### A3. "The word was God" — grammar and meaning
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 1:1c: καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος (*kai theos ēn ho logos*). The word θεός (*theos*, "God") appears before the verb and without the word "the." In Greek, a describing noun placed before the verb often drops "the" even when it refers to something definite. A widely cited grammar principle called Colwell's rule (a 1933 observation about how Greek uses the article when a describing noun comes before the verb "was") states that in such cases the noun typically drops "the" without becoming indefinite. Under this principle, the clause asserts "the Word was God" (definite), not "the Word was *a* god" (indefinite) or merely "the Word was divine" (describing a quality). However, Colwell's rule has been refined by later scholarship: it shows that definite describing nouns *can* drop "the," not that every noun without "the" *is* definite. A quality-focused reading ("the Word was fully of the nature of God") remains grammatically possible. The TT translates the clause as it stands in the Greek word order — "God was the word" — and notes the grammatical ambiguity.

### A4. "Became flesh" — a shift in verb tense
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 1:14a: Καὶ ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο (*kai ho logos sarx egeneto*) — "and the word became flesh." The verb ἐγένετο (*egeneto*) is in the aorist tense, which in Greek views an event as a single completed action — a one-time entry into a new state. This contrasts sharply with the imperfect tense ἦν (*ēn*, "was") used throughout vv.1-4 to describe the Word's ongoing, timeless existence ("was" in the beginning, "was" with God, "was" God). The shift from imperfect to aorist marks the transition from timeless existence to a specific historical event. The Word did not "keep on becoming" flesh (that would require a continuous-action verb form); it became flesh at a point. The tense shift is theologically loaded: the same subject that *was* (continuous, unbounded) *became* (once, bounded) flesh. The TT preserves this verbal contrast.

### A5. "Full of grace and truth" — echoing Exodus 34:6
**[TEXTUAL — PROBABLE]**

John 1:14c: πλήρης χάριτος καὶ ἀληθείας (*plērēs charitos kai alētheias*) — "full of grace/favor and truth." The pairing χάρις + ἀλήθεια (*charis* + *alētheia*) is widely recognized as echoing the Hebrew pair חֶסֶד וֶאֱמֶת (*chesed ve-emet*, "loyal love and faithfulness") from Exodus 34:6, where YHWH proclaims his own character before Mosheh. The Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) of Exod 34:6 renders this pair with different Greek words (πολυέλεος καὶ ἀληθινός), meaning the Johannine echo is not a direct quotation of the Greek OT but likely reflects independent engagement with the Hebrew tradition. If the connection is intentional, the claim in John 1:14 is that the incarnate Word embodies the same character YHWH revealed at Sinai. The TT notes the parallel without asserting dependence.

### A6. "Only-born" (*monogenēs*) — etymology and translation
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 1:14, 18: μονογενής (*monogenēs*). The word is built from μόνος (*monos*, "only") and γένος (*genos*, "kind/type/offspring"), not from μόνος + γεννάω (*gennao*, "to beget/bear"). The evidence (including its use in Hebrews 11:17, where Isaac is called Abraham's *monogenēs* despite Ishmael's prior birth) confirms the meaning "one of a kind, unique" or "only-born" rather than "only-begotten." The traditional rendering "only-begotten" comes from the Latin *unigenitus* in Jerome's Vulgate translation and was reinforced by the Nicene Creed (325 CE), which used μονογενῆ in its statement about the Son's relationship to the Father. The TT renders "only-born" to reflect the Greek etymology without importing the post-Nicene theological freight of "only-begotten."

### A7. v.18: "God no one has seen ever" — emphatic word order
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 1:18a: Θεὸν οὐδεὶς ἑώρακεν πώποτε (*theon oudeis heōraken pōpote*) — "God no one has seen ever." Greek word order is flexible, and fronting an element signals emphasis. By placing θεόν ("God") first, the clause emphasizes the object of the seeing — God himself — before denying the act. The perfect tense ἑώρακεν ("has seen") adds force: not merely "no one saw" but "no one has ever seen" — the completed action with ongoing result. The word πώποτε ("ever, at any time") reinforces the absolute scope. The TT preserves the Greek word order to retain the emphasis.

### A8. John 1:18 textual variant: "only-born God" or "only-born Son"?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The Greek manuscript tradition splits at this verse. The TT base text (NA28) reads μονογενὴς θεός ("only-born God"), supported by the earliest papyri (P66, P75) and major fourth-century codices (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus). The variant ὁ μονογενὴς υἱός ("the only-born Son") is supported by Codex Alexandrinus, the Byzantine majority tradition, and most Latin and Syriac versions. The two readings differ by a single Greek word but carry significant theological weight: "only-born God" is one of the strongest pre-Nicene affirmations of the Son's full divinity. The TT translates the NA28 reading because it is the base text. The reader should know the variant exists — many traditional translations follow the "only-born Son" reading from the Byzantine/Vulgate tradition.

**Source:** Metzger, B.M., *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*, 2nd ed., Deutsche Bibelgesellschaft, 1994.

## B. Ancient Parallels

### B1. Logos in Greek philosophy — Heraclitus, Stoicism, Philo
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

The term λόγος (*logos*) carried extensive philosophical baggage before the Fourth Gospel was written. Heraclitus (c. 535-475 BCE) used *logos* for the rational principle governing the cosmos — the hidden order behind change. The Stoics (3rd century BCE onward) developed *logos* into a central concept: the divine reason (*logos spermatikos*) permeating and organizing all matter. Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BCE - 50 CE) — a Hellenistic Jewish philosopher contemporary with early Christianity — identified the *logos* with the intermediary between the transcendent God and the created world, combining Greek philosophical categories with Jewish Wisdom and Torah traditions. Philo's *logos* is God's agent in creation, the "firstborn son" of God, the "image" of God. Whether John knew Philo, drew on the same tradition pool, or arrived at *logos* independently through Jewish categories alone is unresolved. The verbal overlap is undeniable; the conceptual dependence is debated. The TT notes the philosophical field without collapsing John's *logos* into any one tradition.

**Source:** Borgen, P., "The Logos Was the True Light: Contributions to the Interpretation of the Prologue of John," *Novum Testamentum* 14, 1972; Runia, D.T., *Philo of Alexandria and the Timaeus of Plato*, Brill, 1986.

### B2. Jewish Wisdom tradition — Proverbs 8, Sirach 24, Wisdom of Solomon
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The closest Jewish parallel to John's Prologue is the personified Wisdom (*Chokmah*/*Sophia*) tradition. In Proverbs 8:22-31, Wisdom declares that she was present at creation, beside God "as a master craftsman." Sirach 24:1-12 describes Wisdom coming forth from the mouth of the Most High, dwelling in Israel, and taking root in Jerusalem. Wisdom of Solomon 7:22-8:1 calls Wisdom "a breath of the power of God," "a reflection of eternal light," and "an image of his goodness." The structural parallels with John 1 are extensive: preexistence with God, agency in creation, descent to dwell among humans, rejection by some. If John's Prologue draws on the Wisdom tradition, it substitutes *logos* for *sophia* — raising the question of why the masculine *logos* replaces the feminine *sophia*. Possible reasons include: the masculine gender fitting the incarnation claim; *logos* carrying philosophical resonance that *sophia* lacked in certain contexts; or compositional history (a pre-Johannine Wisdom hymn adapted by the evangelist).

**Source:** Witherington, B., *John's Wisdom: A Commentary on the Fourth Gospel*, Westminster John Knox, 1995; Scott, M., *Sophia and the Johannine Jesus*, JSOT Press, 1992.

### B3. The Prologue and Genesis 1 — structural and verbal parallels
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

John 1 deliberately mirrors the opening of Genesis. The verbal parallels: "in beginning" (John 1:1 / Gen 1:1); creation through speech ("all things came into being through him" / "and God said"); light and darkness ("the light shines in the darkness" / "let there be light... God separated the light from the darkness"); life ("in him was life" / the living creatures, the breath of life). The structural parallel: Genesis 1 moves from primordial state through creative speech to the emergence of life and humanity; John 1 moves from the preexistent Word through creative agency to incarnation as flesh among humans. The Fourth Gospel opens by retelling the creation story with the *logos* as the speaking agent, then extending it beyond Genesis: not merely "and God said" but "the Word became flesh." The TT notes the verbal echoes without asserting that John is merely rewriting Genesis — the Prologue also draws on traditions (Wisdom, philosophical *logos*) absent from Genesis 1.

**Source:** Hooker, M.D., "In the Beginning Was the Word," in *The Beginnings of Christianity*, ed. J. Barclay and J. Sweet, T&T Clark, 2004.

## C. Historical and Archaeological Context

### C1. Beyt-Anyah beyond the Yarden — location identification debate
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — UNCERTAIN]**

John 1:28: "These things took place in Beyt-Anyah beyond the Yarden, where Yochanan was immersing." This location is distinct from the Beyt-Anyah near Yerushalayim (the village of Lazarus in John 11-12). Origen (3rd century CE) reported that he could not find a "Bethany beyond the Jordan" and suggested the reading should be "Bethabara" (Βηθαβαρά, "house of the crossing"), which he found geographically plausible. Some manuscripts reflect this emendation. Modern candidates for the site include Tell al-Kharrar (also called Bethany Beyond the Jordan, in modern Jordan), which has been excavated and shows evidence of Byzantine-period pilgrimage activity, though pre-Byzantine occupation is less clearly established. The identification remains uncertain — the site's importance is theological (the beginning of Jesus' public ministry) rather than archaeological.

**Source:** Riesner, R., "Bethany Beyond the Jordan," in *Anchor Bible Dictionary*, vol. 1, Doubleday, 1992; Waheeb, M., "Wadi al-Kharrar (Bethany Beyond the Jordan)," *Annual of the Department of Antiquities of Jordan* 41, 1997.

### C2. The Perushim and Yehudim in John — historical context
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The Fourth Gospel introduces "the Yehudim" (*hoi Ioudaioi*, 1:19) and "the Perushim" (*hoi Pharisaioi*, 1:24) as distinct groups interrogating Yochanan. The Perushim (Pharisees) were a Jewish movement emphasizing oral Torah, ritual purity, and synagogue-based piety. By the time John's Gospel was composed (likely 90-100 CE), the Perushim had become the dominant surviving Jewish movement after the Temple's destruction in 70 CE. The term *hoi Ioudaioi* in John is notoriously complex: it can mean "the Judeans" (geographic), "the Jews" (ethnic-religious), or "the Jewish authorities" (institutional), depending on context. In 1:19, the delegation comes from Yerushalayim, suggesting an institutional or geographic sense — "the Judeans" as the Jerusalem-based religious establishment. The TT renders the term according to context and notes the semantic range.

**Source:** Reinhartz, A., *Cast Out of the Covenant: Jews and Anti-Judaism in the Gospel of John*, Lexington Books, 2018; Mason, S., "Pharisees," in *Anchor Bible Dictionary*, vol. 5, Doubleday, 1992.

### C3. First-century immersion practices — mikveh archaeology
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Yochanan's activity of immersing (*baptizo*) in the Yarden occurs within a well-attested Jewish context of ritual purification. Archaeological excavations have uncovered hundreds of ritual immersion pools (*miqva'ot*, singular *mikveh*) throughout Judea, Galilee, and the Diaspora, dating from the Hasmonean period (2nd century BCE) through the Second Temple period. These stepped pools — found at Qumran, Sepphoris, Yerushalayim (near the Temple Mount), Herodium, and private residences — demonstrate that full-body water immersion was a widespread Jewish practice. Yochanan's immersion differs from standard mikveh practice in location (a flowing river rather than a constructed pool), frequency (apparently once rather than repeated), and association (with a specific prophet-figure rather than with Temple or purity law). The Dead Sea Scrolls community at Qumran also practiced regular immersion connected to communal identity, providing a partial parallel.

**Source:** Reich, R., *Miqwa'ot (Jewish Ritual Baths) in the Second Temple, Mishnaic and Talmudic Periods*, Israel Exploration Society, 2013; Taylor, J.E., *The Immerser: John the Baptist within Second Temple Judaism*, Eerdmans, 1997.

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. *Monogenēs* — etymology and usage
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The compound μονογενής (*monogenēs*) appears in the New Testament five times (John 1:14, 1:18, 3:16, 3:18; 1 John 4:9) and once in Hebrews 11:17 (of Isaac). Outside the NT, it appears in the LXX (Judges 11:34, of Jephthah's daughter; Psalm 21:21 LXX; Tobit 3:15, 6:11, 8:17) and in classical Greek (e.g., Hesiod, Parmenides). In every non-theological usage, the meaning is "only one of its kind" or "sole offspring" — not "only-begotten" in the sense of a unique mode of generation. The shift to "only-begotten" entered through Jerome's *unigenitus* in the Vulgate and was cemented by the Nicene Creed's use of μονογενῆ to affirm the Son's unique generation from the Father (γεννηθέντα οὐ ποιηθέντα, "begotten not made"). The lexicographical evidence (BDAG, LSJ) confirms the etymological base is *genos* (kind/class), not *gennao* (beget). The TT follows the lexical evidence.

### D2. The double *amēn* (*amēn amēn*) — uniquely Johannine formula
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 1:51: ἀμὴν ἀμὴν λέγω ὑμῖν (*amēn amēn legō hymin*) — "amen, amen, I say to you." This doubled *amēn* formula is unique to the Fourth Gospel, appearing 25 times (John 1:51; 3:3, 5, 11; 5:19, 24, 25; 6:26, 32, 47, 53; 8:34, 51, 58; 10:1, 7; 12:24; 13:16, 20, 21, 38; 14:12; 16:20, 23; 21:18). The Synoptic Gospels use a single *amēn* ("amen, I say to you"), which is itself distinctive — in Jewish liturgical practice, *amēn* was a response formula ("so be it"), not a self-introductory formula. Jesus' use of *amēn* to introduce his own statements (rather than to affirm someone else's) is without known parallel in pre-Christian Jewish literature. The Johannine doubling intensifies the formula further. The TT transliterates *amēn* (Rule 4 applies — the word has crossed into the target languages) and preserves the doubling.

## E. Scientific Correspondence and Non-Correspondence

### E1. "In beginning" and cosmological origins — *logos* as ordering principle
**[SCIENTIFIC COMPARISON — POSSIBLE]**

John 1:1-3 describes all things coming into existence through the *logos* — an ordering rational principle present before creation. Modern cosmology describes the universe's initial conditions as governed by fundamental physical constants and mathematical structures — an inherent "rationality" in the fabric of reality that precedes (logically, not temporally) the emergence of matter and energy. Some physicists and philosophers of science (e.g., Paul Davies, John Barrow) have noted the structural resonance: the universe appears to be "written in mathematics" (Galileo's phrase), and its intelligibility — the fact that it can be described by rational laws at all — is itself unexplained by physics. This is not a claim that John anticipated modern cosmology. The *logos* of John 1 is personal ("was with God," "became flesh"); the mathematical structure of the universe is impersonal. The resonance is suggestive, not probative. The TT notes the parallel without asserting correspondence.

**Source:** Davies, P., *The Mind of God: The Scientific Basis for a Rational World*, Simon & Schuster, 1992; Barrow, J.D., *The Constants of Nature*, Jonathan Cape, 2002.

## F. Later Reception

### F1. The Logos in Trinitarian theology — Nicaea and Chalcedon
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

John 1:1-18 became the primary biblical anchor for the Logos theology that culminated in the Council of Nicaea (325 CE) and the Council of Chalcedon (451 CE). The Nicene Creed's affirmation that the Son is "of one substance (*homoousios*) with the Father" draws heavily on John 1:1c ("the Word was God") and 1:18 ("the only-born God/Son"). Arius (d. 336 CE) argued from the same Prologue that "there was when the Word was not" — reading the *logos* as the first and greatest creature rather than as co-eternal with God. The Council of Nicaea rejected Arius's reading, but the debate demonstrated that John 1:1 does not resolve the metaphysical question by itself — it required conciliar interpretation to produce the Nicene formula. The TT notes: the Prologue predates and does not presuppose the Nicene/Chalcedonian framework. Reading Nicaea back into John is a documented theological practice, not a textual requirement.

**Source:** Ayres, L., *Nicaea and Its Legacy: An Approach to Fourth-Century Trinitarian Theology*, Oxford University Press, 2004; Young, F.M., *From Nicaea to Chalcedon*, 2nd ed., Baker Academic, 2010.

### F2. Jewish readings of John 1 — rejection of divine *logos* identification
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

Jewish engagement with John's Prologue has historically been one of rejection. The identification of the *logos* with a person ("became flesh") who is also identified as God ("the Word was God") crosses what rabbinic theology regards as the boundary of monotheism. Maimonides (12th century) explicitly argued against any doctrine that introduces multiplicity into God's unity (Guide for the Perplexed, 1.50-60). The medieval Jewish-Christian disputations (Paris 1240, Barcelona 1263, Tortosa 1413-14) frequently addressed Logos Christology, with Jewish disputants arguing that the Prologue misrepresents Jewish conceptions of divine speech and wisdom. Modern Jewish scholars (Daniel Boyarin, *Border Lines*, 2004) have complicated this picture, arguing that "binitarianism" — the notion of two divine powers — existed within Judaism before and alongside early Christianity, making John's Prologue less of a departure from Jewish thought than later rabbinic orthodoxy acknowledged.

**Source:** Boyarin, D., *Border Lines: The Partition of Judaeo-Christianity*, University of Pennsylvania Press, 2004; Novak, D., *Jewish-Christian Dialogue: A Jewish Justification*, Oxford University Press, 1989.

### F3. Islamic reception — Isa as word of God (*kalimatu'llah*)
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The Quran describes Isa (Jesus) as a "word from Him" (كَلِمَةً مِّنْهُ, *kalimatan minhu*, Quran 3:45) and "His word which He directed toward Maryam" (كَلِمَتُهُ أَلْقَاهَا إِلَىٰ مَرْيَمَ, *kalimatuhu alqaha ila Maryam*, Quran 4:171). The designation of Jesus as God's "word" (*kalima*) resonates verbally with John 1:1 but differs fundamentally in scope: in Islamic theology, Isa is a word *from* God (a created expression of divine will — the command "be!" that brought about his miraculous birth), not the eternal Word who *was* God. The Quran explicitly denies that Isa is divine (4:171: "do not say 'three'... God is only one God"). Classical Islamic commentators (al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir) interpret *kalima* as referring to the creative command (*kun*, "be") by which God initiated Isa's conception in Maryam's womb, not as a claim of preexistence or divinity. The verbal overlap with John's *logos* — without the claim that the title identifies a divine being — illustrates how the same title can carry entirely different theological content.

**Source:** Parrinder, G., *Jesus in the Qur'an*, Oneworld, 1995; Khalidi, T., *The Muslim Jesus: Sayings and Stories in Islamic Literature*, Harvard University Press, 2001.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. Why does John's Gospel not begin with a birth narrative?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Matthew and Luke begin with detailed birth narratives (genealogy, annunciation, manger, magi, flight to Egypt). John begins with a cosmic prologue that starts before creation. Mark begins with the adult ministry of Yochanan the Immerser. The Fourth Gospel's choice to bypass birth entirely and begin with preexistence is a deliberate compositional decision. The Prologue renders a birth narrative unnecessary by its own logic: if the Word was "in beginning" with God and "became flesh" (1:14), the manner of physical birth is secondary to the claim about what the Word fundamentally is. Some scholars argue the Prologue functions as John's alternative to the Synoptic infancy narratives — not contradicting them but superseding them by pushing the origin of Jesus behind the beginning of time itself. Others note that John's audience may have already known the Synoptic traditions and that the Prologue assumes, rather than replaces, them.

### G2. "You shall see the sky opened" (v.51) — the Jacob's ladder echo
**[TEXTUAL — PROBABLE]**

John 1:51: "you will see the sky opened and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of Man." The language directly echoes Gen 28:12, where Ya'aqov (Jacob) dreams of a ladder (*sullam*) set up on the earth with its top reaching to the sky, and the angels of God ascending and descending on it. The verbal parallel is precise: ἀναβαίνοντας καὶ καταβαίνοντας (*anabainontas kai katabainontas*, "ascending and descending") matches the LXX of Gen 28:12. The substitution is striking: where Genesis has the ladder, John has "the Son of Man." The implication — that the Son of Man is the point of connection between heaven and earth, replacing the ladder of Jacob's dream — is theologically loaded but unstated by the text. The TT notes the verbal echo and the substitution without supplying the theological conclusion.

### G3. Nathanael's skepticism — "Can anything good come from Natseret?"
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE]**

John 1:46: Nathanael's question "Can anything good come from Natseret?" implies that Natseret (Nazareth) had a negligible reputation. Archaeological evidence confirms that first-century Nazareth was a small agricultural village — estimates range from 200 to 400 inhabitants — with no mention in the Hebrew Bible, Josephus, the Talmud, or any pre-Christian source. The village's obscurity appears to be the point of Nathanael's skepticism: the messianic claimant comes not from Yerushalayim, Bethlehem, or any prophetically significant location, but from an unremarkable hamlet in lower Galilee. Whether Nathanael's remark also carries a regional prejudice (Galilean vs. Judean) is possible but unconfirmable from this verse alone. Excavations at Nazareth (the Church of the Annunciation site and the Nazareth Village Farm) have uncovered agricultural installations, rock-cut tombs, and domestic structures consistent with a modest village, not a town of any prominence.

**Source:** Strange, J.F., "Nazareth," in *Anchor Bible Dictionary*, vol. 4, Doubleday, 1992; Pfann, S., "Nazareth," in *The Oxford Encyclopedia of Archaeology in the Near East*, Oxford University Press, 1997.
