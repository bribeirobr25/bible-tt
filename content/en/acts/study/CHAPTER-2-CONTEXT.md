## HOW TO USE THIS COMPANION

This file contains background material organized by topic. Each entry carries **two labels:**

**What kind of claim?** (tells you the source of the information)
- **TEXTUAL** — derived directly from the Greek text
- **STRONG INFERENCE** — logically entailed by the text, not stated explicitly
- **POSSIBLE INFERENCE** — one legitimate reading among others
- **COMPARATIVE PARALLEL** — attested in another ancient source
- **LATER RECEPTION** — post-biblical reading
- **HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL** — based on material or historical evidence
- **SPECULATION** — no strong textual or evidential basis

**How certain?** (tells you the confidence level)
- **VERIFIED** — strong evidence, wide agreement
- **PROBABLE** — well-supported but debated
- **POSSIBLE** — one reasonable reading among others
- **UNCERTAIN** — genuinely unclear
- **SPECULATIVE** — must be rare and clearly flagged
- **DOCUMENTED** — historically attested reception (for LATER RECEPTION entries)

**Reading principle:** Start with TEXTUAL entries — those come from the Greek itself. Then explore outward to parallels, reception, and comparison. The companion presents; you assess.

## A. Source Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. Pentecost / Shavuot — the harvest festival as the chapter's frame
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The chapter opens **ἐν τῷ συμπληροῦσθαι τὴν ἡμέραν τῆς πεντηκοστῆς** (*en tō symplērousthai tēn hēmeran tēs pentēkostēs*, 2:1), "as the day of Pentecost was being fulfilled." **Πεντηκοστή** ("fiftieth") is the Greek name of **Shavuot**, the Feast of Weeks (*chag ha-shavuot*), the pilgrim festival fifty days after Passover (Lev 23:15-21; Deut 16:9-12) — a one-day harvest feast (the offering of first wheat) and one of the three festivals requiring presence in Jerusalem. The TT renders "Pentecost (Shavuot)" and keeps the verb *symplēroō* ("be fulfilled/completed"), framing the day as the appointed time rather than a mere date.

The HB never connects Shavuot with the Sinai law-giving; that association is **later** rabbinic (see §F1). For the festival's pilgrimage and crowds as historical setting, see §I.

### A2. *Pnoē*, *pneuma*, and "tongues like fire" — the theophany word-field
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The Spirit's coming is described through a tight word-field. The sound is "like a rushing violent **wind**" — **πνοή** (*pnoē*, "wind/breath," 2:2), cognate with **πνεῦμα** (*pneuma*, "wind/spirit/breath," 2:4) — and the visible sign is "**tongues** like fire" — **γλῶσσαι ὡσεὶ πυρός** (*glōssai hōsei pyros*, 2:3), where *glōssa* names the tongue-*shape* of the flames. The TT keeps *pneuma* as the locked slash {a:wind/spirit} and preserves the deliberate play whereby the same "tongue" word reappears for the "other {a:tongues/languages}" of v.4.

The wind-and-fire pairing draws the reader toward the Sinai theophany of Exodus 19 (see §B1). **[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE]** The clustering of *pnoē*/*pneuma*/*glōssa* most plausibly signals an intended echo of God's self-manifestation, though the text states the signs, not the interpretation.

### A3. *Synechythē* and the reversal of Babel
**[TEXTUAL / COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

When the crowd hears its own languages, "the multitude **was confounded** (*synechythē*)" (2:6), from *syncheō* ("to pour together, confuse"). This is the same verb the LXX uses at Babel, where YHWH says "let us **confuse** (*syncheōmen*) their language" and the place is named for the "confusing" (Gen 11:7, 9 LXX). At Babel one speech became many in judgment and scattering; at Pentecost the many languages each carry one message in gathering.

The TT keeps "confounded" so the Babel link stays audible at the lexical level. → For the Babel parallel as a literary tradition, see §B2; for the table-of-nations geography of vv.9-11, see §A4 and §I.

### A4. The "table of nations" catalog (vv.9-11) and the diaspora of Israel
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the list); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for its structuring principle)]**

The fifteen names of vv.9-11 run roughly east-to-west — Parthians, Medes, Elamites, Mesopotamia, then Asia Minor, Egypt, Cyrenaican Libya, Rome, and finally Crete and Arabia. The hearers are defined as "Jews (Yehudim), devout men from every nation under the sky" (2:5) plus "proselytes" (2:11) — that is, the Jewish diaspora and its converts, gathered for the festival, not yet the gentiles as such.

The catalog has long been read as a deliberate "table of nations" in the manner of Genesis 10 — a representative map of the inhabited world. **[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE]** Whether the list reproduces a known astrological/geographical *Völkertafel* or simply enumerates the diaspora is debated; the text gives the names without stating the principle. → For the diaspora's geography and the festival economy, see §I.

### A5. The kerygma pattern of Peter's sermon
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the structure); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE (for it being a fixed pattern)]**

Peter's speech (2:14-40) lays out a recurring early-proclamation sequence: an appeal to **Scripture** (Joel, vv.16-21); **the cross** as both God's "determined plan and foreknowledge" and the deed of "lawless hands" (v.23); **the resurrection** attested by witnesses and by Psalm 16 (vv.24-32); **the exaltation** to God's right hand and the outpouring of the Spirit, with Psalm 110 (vv.33-36); and the **call** to "change your minds" and be immersed (vv.38-40). The same skeleton recurs in Acts 3, 10, and 13.

The TT keeps the sermon's seams (the "this is that" formula of v.16; the "men, brothers" address of vv.29, 37) visible. **[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE]** Many scholars take this as an early, pre-Lukan *kerygma* (proclamation) outline; that it is a *fixed* form is an inference, not a statement of the text. → For the divine-plan/human-guilt pairing of v.23, see §D2.

### A6. The three pillar texts — Joel 2, Psalm 16, Psalm 110
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The sermon is built on three HB citations: **Joel 2:28-32** (LXX 3:1-5), the outpoured Spirit and "everyone who calls on the name of *the* Lord" (vv.17-21); **Psalm 16:8-11**, "you will not abandon my soul to Hades" (vv.25-28, 31); and **Psalm 110:1**, "the Lord said to my lord" (vv.34-35). Acts cites the **LXX** form throughout (e.g. *glōssa*, "tongue," for Hebrew *kavod*, "glory," at v.26; *en tais eschatais hēmerais*, "in the last days," where Joel/LXX has "after these things," v.17).

Per Rule 30 the first-person divine portions are marked **@@…@@** (Joel's "I will pour out," vv.17-21; the LORD's command in Ps 110, vv.34-35), while David's own voice in Psalm 16 is human speech and is not. → The citation mechanics, the LXX-vs-Hebrew differences, and the 2:34 double-*kyrios* argument are handled in the **PROPHECY companion**; this companion gives the historical and literary frame only.

### A7. *Metanoia*, *aphesis*, immersion in the name, and the *eis* of 2:38
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexemes); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the force of *eis*)]**

Peter's call (2:38) gathers four locked or loaded terms: **μετανοήσατε** (*metanoēsate*, "change your minds" — the locked GS rendering of *metanoeō*, not penitential "repent"); immersion **ἐπὶ τῷ ὀνόματι Ἰησοῦ** (*epi tō onomati Iēsou*, "upon the name of Yeshua"); **εἰς ἄφεσιν τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν** (*eis aphesin tōn hamartiōn*, "for/unto the release of sins" — *aphesis* as at Luke 1:77; 3:3); and the promised "gift of the holy {a:wind/spirit}." The preposition **εἰς** is genuinely ambiguous — telic ("in order to obtain") or causal/referential ("with reference to") forgiveness — and the TT preserves "for/unto" without resolving it (decision A-005 in the editorial log notes the crux at 2:38).

"Immersion upon the name of Yeshua" picks up "the name of the Lord" of the Joel citation (v.21), where *kyrios* renders YHWH; the same construction is now applied to Jesus. **[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN]** Whether the phrase implies a baptismal formula, a transfer of allegiance, or both cannot be settled from the text. → For the community response that follows (vv.41-47), see §D3.

## B. Ancient Near Eastern and Ancient World Parallels

### B1. The Sinai theophany of wind and fire (Exodus 19)
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The Pentecost signs — a sound from heaven, a violent wind, and fire — recall the Sinai theophany, where YHWH descends on the mountain "in fire," with smoke, a great sound, and trembling (Ex 19:16-18; cf. Deut 4:11-12, "fire… and the voice of words"). Later Jewish tradition vividly elaborated the Sinai voice as dividing into the languages of the nations (see §F1), but the basic wind-and-fire vocabulary of divine self-manifestation is already biblical. The Acts narrative deploys that inherited theophany grammar at the festival the rabbis would associate with Sinai.

The parallel shows a shared symbolic language of divine presence; it does not by itself prove that Acts intends a programmatic "new Sinai." The TT keeps the wind-and-fire wording and lets the resonance stand.

**Source:** Sommer, B.D., *Revelation and Authority: Sinai in Jewish Scripture and Tradition*, Yale University Press, 2015, pp. 27-72; Keener, C.S., *Acts: An Exegetical Commentary*, vol. 1, Baker Academic, 2012, pp. 800-808.

### B2. Babel and the dispersal of languages (Genesis 11)
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

The "confounding" of speech at Pentecost (2:6, *synechythē*) inverts the Babel story (Gen 11:1-9), where humanity's single language is "confused" (*syncheō* LXX) and the people scattered across the earth. Second Temple and later Jewish texts treated Babel as the origin of the seventy nations and their seventy tongues (cf. the table of nations, Gen 10). Read against this, the Pentecost languages signal a reversal or healing of the Babel scattering — the nations' tongues now made vehicles of a single proclamation.

This is a literary and theological parallel attested in the shared Jewish reading of Genesis; it establishes a common storytelling grammar, not Lukan dependence on any single source.

**Source:** Kugel, J.L., *Traditions of the Bible*, Harvard University Press, 1998, pp. 228-242; Barrett, C.K., *A Critical and Exegetical Commentary on the Acts of the Apostles*, vol. 1, ICC, T&T Clark, 1994, pp. 119-122.

### B3. Hellenistic geographical "nation lists"
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — POSSIBLE]**

Lists enumerating the peoples of the inhabited world were a known Greco-Roman literary form, used by geographers and historians (e.g. the catalogs in Strabo's *Geographica*) and, in one debated proposal, organized along astrological-zodiacal lines (Paulus Alexandrinus assigns nations to zodiac signs). Some scholars have suggested the Acts catalog (vv.9-11) reflects such a *Völkertafel* convention; others see only a list of the diaspora regions actually represented in festival Jerusalem. The form is attested; the specific claim that Acts borrows a fixed astrological list is contested.

The TT keeps the names in familiar geographic form; the companion notes the parallel without endorsing the strongest version of it.

**Source:** Metzger, B.M., "Ancient Astrological Geography and Acts 2:9-11," in *Apostolic History and the Gospel* (Gasque and Martin, eds.), Eerdmans, 1970, pp. 123-133; Gilbert, G., "The List of Nations in Acts 2," *Journal of Biblical Literature* 121 (2002), pp. 497-529.

## C. Historical Context

### C1. Shavuot pilgrimage and the festival crowds in Jerusalem
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Shavuot was one of the three pilgrim festivals (*regalim*) for which adult male Israelites were expected to "appear before YHWH" in Jerusalem (Deut 16:16), and the diaspora context of vv.5-11 reflects the real phenomenon of overseas Jews traveling to the festivals. Estimates of festival crowds in first-century Jerusalem vary widely and are uncertain, but the literary and archaeological picture (Josephus's large though rhetorical figures; the scale of the Herodian Temple platform and its approaches; the network of pilgrim *mikva'ot* near the southern steps) confirms that the pilgrim festivals drew very substantial crowds. A gathering of diaspora Jews "from every nation" at Shavuot is historically plausible.

**Source:** Sanders, E.P., *Judaism: Practice and Belief, 63 BCE–66 CE*, SCM Press, 1992, pp. 125-145; Bauckham, R. (ed.), *The Book of Acts in Its Palestinian Setting*, Eerdmans, 1995.

### C2. The Jewish diaspora of vv.9-11
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

By the first century, sizable Jewish communities existed across the regions the catalog names: Parthia, Media, and Elam (the eastern, Babylonian diaspora, the largest); Asia Minor (Cappadocia, Pontus, the province of Asia, Phrygia, Pamphylia); Egypt (Alexandria above all) and Cyrenaican Libya; Rome itself; and the islands and Arabia. Philo and Josephus attest the breadth of the diaspora, and Roman sources note the Jewish community at Rome. The list is a fair, if compressed, snapshot of where diaspora Jews actually lived.

**Source:** Schürer, E., *The History of the Jewish People in the Age of Jesus Christ*, rev. Vermes, Millar, Goodman, T&T Clark, 1986, vol. 3.1, pp. 1-86; Barclay, J.M.G., *Jews in the Mediterranean Diaspora*, T&T Clark, 1996.

### C3. The "third hour" and the morning prayer
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Peter rebuts the charge of drunkenness by noting that "it is *the* third hour of the day" (2:15) — roughly 9 a.m., reckoned from dawn. The third hour was associated with one of the daily times of prayer in Second Temple practice (cf. the hours of prayer at the Temple, Acts 3:1, "the ninth hour"). The point is partly cultural: morning, before the main meal, was too early for the public drunkenness the mockers allege.

**Source:** Sanders, E.P., *Judaism: Practice and Belief*, 1992, pp. 196-202; Keener, C.S., *Acts*, vol. 1, 2012, pp. 877-879.

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. *Glōssa* across the chapter — shape, tongue, language
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

**γλῶσσα** (*glōssa*) does triple duty in Acts 2 and the TT tracks each sense. At v.3 it names a *shape* — "tongues like fire," the tongue-form of the flames. At vv.4 and 11 it is the {a:tongues/languages} of speech, where both the "ecstatic utterance" sense and the "real human language" sense stay live (Rule 2), the latter confirmed by the synonym **διάλεκτος** (*dialektos*, "language/dialect," vv.6, 8). At v.26, inside the Psalm 16 citation, *glōssa* ("my tongue rejoiced") renders the Hebrew *kavod* ("my glory") that the LXX read as "tongue."

The TT keeps the slash on the speech-*glōssai* and the bare "tongue" elsewhere, so the single Greek word's range stays visible rather than being flattened to one English equivalent.

**Source:** Liddell, H.G. and Scott, R., *Greek-English Lexicon*, 9th ed., rev. Jones, 1940, s.v. γλῶσσα; Barrett, C.K., *Acts*, vol. 1, ICC, 1994, pp. 115-122.

### D2. *Tē hōrismenē boulē kai prognōsei* — divine plan and human hands held together
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Verse 23 sets two clauses in tension within a single sentence: Jesus was "given up **by the determined plan and foreknowledge of God**" (*tē hōrismenē boulē kai prognōsei tou theou*) **and** "you… killed *him* through *the* hand of lawless *men*" (*dia cheiros anomōn*). *Hōrismenē* ("determined, marked out," from *horizō*) and *prognōsis* ("foreknowledge") assert God's purpose; *anomōn* ("lawless") and the second-person "you" assert genuine human agency and guilt. The Greek juxtaposes the two without subordinating either.

The TT renders both clauses at full strength and does not resolve the tension toward determinism or toward mere permission. **[the simultaneous affirmation — PROBABLE]** that the text means to hold divine purpose and human responsibility together.

**Source:** Bruce, F.F., *The Acts of the Apostles: Greek Text with Introduction and Commentary*, 3rd ed., Eerdmans, 1990, pp. 124-126; Barrett, C.K., *Acts*, vol. 1, ICC, 1994, pp. 141-143.

### D3. *Koinōnia* and "had all things in common" — the community summary
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexeme); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the social model)]**

The community is marked by four practices (2:42): "the teaching of the apostles, and **the fellowship** (*hē koinōnia*), the breaking of the bread, and the prayers." **κοινωνία** (*koinōnia*) spans "fellowship, participation, sharing, communion"; its concrete economic face appears at vv.44-45, "had all *things* in common (*koina*)… and were distributing… as anyone had need" — using imperfect verbs (ongoing, repeated action), describing a practice rather than legislating a rule. The TT renders "fellowship" while flagging the sharing-of-goods sense.

The wording echoes the covenant ideal of "no needy among you" (Deut 15:4 LXX, *ouk estai en soi endeēs*). **[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE]** Whether vv.44-45 depict total renunciation of property or recurring need-based sharing is debated; the imperfects favor the latter, and Acts 5:4 implies retained ownership. → For the covenant-ideal background, see §F2.

## F. Later Reception in Other Traditions

### F1. The rabbinic association of Shavuot with the giving of the Torah
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The HB presents Shavuot as a harvest festival with no explicit link to Sinai. In later rabbinic tradition, however, Shavuot became *zeman mattan torateinu* ("the season of the giving of our Torah"), dated to the law-giving at Sinai (b. Pesachim 68b; b. Shabbat 86b reckons the Sinai theophany to the festival). A related tradition pictures the Sinai voice dividing into seventy languages so that all nations could hear (Exodus Rabbah 5:9; cf. b. Shabbat 88b). Read alongside Acts 2, these traditions make the Pentecost languages and fire suggestive — but they are **post-biblical** receptions, and their dating relative to Acts is itself uncertain.

The TT and this companion do not assume Acts presupposes the developed Sinai-Shavuot link; the association is recorded as reception, not as the text's stated frame.

**Source:** Sommer, B.D., *Revelation and Authority*, Yale University Press, 2015, pp. 27-72; Strack, H.L. and Stemberger, G., *Introduction to the Talmud and Midrash*, 2nd ed., Fortress Press, 1996 (for the dating of the relevant traditions).

### F2. The "community of goods" in later readings — Qumran, monasticism, and beyond
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The Acts portrait of shared property (2:44-45; 4:32-35) was read, in later traditions, as a charter for common life. The closest contemporary analogue is the Qumran community, whose Community Rule legislated the pooling of property for full members (1QS 6:18-23) — a comparative parallel, not a source. In Christian history the summary undergirded monastic common life and, in the modern period, has been invoked across a wide range of communitarian and social readings. These are documented receptions of the passage, not claims about Luke's intent.

**Source:** Capper, B., "The Palestinian Cultural Context of Earliest Christian Community of Goods," in Bauckham (ed.), *The Book of Acts in Its Palestinian Setting*, Eerdmans, 1995, pp. 323-356; Barrett, C.K., *Acts*, vol. 1, ICC, 1994, pp. 168-172.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. Why fifteen nations — and why does the list end with Crete and Arabia?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the data); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the explanation)]**

The catalog (vv.9-11) names fifteen peoples/regions, beginning in the far east (Parthians) and ending, oddly, with "Cretans and Arabs" appended after Rome — breaking the otherwise roughly geographic sweep. Proposed explanations range from a source-list Luke adapted, to scribal expansion, to a deliberate framing (east to west, then island and desert as outermost edges). The text gives the list as it stands; the principle behind its order and its endpoints is not stated and remains debated.

### G2. "About three thousand" — the size of the response
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the figure); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for its precision)]**

Verse 41 reports "about three thousand souls (*psychai hōsei trischiliai*)" added in a single day. The hedge "about" (*hōsei*) signals an approximation, and ancient numbers in narrative often function rhetorically as much as statistically. The figure also plays on the harvest-festival setting — a great "harvest" of people at the feast of first fruits. The TT keeps the approximating "about" and does not press the number as a census.

### G3. Immersion "upon the name of Yeshua" vs. the triadic formula
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Acts consistently has immersion "upon/in the name of Yeshua" (2:38; cf. 8:16; 10:48; 19:5), whereas Matthew 28:19 gives a triadic "into the name of the Father and of the Son and of the holy spirit." The relationship between the two formulas — whether one is earlier, whether they describe the same rite differently — has been much discussed. The TT renders each book's wording as it stands and does not harmonize them; the divergence is recorded, not resolved.

**Source:** Barrett, C.K., *Acts*, vol. 1, ICC, 1994, pp. 153-155.

### SCENARIO A — Pre-70 CE (Temple still standing)

#### IA-1. Religion — Shavuot as a functioning pilgrim festival
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Acts 2 is set during a living Temple-centered festival. Shavuot was one of the three pilgrim feasts (Deut 16:16) at which the first wheat and the two loaves were offered (Lev 23:15-21); the day drew worshippers to the Temple, and the believers themselves remain "day by day in the temple" (2:46). For a pre-70 audience the scene was lived reality: the Herodian Temple at the center, the festival sacrifices, the pilgrim crowds, and the daily hours of prayer (the "third hour," 2:15). The whole frame assumes the institution that 70 CE would destroy.

**Source:** Sanders, E.P., *Judaism: Practice and Belief, 63 BCE–66 CE*, SCM Press, 1992; Bauckham, R. (ed.), *The Book of Acts in Its Palestinian Setting*, Eerdmans, 1995.

#### IA-2. Daily Life — Pilgrimage logistics and the festival crowds
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The presence of diaspora Jews "from every nation under the sky" (2:5) reflects the real seasonal influx of pilgrims. Festival crowds swelled the city well beyond its resident population; pilgrims lodged in the city and surrounding villages, immersed in the ritual baths near the southern Temple steps, and changed currency and bought sacrificial animals in the Temple precincts. Travel from the far diaspora (Parthia, Egypt, Rome) was costly and slow, so many "dwelling in Jerusalem" (2:5) were likely diaspora Jews who had settled there, alongside short-term pilgrims. The economic and logistical scale of such a festival is well attested archaeologically (the Temple platform, the pilgrim roads, the *mikva'ot*).

**Source:** Sanders, E.P., *Judaism: Practice and Belief*, 1992; Reich, R., *Miqwa'ot (Jewish Ritual Baths) in the Second Temple, Mishnaic and Talmudic Periods*, Israel Exploration Society, 2013.

#### IA-3. Economy — The festival economy and the community of goods
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The Jerusalem festival economy ran on pilgrim spending: lodging, food, the Temple tax and offerings, money-changing, and the sale of sacrificial animals. Into this setting Acts places a community "selling *their* properties and goods and distributing them to all, as anyone had need" (2:45). For poorer pilgrims who remained after the feast, and for a city with chronic dependence on Temple-related income and periodic food insecurity, need-based sharing addressed real economic pressure. The covenant ideal of "no needy among you" (Deut 15:4) is enacted against the concrete economic realities of a festival city.

**Source:** Goodman, M., *The Ruling Class of Judaea*, Cambridge University Press, 1987; Oakman, D.E., *Jesus and the Economic Questions of His Day*, BIBAL Press, 1986.

#### IA-4. Neighboring Peoples — The diaspora geography of vv.9-11
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The nations catalog maps the actual reach of the first-century Jewish diaspora: the large eastern (Parthian/Babylonian) communities; the cities of Asia Minor; Alexandrian Egypt and Cyrenaican Libya; the community at Rome; and the islands and Arabia. These were the surrounding peoples among whom diaspora Jews lived as minorities, speaking Greek, Aramaic, Latin, and local languages — which is precisely why "each one heard… in his own language" (2:6) registers as remarkable. The list situates the Pentecost audience within the real, dispersed map of Second Temple Judaism.

**Source:** Schürer, E., *History of the Jewish People*, vol. 3.1, T&T Clark, 1986; Barclay, J.M.G., *Jews in the Mediterranean Diaspora*, T&T Clark, 1996.

### SCENARIO B — Post-70 CE (Temple destroyed)

#### IB-1. Religion — Reading the Temple festival after the Temple's fall
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

For a reader after 70 CE, the Shavuot pilgrimage and the believers' daily presence "in the temple" (2:46) depicted an institution that no longer existed. With the sacrificial system gone, Shavuot in emerging rabbinic Judaism was increasingly reframed around the giving of the Torah (see §F1) rather than the wheat-harvest offering. The chapter's opening would then read as a portrait of a vanished cultic world — and the outpouring of the Spirit "on all flesh" (2:17) as a form of divine presence not bound to the destroyed sanctuary.

**Source:** Cohen, S.J.D., *From the Maccabees to the Mishnah*, 3rd ed., Westminster John Knox, 2014; Goodman, M., *Rome and Jerusalem*, Allen Lane, 2007.

#### IB-2. Neighboring Peoples — The diaspora as the gospel's trajectory
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

After 70 CE the diaspora became, even more than before, the demographic center of Jewish life, and (for the author's audience) the field across which the movement had spread. The nations list of vv.9-11 — east to west, ending toward Rome — reads, in retrospect, as a preview of the geographic arc Acts itself traces, from Jerusalem "to the end of the earth" (1:8) and finally to Rome (Acts 28). For a post-70 reader in the diaspora, the Pentecost audience prefigured the very communities among whom the book was now read.

**Source:** Goodman, M., *Rome and Jerusalem*, Allen Lane, 2007; Barclay, J.M.G., *Jews in the Mediterranean Diaspora*, T&T Clark, 1996.
