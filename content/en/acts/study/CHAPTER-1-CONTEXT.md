## A. Source Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. The Luke–Acts seam — "the first account" and the dedication
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Acts opens **τὸν μὲν πρῶτον λόγον ἐποιησάμην** (*ton men prōton logon epoiēsamēn*, 1:1), "the first account I made," addressed again to **Θεόφιλος** (*Theophilos*, "friend/beloved of God") — the dedicatee of Luke 1:3. The "first account" is the Gospel of Luke; Acts presents itself as volume two of one work by one author. The **μέν** ("on the one hand") in 1:1 sets up a **δέ** ("on the other") that never overtly arrives — a suspended construction the TT preserves rather than smoothing.

The seam is reinforced by content: both volumes turn on the ascension (Luke 24:50-53; Acts 1:9-11), and Acts 1:1 sums the Gospel as what Yeshua (Jesus) "**began** both to do and to teach" (*ērxato… poiein te kai didaskein*) — implying that what follows is the continuation of that same action. **[STRONG INFERENCE — PROBABLE]** The wording most plausibly frames Acts as the ongoing work of the risen Yeshua through the {a:wind/spirit}, not a separate subject.

### A2. The witness-geography as the book's own outline
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

In 1:8 the risen Yeshua says the disciples will be "my witnesses both in **Yerushalayim** (Jerusalem) and in all **Yehudah** (Judea) and **Shomron** (Samaria) and unto *the* end of the earth." This is not only a promise; it is the literary table of contents of Acts itself — Jerusalem (chs. 1-7), Judea and Samaria (chs. 8-12), and the movement outward to "the end of the earth" (chs. 13-28). The term **μάρτυς** (*martys*, "witness," vv.8, 22) is here legal/testimonial; only later does the word acquire the sense "martyr." Per Rule 30 this speech of the risen Yeshua is marked divine speech (@@…@@), consistent with the Gospel treatment of his words.

### A3. *Anelēmphthē* — the ascension vocabulary and the threefold "into the sky"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The ascension is carried by a cluster of passives that leave God as the unstated agent: **ἀνελήμφθη** (*anelēmphthē*, "he was taken up," 1:2, 11, 22) and **ἐπήρθη** (*epērthē*, "he was lifted up," 1:9). The verb *analambanō* is the same used in the LXX for the taking-up of Eliyahu (Elijah, 2 Kgs 2:9-11). The scene then repeats **εἰς τὸν οὐρανόν** ("into the sky/heaven") three times in vv.10-11, framing the disciples' upward gaze. *Ouranos* means both "sky" and "heaven"; the TT renders the visual "sky" here while keeping the heavenly resonance audible. The passives keep the act God's, not the disciples' achievement; the TT preserves the agency-restraint rather than supplying "he ascended."

### A4. *Synalizomenos* — "assembling" or "eating with"?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the ambiguity); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the sense)]**

At 1:4 the participle **συναλιζόμενος** (*synalizomenos*) is genuinely ambiguous. Derived from *syn* + *halizō* ("to gather, assemble"), it means "being assembled together"; but ancient and modern readers have also connected it to *syn* + *hals* ("salt"), yielding "sharing salt / eating together" (so some render "while eating with them," echoing Luke 24:42-43 and Acts 10:41). The Greek does not decide. The TT keeps the assembling sense in the main text and flags the table-fellowship possibility. The choice matters because the second reading would tie the command to a shared meal with the risen Yeshua.

### A5. The eleven, listed — the Twelve minus one
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The roster of 1:13 names exactly eleven (the Twelve without Yehudah Iscariot), in an order close to but not identical with the Gospel lists (Luke 6:14-16; Mark 3:16-19; Matt 10:2-4). **Kefa** (Peter) heads the list, as throughout the tradition. The names distinguished by patronym or epithet — **Ya'aqov son of Halfai** (James son of Alphaeus), **Shimon the Zealot** (*ho zēlōtēs*), and **Yehudah son of Ya'aqov** (Judas son of James) — separate them from Shimon Kefa and from Yehudah Iscariot. The deliberate count of eleven is what the rest of the chapter (vv.15-26) sets out to repair, restoring the Twelve. → For the apostle roster across the Gospels and Acts, see `acts/PEOPLE.md`.

### A6. *Kyrios* across the chapter — three referents the TT tracks
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the referent shift); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for v.24)]**

The noun **κύριος** (*kyrios*) shifts referent across Acts 1, and the TT tracks each instance rather than flattening to a single "Lord." At 1:6 **κύριε** ("Lord") is addressed to the risen Yeshua; at 1:21 **ὁ κύριος Ἰησοῦς** ("the Lord Yeshua") is the title of the risen Jesus by name; at 1:24 the prayer to **κύριε καρδιογνῶστα** ("Lord, knower of hearts") leaves the referent open — God the Father, or the risen Yeshua who is said to have "chosen" the Twelve (1:2).

**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN]** The v.24 referent cannot be settled from the text: the "heart-knower" title is used of God in the HB (1 Sam 16:7; 1 Kgs 8:39), but the immediate context of *choosing* apostles points to Yeshua. The TT keeps "Lord" and notes the ambiguity rather than deciding. The rare vocative *kardiognōsta* ("knower of hearts") appears in the NT only here and at Acts 15:8.

## B. Ancient Near Eastern and Ancient World Parallels

### B1. Ascension and assumption traditions in the ancient world
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

The taking-up of a righteous figure into heaven is a recognizable HB and Second Temple motif. Within the HB, Chanokh (Enoch) "was no more, for God took him" (Gen 5:24), and Eliyahu (Elijah) is taken up in a whirlwind, with chariots of fire, as Elisha watches (2 Kgs 2:9-12) — the closest structural parallel to Acts 1, including the eyewitness who is left to continue the work. Second Temple literature elaborated such ascents (1 Enoch; the *Assumption/Testament of Moses*). The wider Greco-Roman world also told of *apotheosis* — the rise of heroes and emperors to divine status (Romulus; the deified Augustus). Luke's account stays within the Israelite assumption pattern (a cloud, an eyewitness successor, a commission) rather than the imperial apotheosis pattern. The parallels show a shared narrative grammar; they do not establish dependence.

**Source:** Zwiep, A.W., *The Ascension of the Messiah in Lukan Christology*, Brill, 1997, pp. 36-79; Collins, A.Y., "Ascension," in *Anchor Bible Dictionary*, vol. 1, Doubleday, 1992.

### B2. Restoration-of-the-kingdom hope in Second Temple Judaism
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The disciples' question — "are you at this time restoring the kingdom to Israel?" (1:6, *apokathistaneis tēn basileian tō Israēl*) — voices a widely attested Second Temple hope for national restoration: the regathering of the tribes, the renewal of David's throne, and freedom from foreign rule. The Psalms of Solomon (17-18, 1st c. BCE) pray for a Davidic king to "purge Jerusalem" and gather a holy people; the Qumran texts (e.g. 4Q174, the *Florilegium*) read the Davidic promise of 2 Samuel 7 eschatologically; the Eighteen Benedictions petition for the ingathering and the re-establishment of judges and the Davidic line. The verb *apokathistēmi* ("restore") and its noun *apokatastasis* (Acts 3:21) belong to this restoration vocabulary. The risen Yeshua does not reject the hope but reframes its timing (1:7) and means (1:8).

**Source:** Psalms of Solomon, in Charlesworth, J.H. (ed.), *The Old Testament Pseudepigrapha*, vol. 2, Doubleday, 1985; Schürer, E., *The History of the Jewish People in the Age of Jesus Christ*, rev. Vermes, Millar, Black, T&T Clark, 1979, vol. 2, pp. 488-554.

### B3. Casting lots as discernment of the divine will
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

The choice of Mattityahu (Matthias) by lot (1:26) draws on a deep HB and Second Temple practice of using the lot (*klēros*; Hebrew *goral*) to discern God's choice where human judgment should not decide. Proverbs 16:33 states the principle: "the lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from YHWH." The land of Canaan was apportioned by lot (Num 26:55; Josh 18:6-10); the Yom Kippur goats were assigned by lot (Lev 16:8); Shaul (Saul) was identified as king by lot (1 Sam 10:20-21); the priestly and Temple-service duties were assigned by lot (1 Chr 24-26; cf. Luke 1:9). The Qumran community also used lot-language for assigning rank and membership (1QS). Acts 1:26 is the **last** lot-casting in Acts; after Pentecost the {a:wind/spirit} guides directly (e.g. 13:2). The parallels place the act firmly within Israelite discernment practice.

**Source:** Lindblom, J., "Lot-Casting in the Old Testament," *Vetus Testamentum* 12 (1962), pp. 164-178; Barrett, C.K., *A Critical and Exegetical Commentary on the Acts of the Apostles*, ICC, T&T Clark, 1994, vol. 1, pp. 101-103.

## C. Historical Context

### C1. The "brothers of Jesus" and the family in the early community
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Acts 1:14 places **Miryam** (Mary) the mother of Yeshua and "his brothers" (*hoi adelphoi autou*) in the praying community — Mary's last appearance in the narrative. One of these brothers, Ya'aqov (James), emerges as the leader of the Jerusalem assembly (Acts 15; Gal 1:19; the Letter of James) and is named by Josephus, who reports his execution c. 62 CE (*Antiquities* 20.200). The precise sense of *adelphoi* — full siblings, half-siblings, or wider kin (cousins) — was debated already in antiquity and remains so; the TT keeps the plain "brothers." Their presence signals that Jesus's own family, initially reserved in the Gospels (Mark 3:21, 31-35; John 7:5), is now within the movement.

**Source:** Bauckham, R., *Jude and the Relatives of Jesus in the Early Church*, T&T Clark, 1990, pp. 19-44; Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities* 20.197-203, Loeb Classical Library.

### C2. The upper room, the hundred and twenty, and the early community
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The group gathers in an **ὑπερῷον** (*hyperōon*, "upper room," 1:13) — the upper story of a Jerusalem house, the kind of larger room used for meals and gatherings (cf. Luke 22:12; Acts 20:8). The count of "about a hundred and twenty" (1:15) is plausibly significant: in later rabbinic reckoning, 120 men was the quorum required to constitute a town council with a sanhedrin (Mishnah, *Sanhedrin* 1:6), and the number is also 12 × 10. Whether Luke intends a quorum signal or simply a round figure cannot be settled, but the detail fits a community organizing itself as a recognizable body within Jerusalem's Jewish life.

**Source:** Barrett, C.K., *Acts*, ICC, vol. 1, 1994, pp. 96-97; Jeremias, J., *Jerusalem in the Time of Jesus*, Fortress Press, 1969, pp. 252-262.

### C3. The Mount of Olives, a sabbath's journey, and Zechariah 14
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED (for the geography); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the Zech 14 resonance)]**

The ascension is staged on **Har haZeitim** (the Mount of Olives, 1:12), the ridge east of Jerusalem across the Kidron valley, described as "a sabbath's journey" from the city — about 2,000 cubits (~1 km), the maximum distance permitted for travel on the sabbath under the developing halakhah (cf. *Sotah* 5:3; the limit derived from Num 35:5; Josh 3:4). The detail is topographically exact. The Mount of Olives is also where Zechariah 14:4 sets YHWH's eschatological coming ("his feet will stand on the Mount of Olives"), which may give the location added resonance with the messengers' promise of return (1:11); the text states the geography directly and only implies the prophetic echo.

**Source:** Murphy-O'Connor, J., *The Holy Land: An Oxford Archaeological Guide*, 5th ed., Oxford University Press, 2008, pp. 144-150; Schürer, E., *History of the Jewish People*, rev. ed., T&T Clark, 1979, vol. 2, pp. 472-475 (on sabbath limits).

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. *Baptisthēsesthe en pneumati* — the immersion promise
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexemes); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE (for the wind/spirit resonance)]**

At 1:5 the risen Yeshua contrasts John's water-immersion with the coming gift: **ὑμεῖς… βαπτισθήσεσθε ἐν πνεύματι ἁγίῳ** (*hymeis baptisthēsesthe en pneumati hagiō*), "you will be immersed in *the* holy {a:wind/spirit}." **βαπτίζω** (*baptizō*) = "to dip, immerse"; the TT renders "immerse / immersion" (per the locked GS glossary) rather than the transliterated loanword "baptize." The phrase fulfills the word of Yochanan (John) the Immerser — "I immerse you in water… he will immerse you in *the* holy {a:wind/spirit} and fire" (Luke 3:16; Matt 3:11; Mark 1:8; John 1:33) — and is realized at Pentecost (Acts 2).

The noun **πνεῦμα** (*pneuma*) carries the live range "wind / spirit / breath"; the TT keeps the slash (Rule 2). The construction is anarthrous (no article); the TT supplies "*the*" in italics. The same wind/spirit "immersion" image is reactivated at Pentecost by the "rushing violent wind" (*pnoē*, 2:2) and the tongues "as of fire" (2:3), where the wind sense becomes audible. **[lexical-theological — PROBABLE]** that the chapter intends the full wind/spirit field, not a narrowed "Spirit" only.

**Source:** Liddell, H.G. and Scott, R., *Greek-English Lexicon*, 9th ed., rev. Jones, 1940, s.v. βαπτίζω, πνεῦμα; Dunn, J.D.G., *Baptism in the Holy Spirit*, SCM Press, 1970, pp. 38-54.

### D2. *Apokathistanō* / *apokatastasis* — "restore" and the kingdom question
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The disciples' verb at 1:6, **ἀποκαθιστάνεις** (*apokathistaneis*, "you are restoring"), is from *apokathistēmi*, "to restore to a former state, re-establish." It is the LXX's standard word for the eschatological restoration of Israel — the regathering of the exiles and the renewal of the land and throne (e.g. Jer 16:15; 24:6; Hos 11:11 LXX). The cognate noun **ἀποκατάστασις** (*apokatastasis*, "restoration") returns at Acts 3:21 ("the restoration of all things"). The TT renders "restore / restoration" and keeps the national-restoration sense the disciples plainly intend (note the dative **τῷ Ἰσραήλ**, "to Israel") rather than spiritualizing it. The risen Yeshua's reply (1:7) corrects the *timing* (*chronous ē kairous*, "times or seasons"), not the hope itself.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. ἀποκαθίστημι, ἀποκατάστασις; Barrett, C.K., *Acts*, ICC, vol. 1, 1994, pp. 75-77.

### D3. *Klēros* and *episkopē* — the lot and the office
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Two words bind the Judas-replacement scene. **κλῆρος** (*klēros*) carries the linked senses "lot / portion / share": Yehudah (Judas) had "obtained the *klēros* of this service" (1:17), then "they gave *klērous*… and the *klēros* fell on" Matthias (1:26) — the office is a portion *and* the means of filling it is a lot. **ἐπισκοπή** (*episkopē*, 1:20, citing Ps 109:8 LXX) = "oversight, office, visitation"; the TT renders "overseership" to keep the office sense rather than importing the later ecclesiastical "bishopric," which would read a developed church order back into the text. The word-play between the office (*klēros*) and the lot (*klēros*) is live in the Greek and the TT keeps it visible.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. κλῆρος, ἐπισκοπή; Bauer, W., *A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament*, rev. Danker (BDAG), 3rd ed., University of Chicago Press, 2000, s.v. ἐπισκοπή.

### D4. *Hakeldama* — the Aramaic that glosses itself
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

At 1:19 the narrator reports that the field was called, "in their own language" (*tē idia dialektō*), **Ἁκελδαμάχ** (*Hakeldamach*) — a transliteration of Aramaic *ḥaqel demā*, "field of blood" — and then supplies the meaning: "that is, Field of Blood." The verse is a built-in author's gloss: Luke transliterates the Aramaic term and translates it for his Greek readers. The TT keeps both, marking the transliterated term {t:Hakeldama} and preserving the author's own rendering rather than collapsing them. "Their own language" identifies the Aramaic of Jerusalem's inhabitants, confirming Aramaic as the vernacular of the city. The final consonant -χ likely reflects the Aramaic determined ending heard by a Greek ear.

**Source:** Fitzmyer, J.A., *The Acts of the Apostles*, Anchor Bible 31, Doubleday, 1998, pp. 224-225; Wilcox, M., *The Semitisms of Acts*, Oxford University Press, 1965, pp. 87-89.

## F. Later Reception in Other Traditions

### F1. The forty days, the ascension, and the liturgical calendar
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The "forty days" of resurrection appearances (1:3) and the ascension (1:9-11) became fixed points of the Christian calendar: Ascension is observed forty days after Easter, and the interval to Pentecost (Acts 2) structures the Easter season in Eastern and Western liturgies from late antiquity onward. The number forty also drew the ascension into typological reading alongside Israel's forty wilderness years and Jesus's forty-day testing (Luke 4:2). These are reception-history developments built on the verse, not data of the Greek; the text gives only the interval and the event. The TT renders the plain "forty days" and leaves the calendar to its history.

**Source:** Bradshaw, P.F., *The Search for the Origins of Christian Worship*, 2nd ed., Oxford University Press, 2002, pp. 178-184; Talley, T.J., *The Origins of the Liturgical Year*, 2nd ed., Liturgical Press, 1991.

### F2. Judas's death, harmonization, and Papias
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The divergence between Acts 1:18-19 (Judas buys the field; falls headlong and bursts open) and Matthew 27:3-10 (Judas returns the silver and hangs himself; the priests buy the field) generated harmonizing efforts from antiquity. Augustine and later interpreters proposed that Judas hanged himself and then the body fell and burst (combining both accounts); Papias (early 2nd c., preserved in later catenae) offered a third, grotesquely expanded tradition of Judas swelling monstrously before dying. These are documented attempts at reconciliation or elaboration, none demonstrable from the texts. Per the Prime Directive (Rule 3), the TT renders Acts on its own terms and does not harmonize the two; see the Acts 1 main text, v.18.

**Source:** Metzger, B.M. and Ehrman, B.D., *The Text of the New Testament*, 4th ed., Oxford University Press, 2005 (on Papias fragments); Fitzmyer, J.A., *Acts*, Anchor Bible 31, 1998, pp. 220-224.

### F3. The succession of Matthias and apostolic-office tradition
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The election of Mattityahu (Matthias, 1:15-26) became foundational in later Christian reflection on church order and "apostolic succession" — the idea of a continuing, transmissible office. Eusebius and others noted Matthias as one of the seventy (Luke 10:1) before his enrollment among the Twelve; apocryphal *Acts of Andrew and Matthias* later expanded his legend. The criteria stated in 1:21-22 (companionship from John's immersion to the ascension, and witness to the resurrection) were also invoked in debates over who could count as an "apostle." These are documented receptions; the text itself states only a one-time replacement to restore the Twelve.

**Source:** Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 1.12; 3.25, Loeb Classical Library; Barrett, C.K., *Acts*, ICC, vol. 1, 1994, pp. 98-104.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. The unfinished *men… (de)* of verse 1
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the data); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the explanation)]**

Acts 1:1 opens with **μέν** ("on the one hand," *ton men prōton logon*), which in classical Greek normally anticipates a matching **δέ** ("on the other hand") — here "the present account." But no explicit *de* clause follows. Whether this is a deliberate idiom (*men* used "solitarily," common in Koine), a stylistic loose end, or an implied contrast carried by the whole second volume is debated. The TT preserves the suspended construction rather than supplying the missing clause. It is a small window onto Luke's Greek register at the seam between his two books.

### G2. Two ascension accounts, two intervals
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Luke narrates the ascension twice — once at the close of the Gospel (Luke 24:50-53), apparently on the day of the resurrection, and once here (Acts 1:9-11), explicitly after "forty days" (1:3). The two notices differ in their time-frame, and interpreters have long discussed whether the Gospel telescopes events that Acts spaces out, or whether the accounts simply serve different narrative purposes (the Gospel rounding off a life; Acts launching a mission). The TT renders each account as it stands and does not harmonize the chronology, recording the difference rather than smoothing it.

### G3. Why replace Judas, but not later apostles?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the data); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the inference)]**

The community moves quickly to restore the Twelve to twelve (1:15-26), yet when Ya'aqov (James) son of Zavdai is killed (Acts 12:2) no replacement is sought, and the "Twelve" as a fixed body fades from the narrative thereafter. The most common inference is that the number twelve had a one-time, foundational symbolism — the reconstitution of Israel's tribal structure for the launch of the mission — rather than a perpetual office to be kept filled. The text states the replacement and the silence; the rationale is an inference, reasonable but not stated.

### SCENARIO A — Pre-70 CE (Temple still standing)

#### IA-1. Political — Roman Judea under the prefecture, c. 30 CE
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Acts 1 is set in Jerusalem around 30 CE, in the years when Judea was a Roman province governed by a prefect of equestrian rank — Pontius Pilate held the post c. 26-36 CE — under the legate of Syria, while Galilee and the northeast remained under Herodian client-tetrarchs (Antipas, Philip). Day-to-day Jewish affairs in Jerusalem ran through the high-priestly establishment and the Sanhedrin, with the prefect intervening on taxation, capital cases, and order, and bringing troops up from Caesarea at the pilgrim festivals. For the chapter's first audience, a small Jesus-movement gathering in an upper room and organizing itself in Jerusalem did so within this layered structure of Roman, priestly, and local authority — politically powerless, legally exposed, and as yet unremarkable to the authorities.

**Source:** Smallwood, E.M., *The Jews under Roman Rule from Pompey to Diocletian*, Brill, 1976, pp. 144-180; Schürer, E., *History of the Jewish People*, vol. 1, T&T Clark, 1973, pp. 357-398.

#### IA-2. Religion — The festival-pilgrimage city and the functioning Temple
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The narrative is framed by Jerusalem's pilgrimage rhythm: the disciples are told to wait in the city (1:4), and Pentecost (Shavuot), one of the three pilgrim festivals (Deut 16:16), is approaching (Acts 2:1). At such festivals Jerusalem's population swelled enormously with pilgrims from across Judea and the diaspora; the Temple, expanded by Herod into one of the largest sacred precincts of the ancient world, was the functioning center of sacrifice, priestly rotation, and prayer (cf. Luke 1; Acts 3:1). For a pre-70 reader, the Jesus-movement's continued attachment to Jerusalem and the Temple (Acts 2:46; 3:1) was the natural setting of a Jewish renewal group, not a separate religion. The whole frame assumes the institution that 70 CE would destroy.

**Source:** Sanders, E.P., *Judaism: Practice and Belief, 63 BCE–66 CE*, SCM Press, 1992, pp. 47-145; Jeremias, J., *Jerusalem in the Time of Jesus*, Fortress Press, 1969, pp. 73-84.

#### IA-3. Religion — The Jesus-movement as a Jewish renewal group
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

In c. 30 CE the followers of Yeshua (Jesus) formed one current within the diverse Judaism of the period — alongside Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, the Qumran community, and various prophetic and renewal movements. The Acts 1 community is thoroughly Jewish in idiom: it prays in the Temple's city, reckons sabbath distances (1:12), reads the Psalms as prophecy (1:20), discerns by lot (1:26), and frames its hope as the "restoration of the kingdom to Israel" (1:6). The reconstitution of the Twelve as Israel's tribal number (1:15-26) presents the group as a movement of Israel's renewal, not a breakaway sect. The later self-designations ("the Way," Acts 9:2; "Nazarenes," 24:5) and the eventual name "Christians" at Antioch (11:26) lay in the future.

**Source:** Dunn, J.D.G., *The Partings of the Ways*, 2nd ed., SCM Press, 2006, pp. 1-17; Cohen, S.J.D., *From the Maccabees to the Mishnah*, 3rd ed., Westminster John Knox, 2014, pp. 143-174.

#### IA-4. Daily Life — Aramaic, Greek, and the languages of the city
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Acts 1 reflects the multilingual reality of 1st-century Jerusalem. The narrator notes that the field was named *Hakeldama* "in their own language" (1:19) — Aramaic, the everyday vernacular of Judea — while the book itself is written in Greek, the lingua franca of the eastern Mediterranean and of the diaspora pilgrims who appear in Acts 2. Hebrew remained the language of scripture and much of the liturgy. This layering — Aramaic at home, Greek for the wider world, Hebrew for the sacred text — is exactly the situation the Pentecost narrative of the next chapter dramatizes, when diaspora Jews hear "in our own languages" (2:8-11). The Aramaic gloss in 1:19 is a small, concrete trace of that lived multilingualism.

**Source:** Wilcox, M., *The Semitisms of Acts*, Oxford University Press, 1965; Millar, F., *The Roman Near East, 31 BC – AD 337*, Harvard University Press, 1993, pp. 337-366.

### SCENARIO B — Post-70 CE (Temple destroyed)

#### IB-1. Religion — Reading the Jerusalem-and-Temple frame after the fall
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

For a reader after 70 CE — the most common dating window for the composition of Luke-Acts — the chapter's Jerusalem setting depicted a center that had been destroyed: the Temple razed, the priestly courses ended, the pilgrim festivals as Temple events gone. The command to wait *in Jerusalem* (1:4) and the movement's attachment to the city would read with hindsight as the starting point of a mission that, by the time of writing, had spread "unto the end of the earth" (1:8) precisely as the witness-geography promised — even as Jerusalem itself fell. The Davidic-restoration hope of 1:6, after the catastrophic defeat of the revolt, would be read increasingly as eschatological rather than immediately political.

**Source:** Goodman, M., *Rome and Jerusalem: The Clash of Ancient Civilizations*, Allen Lane, 2007; Esler, P.F., *Community and Gospel in Luke-Acts*, Cambridge University Press, 1987, pp. 131-163.

#### IB-2. Social Structure — A mission outward from a fallen center
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

By the time of writing, the small upper-room community of 1:13-15 had, in the narrative's own telling, become a movement spread across the Roman world — and the historical communities that read Acts were largely diaspora congregations, increasingly Gentile, living far from Judea. For such readers, the careful establishment of the movement's Jerusalem origins, its Jewish credentials, and its continuity with Israel's restoration hope (the Twelve, the Psalms-as-prophecy, the lot) functioned as an assertion of legitimate descent: the worldwide mission of their own day was grounded in the Jerusalem of the apostles and in Israel's covenant story. The "end of the earth" horizon (1:8) addressed exactly their situation.

**Source:** Esler, P.F., *Community and Gospel in Luke-Acts*, Cambridge University Press, 1987; Sterling, G.E., *Historiography and Self-Definition: Josephos, Luke-Acts and Apologetic Historiography*, Brill, 1992, pp. 311-389.
