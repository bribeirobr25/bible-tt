## HOW TO USE THIS COMPANION

This file contains background material organized by topic. Each entry carries **two labels:**

**What kind of claim?** (tells you the source of the information)
- **TEXTUAL** — derived directly from the Greek text
- **STRONG INFERENCE** — logically entailed by the text, not stated explicitly
- **POSSIBLE INFERENCE** — one legitimate reading among others
- **COMPARATIVE PARALLEL** — attested in another ancient source
- **LATER RECEPTION** — post-biblical reading
- **HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL** — based on material or historical evidence
- **SPECULATION** — no strong textual or evidential basis

**How certain?** (tells you the confidence level)
- **VERIFIED** — strong evidence, wide agreement
- **PROBABLE** — well-supported but debated
- **POSSIBLE** — one reasonable reading among others
- **UNCERTAIN** — genuinely unclear
- **SPECULATIVE** — must be rare and clearly flagged
- **DOCUMENTED** — historically attested reception (for LATER RECEPTION entries)

**Reading principle:** Start with TEXTUAL entries — those come from the Greek itself. Then explore outward to parallels, reception, and comparison. The companion presents; you assess.

## A. Source Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. "The name" as the channel of power and salvation
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

**ὄνομα** (*onoma*, "name") is the keyword of the chapter. The healing is performed "in the name of Yeshua *the* Messiah the Nazarene" (3:6), and the explanation repeats it: "upon the faith of his name… his name made *him* firm" (3:16, with *stereoō* tying back to the ankles "made firm" in v.7). Kefa (Peter) explicitly deflects credit from the apostles — "not by our own power or piety" (3:12) — so that "the name," not the wonder-worker, is the agent.

The motif runs through the early chapters of Acts (cf. 2:21, 38; 4:12). In Semitic idiom "the name" stands for the person and his authoritative presence; invoking it is not magical technique but reliance on the one named. The TT keeps the literal "*the* name" rather than paraphrasing ("by Jesus' authority"), so the recurring keyword stays visible. → For "the name" in the immediate sermon, see the verse-by-verse notes at 3:6 and 3:16.

### A2. The Servant (*pais*) and the *archēgos* — two loaded titles for Yeshua
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexemes); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE (for the Isaianic referent)]**

Two Christological terms in the sermon are deliberately open. **παῖς** (*pais*, 3:13, 26), "servant" or "child," is applied to Yeshua under the heading "the God of Avraham (Abraham)… glorified his *pais*" — language that draws on the Servant of Isaiah 52:13 ("my servant… shall be glorified," LXX *doxasthēsetai*). **ἀρχηγός** (*archēgos*, 3:15), "leader / founder / pioneer / originator / author," is set in sharp paradox: "the *archēgos* of life you killed, whom God raised."

The TT renders *pais* "servant" (noting the child/servant range) and *archēgos* "Author *of life*" (flagging the full semantic spread). Neither term is collapsed to a single later title. → For the lexical analysis of both, see §D1 (*archēgos*) and §D2 (*pais*); for the Servant-Song citation mechanics, see the PROPHECY companion.

### A3. The suffering Messiah as "all the prophets"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the claim); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the specific texts in view)]**

The sermon states that "God… foretold through *the* mouth of all the prophets — that his Messiah would suffer — he thus fulfilled" (3:18, *pathein ton Christon autou*). The claim that the prophetic corpus as a whole pointed to a *suffering* anointed one is the interpretive core of the early proclamation (cf. Luke 24:26-27, 46; Acts 17:3; 26:22-23). The chapter does not cite a single proof-text for the suffering itself; it asserts a reading of the prophets collectively, with Isaiah 53 the most natural anchor.

The TT renders "his Messiah would suffer" plainly and does not insert the specific texts. **[STRONG INFERENCE — PROBABLE]** that Isaiah 52:13–53:12 stands behind the *pais*-glorified-after-suffering pattern (§A2), but the verse names no chapter, and the TT does not supply one. → See the PROPHECY companion for the Servant-Song citations.

### A4. "Change of mind" + "turning" — the doubled call (*metanoeō* + *epistrephō*)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The appeal pairs two verbs: **μετανοήσατε… καὶ ἐπιστρέψατε** (*metanoēsate… kai epistrepsate*, 3:19), "change *your* minds… and turn." *Metanoeō* is the locked GS rendering "change of mind" (cf. 2:38; Luke 3:3, 8); *epistrephō*, "turn (back) [to God]," renders the prophetic call to *shuv*, "return." The goal is stated as **εἰς τὸ ἐξαλειφθῆναι** ("for the wiping out") of sins — *exaleiphō*, "wipe away, erase," a ledger or writing-tablet image.

The TT keeps both verbs distinct rather than merging them into a single "repent," preserving the inner-change-plus-outward-return pair that the Greek holds together. The construction leaves open whether the "wiping out" is the purpose or the result of the turning; the TT renders "for the wiping out" without resolving the directionality (cf. the *eis* crux flagged at 2:38). → see the verse-by-verse note at 3:19.

### A5. "Times of refreshing" and the "restoration of all things"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexemes); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the scope)]**

Two rare eschatological phrases sit together. **καιροὶ ἀναψύξεως** (*kairoi anapsyxeōs*, 3:20), "times of refreshing," uses *anapsyxis* ("relief, refreshing, cooling"), a word rare in the Greek Bible (cf. Exod 8:15 LXX). **χρόνοι ἀποκαταστάσεως πάντων** (*chronoi apokatastaseōs pantōn*, 3:21), "times of restoration of all *things*," uses *apokatastasis* ("restoration, re-establishment"), whose scope is genuinely debated.

The TT renders both literally and does not decide whether "restoration of all things" means cosmic renewal, Israel's restoration, or a universal scope. The same root appears at 1:6 ("restore the kingdom to Israel"), which pulls toward an Israel-restoration sense, while the "all things" reaches wider. **[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN]** for the intended scope. → For the lexical and reception range of *apokatastasis*, see §D3 and §F2.

### A6. Divine speech, Moses's reported speech, and the marking decision (Rule 30)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The chapter mixes voices, and the TT's Rule-30 marking tracks them. Kefa's sermon (vv.12-26) is **human** speech and is not marked. The Moses citation (vv.22-23, Deut 18:15-19) is **Moses's** reported speech *about* the LORD ("*the* Lord God will raise up a prophet…"), so it is likewise not divine-marked. But the Abrahamic word (v.25, "And in your seed all the families of the earth will be blessed," Gen 22:18) is **the LORD's own direct covenant speech**, and is marked `@@…@@` (per editorial decision A-004).

The result is that within a single human sermon the only marked words are the one clause where God speaks in his own voice. The TT does not flatten the distinction between a prophet quoting God and God speaking. → For the marking policy across Acts 1–3, see the editorial log (Rule 30 scope).

## B. Ancient Near Eastern and Ancient World Parallels

### B1. Healing at a sanctuary and the lame leaping — Isaiah 35
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

The healed man "leaping" (*hallomenos*, 3:8) into the temple echoes Isaiah 35:6 LXX ("then the lame will leap like a deer"), part of the prophetic vision of the restored people on the highway home. The motif of the disabled made whole as a sign of the in-breaking age recurs in the Gospels (Luke 7:22; Matt 11:5, also drawing on Isa 35). Placing the sign at the temple, the symbolic center of Israel's worship, binds the healing to the hope of national restoration rather than presenting it as an isolated miracle.

The parallel establishes a shared scriptural symbolism — disability reversed as a marker of the promised age — not a borrowing or a claim about the historical event. The TT keeps "leaping" rather than smoothing it to "walking," so the Isaiah resonance stays audible.

**Source:** Beale, G.K. and Carson, D.A. (eds.), *Commentary on the New Testament Use of the Old Testament*, Baker Academic, 2007, pp. 543-547 (on Acts 3); Pao, D.W., *Acts and the Isaianic New Exodus*, Mohr Siebeck, 2000, pp. 84-91.

### B2. "The name" and power in the ancient world
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The first-century Mediterranean world knew a wide repertoire of healing and exorcism "by a name" — Greek magical papyri invoke divine and angelic names as efficacious formulae, and Josephus reports Jewish exorcists using Solomon's name and adjurations (*Antiquities* 8.2.5 §45-49). Acts itself later contrasts genuine invocation with attempted imitation (the sons of Sceva, 19:13-16). Against this backdrop, the sermon's insistence that the healing is "upon the faith of his name" and "not by our own power" (3:12, 16) draws a deliberate line: not a technique mastered by the apostles but reliance on the named person, raised by God.

The parallel illuminates why a name-formula would be intelligible to the crowd; it does not make the early proclamation a species of magic — the text frames it as faith, not incantation. The TT keeps the literal "the name" so the reader can weigh the comparison.

**Source:** Betz, H.D. (ed.), *The Greek Magical Papyri in Translation*, 2nd ed., University of Chicago Press, 1992; Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities*, trans. H.St.J. Thackeray and R. Marcus, Loeb Classical Library, 8.45-49.

## C. Historical Context

### C1. The hour of prayer and the evening sacrifice
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Kefa and Yochanan go up "at the hour of prayer, the ninth" (3:1) — about 3 p.m. by the day-from-dawn reckoning, the time of the evening *tamid* (the perpetual daily sacrifice) and its accompanying public prayer and incense offering. Second Temple practice associated set hours with prayer (cf. Dan 6:10; the morning and evening *tamid* of Exod 29:38-42; Num 28:3-8), and the assembled people prayed at the hours of offering (cf. Luke 1:10). The detail shows the earliest community still keeping the temple's worship-times — within Israel's cult, not apart from it (cf. Acts 2:46).

The TT renders "the hour of prayer, the ninth" literally, leaving the modern clock-equivalent to this note. The narrative thereby locates the healing not on the margins but at the heart of Israel's daily worship.

**Source:** Sanders, E.P., *Judaism: Practice and Belief, 63 BCE–66 CE*, SCM Press, 1992, pp. 77-118; Schürer, E., *The History of the Jewish People in the Age of Jesus Christ*, rev. Vermes, Millar, Black, T&T Clark, 1979, vol. 2, pp. 299-313.

### C2. The Beautiful Gate — a location debate
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — UNCERTAIN]**

The "gate of the temple called Beautiful" (*tēn hōraian*, 3:2, 10) is not attested by that name in any other source, which makes its identification genuinely uncertain. The leading proposal is the **Nicanor Gate**, the bronze gate (later sources call its metal "more precious than silver") between the Court of the Women and the Court of Israel, described by Josephus (*Jewish War* 5.5.3 §201-206) and the Mishnah (*Middot* 1:4; 2:3); some scholars instead favor an outer gate (the Shushan or an eastern gate) on the grounds that a beggar would sit where the public, including Gentiles, passed. The evidence does not settle the question.

The TT keeps "the gate… called Beautiful" without identifying it, and the verse-by-verse note records the Nicanor-Gate possibility as the leading but unproven candidate. The narrative geography (the man "enters" the temple, then the crowd gathers at the eastern Solomon's portico, §C3) is compatible with more than one reconstruction.

**Source:** Josephus, *Jewish War* 5.198-206, Loeb Classical Library; Bauckham, R., "The Beautiful Gate," in *The Book of Acts in Its Palestinian Setting* (ed. R. Bauckham), Eerdmans, 1995, pp. 327-340.

### C3. Solomon's portico as the community's gathering place
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The crowd runs together "at the portico called *Solomon's* (Shlomoh's)" (3:11), a roofed colonnade running along the inside of the eastern wall of the outer court (the Court of the Gentiles). Josephus attributes its origin to Solomon's original construction, retained when the platform was rebuilt (*Antiquities* 20.9.7 §220-221; *Jewish War* 5.5.1 §184-185). In Acts and John it is a recurring meeting-place: the early community gathered there (Acts 5:12), and Yeshua had walked there (John 10:23).

The portico was a sheltered, public space within the temple precincts where teaching and assembly naturally happened, which fits its role as the setting for the sermon. The TT keeps "Solomon's portico" and the verse-note locates it on the temple's east side.

**Source:** Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities* 20.220-221, Loeb Classical Library; Bahat, D., *The Illustrated Atlas of Jerusalem*, Carta, 1990, pp. 50-63.

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. *Archēgos* — "leader / pioneer / originator / author"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the range); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the single best gloss)]**

**ἀρχηγός** (*archēgos*, 3:15) is a compound of *archē* ("beginning, origin, rule") and a root tied to *agō* ("lead"), and its attested senses span "founder," "originator," "first cause," "leader / chief," and "pioneer / one who goes first." In the LXX it renders Hebrew terms for a tribal "head" or military "leader" (e.g. Num 13:2-3; Judg 5:15; 1 Chr 5:24). In the NT it occurs only here, at 5:31 ("leader and savior"), and at Hebrews 2:10 and 12:2 ("the *archēgos* of their salvation," "the *archēgos*… of faith"). The genitive "of life" (*tēs zōēs*) makes the paradox of 3:15 acute: the one who originates and leads into life was the one they "killed."

No single English word captures the whole field — "Author" foregrounds origination, "Pioneer" foregrounds leading-the-way, "Leader / Prince" the rank. The TT renders "Author *of life*" and flags the range rather than committing to one nuance. **[lexical — UNCERTAIN]** which single English word is intended; the breadth is the point.

**Source:** Liddell, H.G. and Scott, R., *Greek-English Lexicon*, 9th ed., rev. Jones, 1940, s.v. ἀρχηγός; Bauer, W., *A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament* (BDAG), 3rd ed., rev. Danker, University of Chicago Press, 2000, s.v. ἀρχηγός.

### D2. *Pais* — "servant" or "child," and the Servant of the LORD
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the range); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE (for the Isaianic referent)]**

**παῖς** (*pais*, 3:13, 26) carries two distinct senses: "child / son" and "servant / slave (in a household)." In the LXX *pais theou* / *pais kyriou* ("servant of God / of the Lord") is a standard rendering for the Hebrew *ʿeved YHWH*, including the "Servant" of the Isaianic Servant Songs (Isa 42:1; 49:6; 52:13). Acts uses *pais* of Yeshua at 3:13, 26 and 4:27, 30, and of David at 4:25 — a vocabulary cluster that points toward the LXX servant-of-the-Lord idiom rather than the "child" sense.

The clinching link is verbal: 3:13 says God "glorified" (*edoxasen*) his *pais*, while Isaiah 52:13 LXX says the servant "shall be glorified" (*doxasthēsetai*). The TT renders "servant" and notes the child/servant range; the Servant-Song background is **probable** but not made explicit by the verse, so the TT does not insert "Isaiah's Servant" into the text. → For the suffering-Messiah claim that rests on the same Servant material, see §A3 and the PROPHECY companion.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. παῖς; Bock, D.L., *Acts*, Baker Exegetical Commentary, Baker Academic, 2007, pp. 169-172; Ottley, R.R. (and the LXX of Isaiah), in Hatch, E. and Redpath, H.A., *A Concordance to the Septuagint*, 2nd ed., Baker, 1998, s.v. παῖς.

### D3. *Apokatastasis* — "restoration of all things" and the scope question
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexeme); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the scope)]**

**ἀποκατάστασις** (*apokatastasis*, 3:21) is "restoration, re-establishment, return to a former state" — used in Hellenistic Greek of the return of stars to their original positions and of the restoration of political or natural order. The cognate verb *apokathistēmi* appears at 1:6 ("restore the kingdom to Israel") and in the Gospels of Elijah who "will restore all things" (Mark 9:12; cf. Mal 3:24 [Eng. 4:6]). The phrase here, "until *the* times of restoration of all *things*, of which God spoke through… his holy prophets," ties the restoration to the prophetic hope of Israel's renewal.

The scope is contested: (a) Israel's eschatological restoration (matching 1:6 and Malachi's Elijah); (b) cosmic renewal of creation; (c) a universal restoration of all beings — the sense later traditions debated (§F2). The Greek "of all things" (*pantōn*) is broad but undetermined by this verse; the TT renders literally and **does not** resolve the scope. **[lexical-theological — UNCERTAIN]**.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. ἀποκατάστασις; Bauer, *BDAG*, s.v. ἀποκατάστασις; Mealand, D.L., "The Restoration of All Things: Acts 3:21," in *Journal of Theological Studies* (relevant discussion), Oxford University Press.

### D4. *Stereoō* and *holoklēria* — the vocabulary of "made firm" and "wholeness"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Two health-words bind the sign to its interpretation. **στερεόω** (*stereoō*, "make firm, solid, strong") describes the ankles "made firm" at the moment of healing (3:7) and recurs at 3:16, "his name **made** *him* **firm**" — so the physical firmness and the firmness given by the name share one verb. **ὁλοκληρία** (*holoklēria*, 3:16), "wholeness, complete soundness," from *holos* ("whole") + *klēros* ("lot, portion"), denotes integrity of every part — a NT *hapax legomenon* (occurring only here).

The TT renders *stereoō* "made firm" in both verses to keep the deliberate verbal echo, and *holoklēria* "wholeness" rather than a flatter "perfect health." The doubled, crowded syntax of v.16 (faith / the name / this man / the faith through him) is preserved rather than smoothed. → see the dense verse-note at 3:16.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. στερεόω, ὁλοκληρία; Bauer, *BDAG*, s.v. ὁλοκληρία.

## F. Later Reception in Other Traditions

### F1. The Beautiful Gate in Christian art and pilgrimage tradition
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The healing at the Beautiful Gate became a fixed subject in Christian art from late antiquity (it appears in early cycles of apostolic acts and in medieval and Renaissance painting, e.g. Raphael's tapestry cartoons), and Byzantine and later pilgrimage traditions identified physical gates of the temple platform with the "Beautiful Gate," sometimes conflating it with the eastern (Golden) Gate. These identifications are devotional and artistic developments, not data about the first-century gate, whose location remains uncertain (§C2).

The TT records the location debate and does not adopt any of the later identifications. These are documented receptions of the scene, not evidence for its historical setting.

**Source:** Schiller, G., *Iconography of Christian Art*, trans. J. Seligman, Lund Humphries, 1972; Wilkinson, J., *Jerusalem Pilgrims before the Crusades*, Aris & Phillips, 1977.

### F2. *Apokatastasis* and the universal-restoration debate
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The phrase "restoration of all things" (3:21) became a key text in later Christian debates over the scope of final salvation. Origen of Alexandria (3rd century) and some who followed him used *apokatastasis* for a doctrine of the ultimate restoration of all rational beings — a position later condemned in some church councils; other interpreters restricted the term to Israel's restoration or to cosmic renewal. The doctrinal history shows the verse's openness was felt early.

This is reception and doctrinal history, not a datum of the Greek; the lexical situation (§D3) leaves the scope undetermined, and the TT renders the phrase literally without adjudicating the dispute.

**Source:** Ramelli, I.L.E., *The Christian Doctrine of Apokatastasis*, Brill, 2013; Kelly, J.N.D., *Early Christian Doctrines*, 5th ed., A&C Black, 1977, pp. 473-474.

### F3. The prophet-like-Moses in Jewish and Samaritan expectation
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

Deuteronomy 18:15-19, cited at 3:22-23, fed a stream of "prophet-like-Moses" expectation in the Second Temple and later periods. The Qumran texts list a coming prophet alongside messianic figures (1QS 9:11; the *Testimonia*, 4Q175, quotes Deut 18 among its proof-texts), and Samaritan tradition developed the figure of the *Taheb*, a restorer modeled on Moses. The early Christian identification of this prophet with Yeshua (3:22; cf. 7:37; John 6:14) is one reading among the period's several expectations.

These are documented receptions of Deut 18, not lexical claims about Acts; the TT renders the citation as Moses's reported speech (unmarked, §A6) and leaves the identification to the sermon's own argument.

**Source:** Vermes, G., *The Complete Dead Sea Scrolls in English*, rev. ed., Penguin, 2011 (4Q175, 1QS 9:11); Anderson, R.T. and Giles, T., *The Samaritan Pentateuch*, Society of Biblical Literature, 2012.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. Was the lame man's daily station inside or outside the temple?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the data); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the inference)]**

The man is set "at the gate" to ask alms "from those entering into the temple" (3:2), then after the healing "entered with them into the temple" (3:8) — implying his begging-station was at a threshold, not in the inner courts. Whether the gate was an outer gate (where Gentiles too passed) or the inner Nicanor Gate bears on whether a man "lame from birth" — possibly counted among those barred from the inner precincts — would have sat there (cf. the disability-exclusion concerns of Lev 21:18; 2 Sam 5:8; and the Qumran rule 1QSa 2:5-9). The text states the threshold position but not the exact gate (§C2), and the TT does not resolve it.

### G2. "Rise and walk" — a disputed reading at verse 6
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The words **ἔγειρε καί** ("rise and") before "walk" in 3:6 are bracketed in NA28 — the manuscript witnesses are divided, with major early witnesses omitting them. The TT marks "*rise and*" in italics as the disputed words and keeps "walk," which is textually secure. The sense is unaffected either way (the man does rise, v.7), but the bracketing is a real text-critical datum, and the TT shows rather than hides it.

**Source:** Metzger, B.M., *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*, 2nd ed., German Bible Society, 1994, pp. 269-270.

### G3. "Having raised up his servant" (v.26) — resurrection or historical raising-up?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the data); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the sense)]**

The participle **ἀναστήσας** (*anastēsas*, 3:26), "having raised up," can mean either "raised up [onto the stage of history]" (as a prophet is "raised up," matching 3:22 of the prophet-like-Moses) or "raised [from the dead]" (the resurrection sense of *anistēmi* used elsewhere in the sermon). The immediate logic — "sent him" to bless "you first" — fits the historical-mission sense, while the chapter's resurrection theme (3:15) keeps the other sense in play. The TT renders "having raised up" precisely because the Greek does not disambiguate; the verse-note flags both possibilities.

### SCENARIO A — Pre-70 CE (Temple still standing)

#### IA-1. Religion — The Herodian temple precincts: courts, gates, and porticoes
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Acts 3 is set inside Herod the Great's vastly enlarged temple complex (rebuilt from c. 20/19 BCE, with work continuing into the 60s CE). The platform was ringed by colonnaded porticoes — the Royal Stoa on the south, Solomon's portico (3:11) on the east — surrounding the outer Court of the Gentiles; inner gates led through to the Court of the Women, then (via the bronze Nicanor Gate) to the Court of Israel and the priests' court before the sanctuary. The graded courts, the warning inscriptions barring Gentiles from the inner precincts (two of which survive), and the named gates are described by Josephus (*Jewish War* 5.5; *Antiquities* 15.11) and the Mishnah (*Middot*). For a pre-70 audience the gate-and-portico geography of the chapter (§C2, §C3) was simply the lived layout of the most important building in the Jewish world.

**Source:** Josephus, *Jewish War* 5.184-227 and *Jewish Antiquities* 15.380-425, Loeb Classical Library; Sanders, E.P., *Judaism: Practice and Belief*, SCM Press, 1992; *Mishnah, Middot* (tractate).

#### IA-2. Daily Life — Temple piety, the hours of prayer, and almsgiving
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The scene assumes the rhythms of temple piety: fixed hours of prayer tied to the morning and evening *tamid* offerings (3:1, §C1), with crowds present at the times of sacrifice and incense. Almsgiving (*eleēmosynē*, 3:2-3) was a central act of righteousness in Second Temple Judaism — the temple gates and approaches were natural places to solicit it, since pilgrims and worshippers arrived in a devotional and charitable frame of mind (cf. Tobit 4:7-11; Sirach 3:30; 7:10 on alms). For a pre-70 audience, a man "set daily" at a temple gate to beg, and worshippers "entering" at the hour of prayer, depicted an ordinary scene at the sanctuary, against which the healing erupts as extraordinary.

**Source:** Sanders, E.P., *Judaism: Practice and Belief*, SCM Press, 1992; Sirach and Tobit, in the LXX / Apocrypha; Schürer, E., *History of the Jewish People*, vol. 2, T&T Clark, 1979.

#### IA-3. Social Structure — Disability, begging, and the temple in Second-Temple society
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

A man "lame from his mother's womb" (3:2) belonged to a class of the disabled who, without modern support systems, depended on alms and on being "carried" by others to a place where they could beg. Some Second Temple texts express purity-based exclusion of the physically impaired from the inner sanctuary or from full participation (Lev 21:17-23 of priests; 2 Sam 5:8; the Qumran *Rule of the Congregation*, 1QSa 2:5-9, bars the lame and blind from the eschatological assembly), though the degree to which the lame were barred from the outer courts in practice is debated. The narrative's emphasis that the healed man "entered… into the temple" (3:8) reads, against this backdrop, as more than mobility restored — a previously marginal figure now moves into the worshipping community.

**Source:** Olyan, S.M., *Disability in the Hebrew Bible*, Cambridge University Press, 2008; Vermes, G., *The Complete Dead Sea Scrolls in English*, rev. ed., Penguin, 2011 (1QSa); Ilan, T., *Jewish Women in Greco-Roman Palestine*, Hendrickson, 1995 (on dependence and charity).

#### IA-4. Religion — Messianic and restoration hope under Roman rule
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The sermon's language of a coming "Messiah" (3:20), "times of refreshing" (3:20), and "restoration of all things" (3:21) sits within the live restoration-hope of first-century Judea under Roman control. A range of groups — reflected in the Psalms of Solomon, the Qumran scrolls, and later apocalyptic — looked for God to restore Israel, raise an anointed deliverer, and renew the age; the disciples' own question, "will you at this time restore the kingdom to Israel?" (1:6), voices the same hope. For a pre-70 audience the sermon's eschatological vocabulary would register against this background of expectation, while the TT keeps the scope of "restoration" (§A5, §D3) deliberately open.

**Source:** Psalms of Solomon, in Charlesworth, J.H. (ed.), *The Old Testament Pseudepigrapha*, vol. 2, Doubleday, 1985; Collins, J.J., *The Scepter and the Star*, 2nd ed., Eerdmans, 2010.

### SCENARIO B — Post-70 CE (Temple destroyed)

#### IB-1. Religion — Reading the temple scene after the temple's fall
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

For a reader after 70 CE, the chapter's detailed temple geography — the Beautiful Gate, the hour of the evening sacrifice, Solomon's portico, the crowds at prayer — depicted an institution that no longer existed. The sacrificial system, the priestly establishment named in the previous chapters, and the gates and porticoes had been destroyed in the Roman war; Jewish religious life was reorganizing around synagogue, prayer, study, and acts of loving-kindness. The chapter's frame would then read as a portrait of a vanished sanctuary in which the early proclamation had begun — lending the "times of refreshing" and "restoration of all things" (3:20-21) the weight of a community that had lived through ruin and still awaited renewal.

**Source:** Cohen, S.J.D., *From the Maccabees to the Mishnah*, 3rd ed., Westminster John Knox, 2014; Goodman, M., *Rome and Jerusalem*, Allen Lane, 2007.

#### IB-2. Social Structure — Almsgiving and care for the disabled without the temple
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

After 70 CE, with the temple gone, the locus of almsgiving and charity shifted decisively toward the synagogue and the local community, and the emerging rabbinic movement made *tzedakah* (charitable giving) a central pillar of religious life. In this setting, the image of a disabled man dependent on alms at a temple gate (3:2) would read as belonging to a lost world, even as the underlying obligation — care for the poor and disabled — intensified in communal practice. The chapter's restoration of a marginal figure to the worshipping community (3:8) could speak afresh to a post-temple audience reconstituting its religious and social bonds around new institutions.

**Source:** Cohen, S.J.D., *From the Maccabees to the Mishnah*, 3rd ed., Westminster John Knox, 2014; Olyan, S.M., *Disability in the Hebrew Bible*, Cambridge University Press, 2008.
