## Note on Acts 2's mode of allusion

Acts 2 is the most **citation-dense** chapter of the book's opening — the heart of Kefa's (Peter's) first sermon is built on **three explicit, formula-introduced quotations** from the Hebrew Bible: **Joel 2:28–32** (the outpoured Spirit, introduced "this is what has been spoken through the prophet Yoel," 2:16–21), **Psalm 16:8–11** (the holy one not abandoned to Sheol/Hades, "David says concerning him," 2:25–28), and **Psalm 110:1** (the enthronement, "he himself says," 2:34–35). Around them sit two **allusions**: the Davidic oath of **2 Samuel 7 / Psalm 132:11** behind v.30, and the "those far off" of **Isaiah 57:19** behind v.39. The chapter follows the **Septuagint** wording Peter cites (the Greek text is the one the apostle quotes), and three features carry the TT's restraint: (1) Acts's **redactional modifications** of Joel ("the last days"; the doubled "my"; the appended "and they will prophesy"); (2) **κύριος → YHWH** in the citations under Option C, with the same κύριος then confessed of Yeshua at v.36 — the referent tracked, not collapsed (decision A-005); and (3) the **double-κύριος of Psalm 110:1** at v.34, two distinct referents under one Greek word (Rule 13). The first-person divine speech inside the citations (Joel's "I will pour out…"; the LORD's "Sit at my right hand…") is **divine-marked** `@@…@@` (Rule 30 / A-004); Peter's own preaching, and David's human voice in the Psalms ("I saw the Lord"), are **human** speech and are not.

## Joel 2:28–32 (LXX 3:1–5) — the outpoured Spirit and "the last days" (Acts 2:17–21)

**Verse:** Acts 2:17–21
**Text says:** "but this is what has been spoken through the prophet Yoel (Joel): @@'And it will be in the last days, says God, *that* I will pour out from my {a:wind/spirit} upon all flesh, and your sons and your daughters will prophesy… ²⁰The sun will be turned into darkness and the moon into blood, before the day of *the* Lord comes — the great and manifest *day*. ²¹And it will be *that* everyone who calls upon the name of *the* Lord will be saved.'@@"
**Context:** Kefa (Peter) answers the mockery of drunkenness (2:13–15) by reading the Pentecost Spirit-event as the prophesied last-days outpouring. The citation is introduced by an explicit fulfillment formula ("this is what has been spoken through the prophet Yoel") and runs as God's own first-person speech — the chapter's first divine-marked block (`@@…@@`, Rule 30 / A-004).
**Subject:** The democratized Spirit and the universal call. Joel 2:28–32 (MT; LXX 3:1–5) promises that YHWH will pour out his spirit "on all flesh" — sons and daughters, young and old, male and female servants — with cosmic signs before "the day of YHWH," so that "everyone who calls on the name of YHWH" will be delivered. Per Option C, the κύριος of vv.20–21 renders YHWH ("the day of YHWH"; "the name of YHWH"); the sermon will then redefine "the name" christologically (immersion "upon the name of Yeshua," v.38) under the same κύριος applied to Jesus at v.36 — the TT keeps "the Lord" and tracks the referent rather than collapsing it.
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED
**Fulfillment notes:** This **is an explicit citation** — formula-introduced ("this is what has been spoken… Joel") and read by Peter as fulfilled *now* ("this is" that). Three redactional features carry the TT's restraint (Rule 13): (1) Acts reads **"in the last days"** (*en tais eschatais hēmerais*) where Joel/LXX has "after these things" (*meta tauta*) — interpreting the Spirit's coming as the inauguration of the last days; (2) Acts doubles the possessive — **"my** menservants… **my** maidservants" (*doulous mou… doulas mou*) — sharpening that these are God's servants; (3) Acts **appends "and they will prophesy"** (*kai prophēteusousin*, v.18), absent from Joel, making the prophetic gift the explicit point. The cosmic signs (vv.19–20) are cited in full but read as the frame of the dawning Day rather than as discrete predictions ticked off. The κύριος of vv.20–21 is YHWH (Option C); its application to Yeshua is the sermon's developing claim, not a narrator's equation. **[textual — VERIFIED (citation); the Acts modifications — VERIFIED (textual); referent-fusion — POSSIBLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Joel 2:28–32 → Acts 2:17–21) in `content/en/acts/CHAPTER-2.md`.
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Joel 2:28–32 (Heb. 3:1–5) is read as a promise of a future outpouring of YHWH's spirit on all Israel, accompanied by cosmic portents before the Day of YHWH and deliverance for "all who call on the name of YHWH" (with the remnant "in Mount Zion"). It is not read as fulfilled at a first-century Pentecost; the "last days" framing and the appended "they will prophesy" are Acts's own. The promise remains a future hope. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Acts reads Pentecost as the inaugural fulfillment of Joel — the Spirit poured out on all flesh, the last days begun, the gospel call ("everyone who calls on the name of the Lord") opened to all who will respond, with "the Lord" read toward Yeshua as well as the Father. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: The Quran does not engage Joel or a Pentecost outpouring; prophecy and revelation are mediated through the prophets and ultimately sealed in Muhammad, and the Holy Spirit (*al-rūḥ al-qudus*) is associated with Jibril (Gabriel) rather than a poured-out gift on a community. [DOCUMENTED]

## Psalm 16:8–11 (LXX 15) — the holy one not abandoned to Sheol (Acts 2:25–28)

**Verse:** Acts 2:25–28
**Text says:** "For David says concerning him: 'I saw the Lord before me always, because he is at my right *hand*, that I may not be shaken… ²⁷because you will not abandon my soul to {t:Hades}, nor will you give your holy *one* to see corruption. ²⁸You made known to me ways of life; you will fill me with gladness with your face.'"
**Context:** Having proclaimed Yeshua's death by God's plan and human hands and his resurrection (2:23–24), Peter grounds the resurrection in Scripture. The citation is **David's** human voice (not divine speech — not marked `@@…@@`); Peter then argues (vv.29–31) that since David died, was buried, and his tomb remains, the psalm points beyond David to the Messiah.
**Subject:** The trust of the holy one whom God will not abandon to death. Psalm 16:8–11 (LXX 15) is David's confidence that YHWH at his right hand keeps him secure, that his soul will not be left to Sheol nor his "holy one" (Heb. *ḥasid*) see "the pit/corruption," and that God makes known "the path of life." Per Option C, the κύριος of v.25 renders YHWH ("I saw YHWH before me"). The TT follows the **LXX** wording Peter cites at two points the notes flag: "my tongue rejoiced" (v.26, LXX *glōssa* for Hebrew *kevodi*, "my glory") and "to see corruption" (v.27, LXX *diaphthora* for Hebrew *shaḥat*, "pit" — the rendering on which Peter's "did not decay" argument depends).
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED
**Fulfillment notes:** This **is an explicit citation** ("David says concerning him") — and Peter builds an argument *from the wording*: David died and decayed (his tomb "is among us until this day," v.29), so "you will not… see corruption" was not fulfilled in David and must point to one not held by death — the risen Messiah (v.31). Two text-traditional features the TT preserves without resolving (Rule 13): (1) the argument **leans on the LXX** — Hebrew *shaḥat* ("pit") versus LXX *diaphthora* ("corruption/decay"); Peter's reasoning works on the Greek "decay," and the TT keeps the LXX wording Acts quotes while noting the Hebrew; (2) "my tongue rejoiced" (LXX) for Hebrew "my glory" (v.26) — a known LXX/MT divergence the TT follows in the Greek Peter cites. The CLAIMED label reflects that Peter explicitly applies the psalm to the Messiah's resurrection; the application is the sermon's argument, and the Hebrew/LXX question is live. **[textual — VERIFIED (citation); the LXX-dependent "corruption" argument — PROBABLE; messianic application — the sermon's claim]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ps 16:8–11 → Acts 2:25–28).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Psalm 16 is read as David's own expression of trust in YHWH — confidence of security in life and (in most readings) of not being abandoned by God, with *shaḥat* as "the pit/grave" rather than "decay." It is not read as a prediction of a resurrected Messiah; Peter's argument depends on the LXX "corruption," not the Hebrew "pit." [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Acts reads Psalm 16 as David prophesying, "concerning him," the Messiah's resurrection — the holy one whose flesh did not decay because death could not hold him. The LXX "corruption" is treated as the providentially apt rendering. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Dawud (David) is honored as a prophet-king given the Zabur (Psalms), but the Quran does not engage Psalm 16 or a resurrection argument from it, and it does not affirm the crucifixion-and-resurrection of Isa as Christian reading presents it (cf. Surah 4:157–158). [DOCUMENTED]

## Psalm 110:1 (LXX 109) — "The Lord said to my lord": the double-κύριος enthronement (Acts 2:34–35)

**Verse:** Acts 2:34–35
**Text says:** "For David did not ascend into the heavens, but he himself says: 'The Lord said to my lord, @@\"Sit at my right *hand*, ³⁵until I set your enemies *as* a footstool for your feet.\"@@'"
**Context:** The climax of the Davidic argument. David did not himself ascend (v.34a), so when he says "The Lord said to my lord… Sit at my right hand," he speaks of another — the exalted Messiah (cf. v.33, "exalted to the right hand of God"). The conclusion follows immediately: "God made him both **Lord and Messiah**" (v.36). The LORD's quoted command ("Sit at my right hand…") is God's first-person speech → divine-marked `@@…@@` (Rule 30); David's framing words ("he himself says: The Lord said to my lord") are human speech and are not.
**Subject:** The enthronement of David's "lord" at God's right hand. Psalm 110:1 (LXX 109) is YHWH's oracle to the king: "YHWH said to my lord (*ladoni*): sit at my right hand until I make your enemies your footstool." In the Hebrew the **first** "LORD" is the Tetragrammaton (YHWH, the speaker) and the **second** ("my lord," *adoni*) is the Davidic king the psalmist addresses — two distinct referents. The LXX renders both with **κύριος** (*eipen ho kyrios tō kyriō mou*), so the Greek Peter cites carries both senses under one word. Per Option C and **decision A-005**, the TT renders **"*The* Lord said to my lord"**: the first κύριος = **YHWH** (the speaker; DE→JHWH, PT/ES→YHWH in propagation), the second τῷ κυρίῳ μου = **the Messiah** (David's "lord," the addressee). Psalm 110:1 is the **most-cited HB verse in the Greek Scriptures**.
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED
**Fulfillment notes:** This **is an explicit citation** ("he himself says") and the **headline crux** of the chapter (decision A-005). The whole sermon turns on the **double-κύριος**: two distinct referents — YHWH (speaker) and the Messiah (addressee) — under one Greek word. The TT preserves both rather than flattening them, lower-casing the second ("my lord") to mark the human-addressee referent while keeping "the Lord" for YHWH, and flags the distinction for Hellenist review (Rule 13). The argument structure is: David did not ascend (so the psalm is not about David) → David, as prophet, foresaw the Messiah's enthronement → therefore the exalted one is David's "lord," confirmed by the resurrection (v.32) and the outpoured Spirit (v.33). The CLAIMED label reflects that Peter explicitly applies Psalm 110:1 to Yeshua's exaltation; the messianic application is the sermon's claim, and the same κύριος is then confessed of the crucified Yeshua at v.36 ("both Lord and Messiah") — the juxtaposition, not a narrator's equation, doing the work. **[the two referents — VERIFIED (Hebrew/LXX); messianic application — the sermon's claim; PROBABLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ps 110:1 → Acts 2:34–35) and the verse-34 note in `content/en/acts/CHAPTER-2.md`; logged at `docs/editorial-log/acts.md` A-005.
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Psalm 110 is read in its royal-court setting — the psalmist (or a court poet) addressing the Davidic king: YHWH (the first "LORD") invites "my lord" (*adoni*, the king) to sit at his right hand and promises victory over his enemies. The second "lord" is the human king, not a divine figure, and the psalm is not read as a prediction of a heavenly enthronement of the Messiah. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Acts (with the Gospels, cf. Mk 12:36) reads Psalm 110:1 as David, by the Spirit, calling the Messiah "my lord" — proof that the Messiah is greater than David and is exalted to God's right hand. The double-κύριος is read as Scripture's own pointer to the exalted Christ confessed as "Lord." [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: The Quran does not engage Psalm 110 or a session "at the right hand" of God; such enthronement language, and the confession of Isa as "Lord," runs against the Quran's insistence on God's absolute oneness and the prophet-status (not divine status) of Isa (Surah 4:171; 5:72–75). [DOCUMENTED]

## 2 Samuel 7:12–13 + Psalm 132:11 — the oath to seat David's son on the throne (Acts 2:30)

**Verse:** Acts 2:30
**Text says:** "Therefore, being a prophet, and knowing that God swore to him with an oath *that* of *the* fruit of his loins *he would seat one* upon his throne,"
**Context:** The hinge between the two Davidic citations. Peter establishes that David — "a prophet" — knew God's sworn promise to seat his descendant on his throne, which is why David's psalms (16 and 110) can be read as foreseeing the Messiah's resurrection and enthronement. It bridges Psalm 16 (vv.25–28) to Psalm 110 (vv.34–35).
**Subject:** The sworn Davidic dynastic promise. "God swore to him with an oath… of the fruit of his loins" draws together the Davidic covenant of **2 Samuel 7:12–13** ("I will raise up your seed after you… and I will establish his throne forever") and its psalmic restatement as a sworn oath in **Psalm 132:11** ("YHWH swore to David a sure oath… of the fruit of your body I will set upon your throne"). The wording "the fruit of his loins" (*ek karpou tēs osphyos autou*) is close to Psalm 132:11 LXX (*ek karpou tēs koilias sou*), framed as the **oath** (*horkō ōmosen*) of the psalm rather than a quotation of 2 Samuel's prose.
**Fulfillment status:** PARTIAL
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion to the Davidic-covenant oath**, not a formula citation — Peter does not quote a verse but invokes the sworn promise ("God swore to him with an oath… the fruit of his loins… upon his throne") in HB/LXX diction, fusing 2 Sam 7:12–13 with Ps 132:11. The Davidic-king-with-an-established-throne expectation is **broader than any one claimant**: 2 Samuel 7 in its setting concerns David's dynastic line (immediately Shelomoh/Solomon and the temple), and the oath is corporate-dynastic. The PARTIAL label reflects that the underlying promise is dynastic, while Peter's *application* to the resurrected-and-enthroned Yeshua is CLAIMED — the oath functions in the argument to license reading David's psalms prophetically (David "knew," so he "looked ahead," v.31), not as a standalone prediction-and-fulfillment. **[comparative parallel — PROBABLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (2 Sam 7 / Ps 132:11 → Acts 2:30).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: 2 Samuel 7 and Psalm 132:11 are read as the foundational dynastic promise to the house of David — a sure, sworn covenant that David's line will hold the throne (fulfilled proximately in Solomon and the temple, and nourishing the hope of a future Davidic king). The promise is not read as predicting a resurrection or a heavenly enthronement of the Messiah. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Acts reads the Davidic oath as the ground for David's prophetic foresight: knowing God's sworn promise, David spoke (in Pss 16 and 110) of the Messiah's resurrection and enthronement, fulfilled in Yeshua, the son of David raised and exalted. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Dawud (David) and Sulayman (Solomon) are honored as prophet-kings, but the Quran does not frame an everlasting Davidic-throne covenant culminating in Isa, nor connect such a promise to resurrection or enthronement. [DOCUMENTED]

## Isaiah 57:19 (cf. Joel 2:32) — "to all those at a distance" (Acts 2:39)

**Verse:** Acts 2:39
**Text says:** "For to you is the promise, and to your children, and to all those at a distance, as many as *the* Lord our God may call to himself."
**Context:** Part of Peter's call to response (vv.38–40), after "change your minds and be immersed." Having quoted Joel's "everyone who calls on the name of the Lord" (v.21), Peter now extends the promise — "to you… your children… all those at a distance" — quietly widening the horizon beyond those present.
**Subject:** The promise extended to the near and the far. "To all those at a distance" (*pasin tois eis makran*) echoes **Isaiah 57:19** ("Peace, peace, to the far and to the near, says YHWH"), and the closing clause "as many as the Lord our God may call" echoes **Joel 2:32** ("and among the survivors whom YHWH calls"). Per Option C, "the Lord our God" (*kyrios ho theos hēmōn*) renders YHWH. Within Acts's program (1:8, "to the end of the earth"), "those far off" carries a trajectory toward the nations, though here the immediate referents are the diaspora Jews and their descendants.
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion by phrase**, not a formula citation — Peter weaves Isaiah's "far / near" and Joel's "whom YHWH calls" into his own exhortation without an "as it is written." The connection is deliberate (the Joel echo binds back to v.21's quotation; "those far off" is recognizable Isaianic diction) but the mode is **applied recollection**, not prediction-and-fulfillment. The CLAIMED label reflects that Peter intentionally invokes the near/far promise; whether "those far off" already signals the gentile mission or stays within diaspora Israel is left open by the text and not resolved here (the wider reach is developed only later in Acts). **[comparative parallel — POSSIBLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Isa 57:19 / Joel 2:32 → Acts 2:39).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Isaiah 57:19 is read as God's promise of peace to the far and the near — commonly, the exiled/scattered and those at home, or the penitent — within Israel; Joel 2:32 of the survivors/remnant whom YHWH calls. Neither is read as opening the covenant to the nations through Yeshua. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Acts reads "those far off" as anticipating the gospel's reach beyond Jerusalem — to the diaspora and ultimately the nations (Eph 2:13, 17 makes the gentile reading explicit) — all "whom the Lord our God calls." [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: The Quran's universalism is framed differently — a final message to all peoples through Muhammad — and it does not engage Isaiah 57:19 or Joel 2:32 or a near/far covenant extension. [DOCUMENTED]
