## 1. The holy wind/spirit — promised, poured out, the power behind the signs

**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 1, 2, 3

The *pneuma hagion* ("holy {a:wind/spirit}") is the engine of the opening three chapters, moving through three stages across them. In chapter 1 it is **promised**: the risen Yeshua orders the apostles to await "the promise of the Father" — "Yochanan immersed in water, but you will be immersed in *the* holy {a:wind/spirit}" (1:5) — and he ties the coming power directly to it: "you will receive power *when* the holy {a:wind/spirit} has come upon you" (1:8). In chapter 2 it is **poured out**: at Pentecost the sound "like a rushing violent wind" fills the house, the {a:wind/spirit} fills them all, and they speak "just as the {a:wind/spirit} was giving them to utter" (2:1-4); Kefa reads the event as Yoel's "I will pour out from my {a:wind/spirit} upon all flesh" (2:17-18), and the exalted Yeshua "poured out this which you see and hear" (2:33). In chapter 3 it is **the power behind the signs**: though *pneuma* is not named at the Beautiful Gate, the healing "in the name" and the apostolic "wonders and signs" (2:43) flow from the same outpouring, and Kefa deflects all credit from the apostles' "own power" (3:12). The TT keeps the wind/spirit slash throughout (the locked GS glossary, Rule 2), declining the imported title "the Holy Spirit," so the term's ambiguity and its recurrence stay visible — and so that the word-field linking the wind-*sound* (*pnoē*, 2:2) to the {a:wind/spirit} (*pneuma*, 2:4) is not flattened.

**Source:** Barrett, C.K., *A Critical and Exegetical Commentary on the Acts of the Apostles, Volume I*, ICC, T&T Clark, 1994, on the Lukan pneumatology of Acts 1–2.

## 2. "The name" — calling, immersion, healing

**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 2, 3

"The name" (*onoma*) recurs as a load-bearing keyword, and its referent migrates across the chapters in a way the TT keeps legible by rendering it literally ("*the* name") rather than paraphrasing. In Yoel's oracle the formula is "everyone who **calls upon the name** of *the* Lord will be saved" (2:21), where *kyrios* carries YHWH (Option C). By the end of the same sermon the call has been re-aimed: Kefa tells the crowd to **be immersed upon the name of Yeshua** *the* Messiah "for *the* release of your sins" (2:38) — the same word "name," now christological. In chapter 3 the name **heals**: Kefa gives the lame man what he has, "in the name of Yeshua *the* Messiah the Nazarene — walk" (3:6), and the sermon's interpretation makes the link explicit and emphatic — "upon the faith of his name… his name made *him* firm… the faith *that is* through him gave him this wholeness" (3:16). The TT preserves the crowded, doubled syntax of 3:16 rather than smoothing it, because the redundancy *is* the point: the name is the channel of both salvation and healing. The motif lets the reader track, in the text's own vocabulary, how "the name of the Lord" of the Joel citation becomes "the name of Yeshua" without the narrator equating them by fiat.

**Source:** Bock, D.L., *Acts* (Baker Exegetical Commentary), Baker, 2007, on the "name" theme across the early speeches.

## 3. Witness to the resurrection — the apostolic criterion

**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 1, 2, 3

The word *martys* ("witness") and its claim — firsthand testimony to the resurrection — anchor all three chapters and supply the logic of the book's mission. In chapter 1 it is both **commission** and **criterion**: the risen Yeshua says "you will be my **witnesses**… unto *the* end of the earth" (1:8), and the qualification for replacing Yehudah is that the candidate, having accompanied the others "beginning from the immersion of Yochanan until the day *on* which he was taken up," "become a **witness** of his resurrection together with us" (1:22). In chapter 2 the criterion is **discharged** within the sermon: "This Yeshua God raised up, of which we all are **witnesses**" (2:32). In chapter 3 it is **discharged again**: "the Author of life you killed, whom God raised from *the* dead — of which we are **witnesses**" (3:15). The TT renders *martys* as "witness" (legal/testimonial), noting that the later sense "martyr" is not yet active here. The motif binds the geographic program of 1:8 to its epistemic ground: the spread "to the end of the earth" rests on eyewitness testimony to a specific event, repeated as the hinge of each sermon.

**Source:** Marshall, I.H., *The Acts of the Apostles* (Tyndale New Testament Commentaries), Eerdmans, 1980, on witness and the apostolic kerygma.

## 4. The kerygma — the shared sermon pattern

**[STRONG INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**
**Chapters:** 2, 3

The two apostolic sermons (chapters 2 and 3) follow a recurring proclamation (*kērygma*) shape, recognizable as the same skeleton filled out differently — a pattern long noted in scholarship and visible here in the TT's own rendering. The recurring movement runs: **Scripture** (Yoel and the Psalms in ch. 2; "all the prophets," Mosheh, the Abrahamic word in ch. 3) → **the cross**, held as *both* God's plan *and* human hands (2:23, "given up by the determined plan and foreknowledge of God, you… killed"; 3:13-15, "you handed over and denied… the Author of life you killed") → **the resurrection** ("whom God raised up," 2:24, 32; 3:15) → **the exaltation** ("exalted to the right *hand* of God," 2:33; "whom heaven must receive," 3:21) → **the call to change of mind** ("Change *your* minds, and let each of you be immersed," 2:38; "Change *your* minds, therefore, and turn," 3:19). The two sermons differ in occasion (the Pentecost crowd vs. the crowd at Solomon's portico) and in their lead text (Yoel/Psalms vs. the Servant/Mosheh/Avraham), but the architecture is the same. The motif is presented as a pattern in the text, not as a claim about its compositional history; the TT keeps the locked terms (*metanoia* = "change of mind," *aphesis* = "release/forgiveness," *Christos* = "anointed/Messiah") stable across both sermons so the parallel structure is audible.

**Source:** Dodd, C.H., *The Apostolic Preaching and Its Developments*, Hodder & Stoughton, 1936, on the *kerygma* outline of the Acts speeches (with the caution that the schema is a modern reconstruction).

## 5. Scripture-fulfillment — the Psalms, Yoel, Mosheh, Avraham, "all the prophets"

**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 1, 2, 3

The events are read throughout as the fulfillment of named and unnamed Scripture, signaled by a recurring vocabulary of necessity and prophecy. In chapter 1 the Psalms are read as foretelling Yehudah's end and the vacancy of his office: "it was necessary (*edei*) *for* the scripture to be fulfilled which the holy {a:wind/spirit} foretold through *the* mouth of David" (1:16, citing Ps 69:25 and Ps 109:8). In chapter 2 three texts carry the sermon — Yoel 2 ("this is what has been spoken through the prophet Yoel," 2:16), Psalm 16 (David, "being a prophet," 2:30-31), and Psalm 110 ("David did not ascend… but he himself says," 2:34) — with David twice cast as a prophet whose own death proves the psalms point beyond him. In chapter 3 the net widens to the whole prophetic corpus: God "foretold through *the* mouth of **all** the prophets — that his Messiah would suffer" (3:18), Mosheh's prophet-like-me (Deut 18, 3:22-23), "**all** the prophets, from Shmuel… also announced these days" (3:24), and the Abrahamic covenant word (Gen 22:18, 3:25). The TT renders the citations at face value, preserving where Acts diverges from the Hebrew/LXX wording (e.g. Yoel's "in the last days," 2:17; the LXX "tongue" for "glory" in Ps 16, 2:26) rather than harmonizing, so the interpretive moves of the text remain inspectable.

**Source:** Hays, R.B., *Echoes of Scripture in the Gospels*, Baylor University Press, 2016, on early Christian christological reading of the Psalms and prophets (applied to the Acts speeches).

## 6. Israel and the nations — Yerushalayim first, the end of the earth

**[STRONG INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**
**Chapters:** 1, 2, 3

A single widening horizon — from Israel outward to all peoples — runs through the three chapters and functions as the literary program of the whole book. It is **stated** in chapter 1 as a geography: "you will be my witnesses both in **Yerushalayim** and in all **Yehudah** and **Shomron** and unto *the* **end of the earth**" (1:8) — the outline Acts then follows. It is **dramatized** in chapter 2: the audience is Israel-in-dispersion, "Jews (Yehudim), devout men from **every nation** of those under the sky" (2:5), itemized in the table-of-nations list "Parthians and Medes and Elamites…" (2:9-11) that runs east to west and evokes Genesis 10; and the promise reaches "to your children, and to **all those at a distance**" (2:39). It is **grounded** in chapter 3 in the Abrahamic covenant: "in your seed **all the families of the earth** will be blessed" (3:25), with Israel addressed "**To you first**" (3:26) — "first" implying a "then." The TT keeps the distinctions in the vocabulary (*Yehudim*, *ethnos*/"nation," *laos*/"people," the geographic names in familiar form) and renders "first" without softening, so the universal reach emerges from the text's own terms while Israel's priority of address stays explicit.

**Source:** Jervell, J., *The Theology of the Acts of the Apostles*, Cambridge University Press, 1996, on Israel and the nations in the Lukan program.

## 7. Continuity with Israel's worship — temple, portico, festival

**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 2, 3

The new community is shown remaining *within* Israel's worship rather than departing from it — a continuity carried by concrete places and times across the chapters. The decisive outpouring falls on **Shavuot** — "the day of Pentecost (Shavuot)" (2:1), the pilgrim harvest-festival (Lev 23:15-21). The community's life is set "with one accord **day by day in the temple**" alongside meeting "from house to house" (2:46). In chapter 3 Kefa and Yochanan "were going up to the **temple at the hour of prayer, the ninth**" (3:1, ≈ 3 p.m., the hour of the evening sacrifice), and the crowd gathers at "the portico called **Shlomoh's (Solomon's)**" (3:11), a colonnade of the temple precinct. The TT renders the festival ("Pentecost," glossed Shavuot), the prayer-hours, and the temple topography at face value, neither modernizing nor abstracting them into "church," so the texture of a movement still inside Second-Temple Judaism is preserved on the page. The motif sets the boundary the rest of Acts will test: the gospel goes out from a community that is, at the opening, fully observant of Israel's sacred times and spaces.

**Source:** Barrett, C.K., *A Critical and Exegetical Commentary on the Acts of the Apostles, Volume I*, ICC, T&T Clark, 1994, on the temple and Jewish piety in early Acts.

## 8. Restoration — the kingdom, the Twelve, all things

**[STRONG INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**
**Chapters:** 1, 3

A restoration motif, carried by the *apokathistēmi*/*apokatastasis* word-family, frames the opening and the second sermon, holding together a hope the text neither rebukes nor resolves. In chapter 1 the disciples ask, "Lord, *is it* at this time *that* you are **restoring** (*apokathistaneis*) the kingdom to Israel?" (1:6) — a question answered as *mistimed*, not mistaken ("It is not yours to know times or seasons," 1:7). The same chapter enacts a restoration of its own: the reconstitution of **the Twelve**, replacing Yehudah by lot so that the tribal number of Israel is made whole again (1:15-26). In chapter 3 the word returns at cosmic scale: the heavens must receive the Messiah "until *the* times of **restoration of all *things*** (*apokatastaseōs pantōn*), of which God spoke through *the* mouth of his holy prophets from of old" (3:21). The TT keeps *apokatastasis* literally ("restoration of all things") without resolving its disputed scope (Israel's renewal / cosmic renewal / universal), and keeps the disciples' Israel-centered hope of 1:6 intact rather than spiritualizing it — letting the tension between "not for you to know the time" and "the restoration of all things" stand as the text leaves it.

**Source:** Bock, D.L., *Acts* (Baker Exegetical Commentary), Baker, 2007, on *apokatastasis* and the restoration hope in Acts 1 and 3.
