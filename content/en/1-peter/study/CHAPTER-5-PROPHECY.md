## Note on 1 Peter's mode of allusion (an epistle, not a narrative)

1 Peter is a **letter** — *argument and exhortation* in the apostle's own voice, not a narrative of events fulfilling prediction. So its use of the Hebrew Bible is not "prophecy → fulfillment" in the gospel-and-Acts sense. The closing chapter **deploys** the Hebrew Bible *paraenetically and typologically*: it **quotes** it to ground a command ("God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble," 5:5, from Proverbs), it **echoes** the shepherd-prophecy of Ezekiel and the shepherd-psalm onto the elders and the chief Shepherd (5:2–4), and it **borrows** Exodus and Psalm diction (the "mighty hand," the cast-off burden, the roaring lion) for its exhortation to humility and vigilance (5:6–8). The dual-label and citation-vs-allusion system carries over unchanged from the gospel companions; what shifts is how to read the **status line**. Here "fulfillment status" should be read as **mode of deployment**:

- **CLAIMED** ≈ **explicit quotation** — the letter cites the OT verbatim and presses it as authoritative for the readers (Prov 3:34, 5:5).
- **PARTIAL** ≈ **typological application** — the letter reads an Israel institution or oracle (the shepherd-of-the-flock prophecy; the shepherd-king imagery) as the pattern realized in the Messiah and his under-shepherds, without quoting a verse (Ezek 34; Ps 23, 5:2–4).
- **DEBATED** ≈ **echo** — the diction or imagery of an OT scene is interiorized into the exhortation (the Exodus "mighty hand," the cast burden, the roaring lion), without citation or fulfillment-claim (5:6–8).

Because the **one formal citation in this chapter — Proverbs 3:34 (5:5) — is the *sage's* voice, not God's first-person speech**, the chapter carries **no `@@…@@` divine-marking** (Rule 30 / P-005 confirmation; cf. the CHAPTER-5 speech-marking note). 1 Peter's other uses here are echoes and typology, none of them quoted divine first-person word. Where κύριος in a source verse stands for YHWH, the entries note it per **Option C** (the main text renders "the Lord" + Tier-2; the source-referent is YHWH; κύριος Option C in `1-peter.md`).

## Ezekiel 34 — "shepherd the flock of God… not for gain… as examples" (1 Peter 5:2–3)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 5:2–3
**Text says:** "shepherd the flock of God among you, overseeing not by compulsion but willingly, according to God; nor for shameful gain but eagerly; nor as lording *it* over those allotted *to you*, but becoming examples of the flock."
**Context:** The pastoral charge to the elders (5:1–4): writing as a "fellow-elder" (*sympresbyteros*, a self-lowering), Peter commissions the elders to **shepherd** (*poimainō*) the "flock of God," with three this-not-that contrasts — willingly not by compulsion, eagerly not for gain, as examples not lording it over. The shepherd-of-the-flock language is a **typological echo** of Ezekiel's shepherd-oracle; no verse is cited.
**Subject:** The shepherds of God's flock, set against the self-serving shepherds of Ezekiel. **ποιμάνατε τὸ… ποίμνιον τοῦ θεοῦ ἐπισκοποῦντες** (*poimanate to… poimnion tou theou episkopountes*, "shepherd the flock of God, overseeing") deploys the shepherd-and-flock field of **Ezekiel 34**, where YHWH indicts the "shepherds of Israel" who "feed themselves and not the flock," who rule "with force and harshness" and pursue gain (Ezek 34:2–4), and promises to set over them a true shepherd and himself to seek the lost (34:11–16, 23). 1 Peter's three contrasts map onto Ezekiel's charges almost point for point: not for **shameful gain** (*aischrokerdōs*; cf. the shepherds who fleece the flock), not **lording it over** (*katakyrieuontes*; cf. ruling "with force"), but as **examples** (*typoi*) — the under-shepherds patterned on the chief Shepherd (5:4; 2:25, "the shepherd and overseer of your souls"). The pastoral-verb (*poimainō*) + "oversee" (*episkopeō*) pairing is the same that the Johannine "shepherd" tradition draws (Jn 10; Jn 21:16, "shepherd my sheep"). 1 Peter quotes none of this; it **applies** the shepherd-of-the-flock prophecy to Christian eldership.
**Fulfillment status:** PARTIAL *(mode: typological application)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **typology / echo**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse and uses no formula; it **deploys** Ezekiel's shepherd-oracle (the LORD against false shepherds, promising a true one) as the pattern now realized in the elders under the chief Shepherd. The connection is thematic and lexically anchored: the shepherd-and-flock vocabulary (*poimainō / poimnion*), the indictment of **gain** and **domineering**, and the contrast with the true shepherd all track Ezekiel 34. Which prophetic shepherd-text is foremost — **Ezekiel 34** (the most extended), the broader shepherd-prophecies (Jer 23:1–4, the scattering shepherds; Zech 11), or the shepherd-psalm field (Ps 23, 80) — is not specified by the text and is not resolved here; the "lording it over… examples" contrast also draws on **Yeshua's own teaching against domineering leadership** (Mk 10:42–45), so the echo is dominical as much as prophetic (5:3 note; Rule 13). The PARTIAL label marks this as typological application (the prophecy's pattern read as realized in the church's eldership), not an explicit quotation and not a fulfilled prediction. **[comparative parallel — PROBABLE (typology, not citation); the foremost shepherd-text (Ezek 34 vs. the wider shepherd field) — POSSIBLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ezek 34 → 1 Pet 5:2–3) in `content/en/1-peter/CHAPTER-5.md`. For the Messiah as shepherd, cf. 1 Pet 2:25 and the gospel "good shepherd" tradition (Jn 10).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Ezekiel 34 is read as YHWH's judgment on the failed leaders ("shepherds") of Israel — kings and rulers who exploited the people — and his promise to gather the scattered flock himself and to "set up over them one shepherd, my servant David" (34:23), a hope of righteous Davidic leadership and national restoration. The shepherd-oracle belongs to Israel's covenant story; its application to the elders of a Christian church under a "chief Shepherd" is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Ezekiel 34 is read as foreshadowing the Messiah, the true shepherd (the "good shepherd" of Jn 10, the "great shepherd" of Heb 13:20); 1 Peter 5:2–4 is treated as the apostolic application — the elders shepherd God's flock as under-shepherds, accountable to the chief Shepherd, with Ezekiel's warnings against gain and domineering pressed onto Christian leadership. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam values just leadership and the trust (*amāna*) of authority — leaders as shepherds answerable for their flock is a well-known prophetic saying (hadith), and the Quran condemns leaders who exploit those under them; the specific Ezekiel-34 shepherd-oracle and its application to a "chief Shepherd" Messiah and church elders are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Psalm 23 (with the shepherd-king / messianic-shepherd imagery) — the "chief Shepherd" and the crown (1 Peter 5:4)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 5:4
**Text says:** "And when the chief Shepherd is revealed, you will receive the unfading crown of glory."
**Context:** The promise that crowns the pastoral charge (5:4): the elders who shepherd rightly will, "when the chief Shepherd is revealed," receive "the unfading crown of glory." The "chief Shepherd" (*archipoimēn*) and the "crown" gather the shepherd-king imagery; no verse is cited.
**Subject:** The chief Shepherd over the under-shepherds, and the shepherd's reward. **τοῦ ἀρχιποίμενος… τὸν ἀμαράντινον τῆς δόξης στέφανον** (*tou archipoimenos… ton amarantinon tēs doxēs stephanon*, "the chief Shepherd… the unfading crown of glory") sets the Messiah as the supreme Shepherd over the elders — drawing on the deep shepherd-field of the Hebrew Bible where **YHWH himself is the shepherd of his people** (**Psalm 23:1**, "YHWH is my shepherd"; Ps 80:1, "shepherd of Israel"; Ps 100:3, "the sheep of his pasture") and where that shepherding is mediated through the Davidic king (the messianic shepherd, Ezek 34:23; Mic 5:4, "he shall shepherd in the strength of YHWH"). *Archipoimēn* (only here in the GS) names the Messiah as that shepherd, "revealed" at his coming (the *apokalypsis*-language of 1:7, 13; 4:13). The "unfading (*amarantinon*) crown of glory" pairs with the "unfading (*amaranton*) inheritance" (1:4) — the imperishable reward over against the withering of all flesh (1:24, Isa 40); the *stephanos* is the victor's wreath, not a royal diadem. 1 Peter quotes none of these; it **applies** the shepherd-imagery (YHWH-as-shepherd, the Davidic/messianic shepherd) to the Messiah as chief Shepherd.
**Fulfillment status:** PARTIAL *(mode: typological application)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **typology / echo**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse and names no source; it **deploys** the shepherd-of-Israel field (the LORD as shepherd, Ps 23; the messianic/Davidic shepherd) as the pattern realized in the "chief Shepherd," the Messiah. The connection is the shepherd-and-flock imagery shared with 5:2–3, here lifted to the supreme Shepherd over the elders. Which strand is foremost — **YHWH-as-shepherd** (Ps 23, 80) or the **Davidic/messianic shepherd** (Ezek 34:23; Mic 5:4) — is not specified, and 1 Peter blends the field rather than citing one passage (5:4 note; Rule 13). The "crown of glory" likewise gathers the reward-imagery (the victor's wreath; the crown of life, cf. Jas 1:12; Rev 2:10) without quoting a verse. The PARTIAL label marks this as typological application (the shepherd-imagery read as realized in the Messiah), not an explicit quotation and not a fulfilled prediction. **[comparative parallel — PROBABLE (typology); the foremost shepherd-strand (YHWH-shepherd vs. Davidic-messianic) — POSSIBLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ps 23 → 1 Pet 5:4). For the shepherd-and-overseer of souls, cf. 1 Pet 2:25; for the chief-Shepherd reward against perishable flesh, cf. 1 Pet 1:4, 24 (Isa 40).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Psalm 23 ("YHWH is my shepherd") and the shepherd-psalms read God as the shepherd of Israel — guiding, providing, protecting his people; the Davidic king shepherds under YHWH (Ezek 34:23; Mic 5). The shepherd-imagery is central to Israel's trust in God; its application to a "chief Shepherd" Messiah who gives an eschatological "crown of glory" to church elders is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The Messiah is read as the shepherd in whom YHWH's own shepherding and the promised Davidic shepherd converge — the "good shepherd" (Jn 10), the "great shepherd" (Heb 13:20), here the "chief Shepherd" (1 Pet 5:4) who rewards faithful under-shepherds at his appearing with the unfading crown; the shepherd-psalm and the messianic-shepherd prophecies are read as realized in him. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam affirms God as the guardian and guide (*al-Walī*, *al-Hādī*) of the believers and honors the prophet-as-shepherd motif; the specific Psalm-23 shepherd-imagery and its application to the Messiah as "chief Shepherd" bestowing an eschatological crown are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Proverbs 3:34 (LXX) — "God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble" (1 Peter 5:5)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 5:5
**Text says:** "Likewise, younger *ones*, submit to *the* elders; and all *of you*, clothe yourselves with humility toward one another, because 'God opposes *the* proud, but to *the* humble he gives grace.'"
**Context:** The turn from the elders to the whole community (5:5): the younger submit to the elders, and all "clothe themselves" (*enkombōsasthe*, tie on as an apron) with humility — grounded by a formal citation of Proverbs. This is the **chapter's one explicit quotation**, and because it is the **sage's voice** (not God's first-person word), it is **not** divine-marked (Rule 30).
**Subject:** God's opposition to the proud and grace to the humble. The Greek **ὁ θεὸς ὑπερηφάνοις ἀντιτάσσεται, ταπεινοῖς δὲ δίδωσιν χάριν** (*ho theos hyperēphanois antitassetai, tapeinois de didōsin charin*, "God opposes the proud, but to the humble he gives grace") quotes **Proverbs 3:34 LXX**. The **Septuagint** reads **"God/Lord"** (κύριος in most LXX witnesses; the form 1 Peter and James share has **ὁ θεός**, "God") as the subject, where the **Hebrew MT reads YHWH** (*ʾim-lalleṣîm hûʾ yālîṣ wĕla-ʿănāwîm yitten-ḥēn*, "toward the scorners he scorns, but to the humble he gives grace") — so the source-referent is **YHWH** (Option C), rendered here as the LXX gives it ("God"). The decisive verbal note: **this is the same citation as James 4:6** — both letters quote Proverbs 3:34 LXX with the substituted subject "**God** (ὁ θεός)" against the proud (where the LXX standard is "the Lord"), evidence of a **shared early-Christian testimonium tradition** (a circulating wisdom-proof-text used in paraenesis, perhaps catechetical), not direct literary borrowing between the two letters. 1 Peter presses it as the ground for mutual humility; the line is the **sage's** generalizing maxim about God, not God's quoted first-person word.
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED *(mode: explicit quotation)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This **is the chapter's one formal citation** — introduced with "because (*hoti*)" + the quotation — and it is a **verbatim** quotation of **Proverbs 3:34 LXX** (in the form that reads "God" for the subject). The deployment is **paraenetic**, not predictive: 1 Peter does not claim a prophecy was fulfilled; it **applies** the wisdom-maxim to ground the command to mutual humility (the "clothe yourselves with humility," *enkombōsasthe*, perhaps recalling Yeshua girding to wash feet, Jn 13). Two notes carry the TT's transparency: (1) **the shared tradition** — the **identical citation appears at James 4:6**, with the same "God (ὁ θεός)"-for-"the Lord" subject against the LXX norm, marking a **common early-Christian use of Proverbs 3:34** rather than one letter quoting the other (5:5 note); (2) **the divine name** — the LXX reads "God/Lord" where the **Hebrew has YHWH** (Option C); the TT renders the citation as 1 Peter gives it ("God") and notes the YHWH source-referent (5:5 note). Because the quoted line is the **sage's** human maxim *about* God (third person), it is **not** marked `@@…@@` (Rule 30 / P-005); the chapter carries no divine-marked saying. **[textual — VERIFIED (citation); the shared Prov 3:34 / Jas 4:6 testimonium tradition — PROBABLE; the LXX "God"-for-YHWH subject — VERIFIED]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Prov 3:34 → 1 Pet 5:5) in `content/en/1-peter/CHAPTER-5.md`. For the same citation elsewhere in the GS, cf. James 4:6 (and the humility-exaltation pattern, Lk 14:11; 18:14).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Proverbs 3:34 belongs to the wisdom-instruction of a father to a son (Prov 1–9) — "toward the scorners he scorns, but to the humble (*ʿănāwîm*) he gives grace": YHWH's settled disposition against arrogance and toward the lowly, a recurring wisdom and prophetic theme (cf. Prov 11:2; 16:18–19; Isa 57:15, God dwelling "with the contrite and humble"). It is read within Israel's wisdom and piety; its citation to ground submission within a Christian community is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Proverbs 3:34 is read as the scriptural ground of Christian humility — God's grace flowing to the lowly, as in the Messiah's own self-humbling (Phil 2:5–11) and his teaching ("the one who humbles himself will be exalted," Lk 14:11; 18:14). 1 Peter 5:5 (with James 4:6) is treated as the apostolic application of the maxim to life within the church; the shared citation is read as common apostolic catechesis. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam strongly affirms that God loves the humble and opposes the arrogant — pride (*kibr*) is condemned and humility (*tawāḍuʿ*) commended (cf. Surah 16:23, "He does not love the arrogant"; 31:18; 7:146), and the very name *Iblīs*' fall is traced to arrogance; the specific Proverbs-3:34 citation and its Christian-community application are not part of the Quranic presentation, though the principle is deeply congenial. [DOCUMENTED]

## Exodus 3:19; Deuteronomy 9:26 — "the mighty hand of God" (1 Peter 5:6)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 5:6
**Text says:** "Humble yourselves therefore under the mighty hand of God, so that he may lift you up in *due* time,"
**Context:** The maxim of 5:5 (grace to the humble) made personal imperative (5:6): humble yourselves **now**, under God's mighty hand, with the promise of being "lifted up (*hypsōsē*) in due time" — the suffering-then-glory shape (1:6–7; 5:10). The "mighty hand" is an **echo** of the Exodus deliverance idiom; no verse is cited.
**Subject:** The mighty hand of God under which Israel — and the readers — are humbled and saved. **ὑπὸ τὴν κραταιὰν χεῖρα τοῦ θεοῦ** (*hypo tēn krataian cheira tou theou*, "under the mighty hand of God") borrows the **Exodus deliverance idiom**: YHWH brings Israel out of Egypt "with a **mighty hand** (*ʿyad ḥăzāqā*; LXX *en cheiri krataia*)" — the strong hand that both judges Egypt and saves Israel (**Exodus 3:19**, "not… except by a mighty hand"; **Deuteronomy 9:26**, Moses pleading that YHWH not destroy "your people… whom you redeemed by your **mighty hand**"; cf. Ex 6:1; 13:9; Deut 4:34; 5:15). The idiom names God's sovereign power. 1 Peter takes it and turns it toward humility-and-exaltation: to humble oneself **under** that hand is to trust the power that "lifts up in due time" — the same hand that delivered Israel now bearing the readers through their "little" suffering (5:10). The Exodus diction is recognizable; the application is to the readers' present trial and hope.
**Fulfillment status:** DEBATED *(mode: echo)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion / echo**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse and uses no formula; the "mighty hand of God" is a **standing Exodus-deliverance idiom** (and a wider biblical phrase for God's power), here applied to the posture of humility under God's sovereignty. The connection is deployment, not prediction: the letter borrows the idiom of the hand that humbled Egypt and redeemed Israel and presses it on its diaspora readers — consonant with the letter's whole exile-and-deliverance framing (1:1, 17; 2:9–10). Because "mighty hand" recurs across the Pentateuch (Ex 3:19; 6:1; 13:9; Deut 4:34; 9:26; etc.), **no single oracle** is in view; the entry marks the idiom, not a quotation (5:6 note; Rule 13). The DEBATED label marks this as an echo — diction borrowed and applied — carrying no fulfillment-claim and no explicit citation. **[comparative parallel — POSSIBLE; a recurring Exodus idiom, no single source-verse]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ex 3:19; Deut 9:26 → 1 Pet 5:6).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: The "mighty hand" (and "outstretched arm") is the classic idiom of the Exodus — YHWH delivering Israel from Egypt "with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm" (Deut 5:15; 26:8), recited in the Passover Haggadah as the paradigm of redemption; it names God's saving and judging power. It belongs to Israel's deliverance story; its application to humbling oneself under God toward a Messiah-shaped exaltation is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The "mighty hand of God" is read as God's sovereign power under which believers humble themselves in trust, confident he will exalt "in due time" — the Exodus deliverance read as the pattern of God's care for his people through suffering; 1 Peter 5:6 frames humility as submission to the saving hand that will lift up. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam affirms God's absolute power and that all things are in his hand (cf. Surah 36:83; 67:1), and the deliverance of Musa and Israel from Pharaoh by God's power is narrated repeatedly (Surah 26; 28); the specific "mighty hand" idiom applied to self-humbling before God in 1 Peter is not part of the Quranic presentation, though God's saving power is a central theme. [DOCUMENTED]

## Psalm 55:22 — "cast all your anxiety on him" (1 Peter 5:7)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 5:7
**Text says:** "having cast all your anxiety upon him, because it matters to him concerning you."
**Context:** A participle dependent on "humble yourselves" (5:6): humbling oneself under God's hand **includes** casting one's cares on him, "because it matters to him concerning you" (*autō melei peri hymōn*) — God's care is the ground. The "cast your burden" language is an **echo** of the Psalter; no verse is cited.
**Subject:** Casting one's burden on God, who sustains. **πᾶσαν τὴν μέριμναν ὑμῶν ἐπιρίψαντες ἐπ' αὐτόν** (*pasan tēn merimnan hymōn epiripsantes ep' auton*, "having cast all your anxiety upon him") echoes **Psalm 55:22** (MT 55:23; LXX 54:23) — "**Cast your burden on YHWH** (*hashlēk ʿal-YHWH yĕhābĕkā*; LXX *epirripson epi kyrion tēn merimnan sou*), and he will sustain you; he will never let the righteous be shaken." The verb **ἐπιρίπτω** ("cast upon") matches the LXX, and "anxiety/care" (*merimna*) matches the LXX's *merimna* ("burden, care"). In the psalm κύριος = **YHWH** (Option C). 1 Peter takes the psalmist's confidence — hand the weight to God, who holds you — and grounds it in God's personal care ("it matters to him concerning you"). 1 Peter does not quote the psalm (no formula, the verb shifted to a participle joined to v.6); it **echoes** its diction and its assurance.
**Fulfillment status:** DEBATED *(mode: echo)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion / echo**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse and uses no formula; the "cast your care upon him" phrase reworks **Psalm 55:22** (LXX 54:23) into a participle dependent on "humble yourselves" (5:6), with the LXX verb *epirripto* and noun *merimna* preserved. The connection is deployment, not prediction: the letter borrows the psalmist's act of handing the burden to God and roots it in God's care for the readers; the wisdom-echo also resonates with the broader "cast your care on God" Psalter and wisdom tradition (cf. Ps 37:5; Ps 22:8–10), so the entry marks the **diction and assurance** rather than a quoted oracle (5:7 note; Rule 13). On the divine name: in Psalm 55 κύριος = YHWH (Option C); 1 Peter applies the care to "him" (God of v.6), and the TT notes the YHWH source-referent. The DEBATED label marks this as an echo — diction borrowed and applied — carrying no fulfillment-claim and no explicit citation. **[comparative parallel — POSSIBLE; a Psalter echo, no formula citation]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ps 55:22 → 1 Pet 5:7).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Psalm 55 is a lament — betrayed by a close friend, the psalmist cries to YHWH and then turns to trust: "Cast your burden on YHWH, and he will sustain you" (55:22), the confidence that God upholds the righteous. It is read within Israel's prayer and trust in God amid trouble; its reuse to ground the readers' casting of anxiety in the context of Christian suffering is the letter's application, not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Psalm 55:22 is read as the scriptural warrant for casting all anxiety on God in trust of his care; 1 Peter 5:7 is treated as the apostolic application — humbling oneself under God's hand means handing him every care, "because he cares for you" — the psalm's confidence renewed for the suffering church. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam centrally teaches reliance on God (*tawakkul*) — casting one's affairs and worries upon him while doing one's part (cf. Surah 65:3, "whoever relies upon God, he is sufficient for him"; 3:159); the specific Psalm-55 "cast your burden" verse is not part of the Quranic presentation, though entrusting one's cares to God is a core Islamic disposition. [DOCUMENTED]

## Psalm 22:13 — the roaring lion (1 Peter 5:8)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 5:8
**Text says:** "Be sober, be watchful. Your adversary, *the* slanderer, walks about like a roaring lion, seeking *someone* to devour;"
**Context:** The call to vigilance (5:8–9): be sober and watchful, for the "adversary" (*antidikos*, a legal opponent), the "slanderer" (*diabolos*), prowls "like a roaring lion" seeking prey — to be resisted "firm in the faith." The lion-imagery is an **echo** of the Psalter's predator-figure; no verse is cited.
**Subject:** The devouring predator — the adversary figured as a roaring lion. **ὡς λέων ὠρυόμενος… ζητῶν τινα καταπιεῖν** (*hōs leōn ōryomenos… zētōn tina katapiein*, "like a roaring lion… seeking someone to devour") echoes the predator-imagery of the Psalter, paradigmatically **Psalm 22:13** (MT 22:14; LXX 21:14) — "they open their mouths against me, **like a ravening and roaring lion**" (LXX *hōs leōn ho harpazōn kai ōryomenos*) — the lament-figure of murderous enemies surrounding the sufferer (cf. Ps 7:2; 10:9; 17:12; 35:17, the lion seeking to tear). 1 Peter applies the figure to the **adversary** — *antidikos* (legal "opponent/accuser," courtroom language) paired with *diabolos* (lit. "slanderer/accuser," the term behind "devil") — casting the readers' spiritual danger in the Psalter's predator imagery. The verb "devour" (*katapiein*, "swallow down") sharpens the threat. 1 Peter quotes no verse; it **echoes** the roaring-lion figure of the laments.
**Fulfillment status:** DEBATED *(mode: echo)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion / echo**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse and uses no formula; the "roaring lion" is a **Psalter lament-figure** for the deadly enemy/predator, here applied to the *diabolos*. The connection is deployment, not prediction: the letter borrows the imagery of the lion that roars and devours (Ps 22:13 closest in diction — *leōn… ōryomenos*; with the wider predator-Psalter behind it) and presses it onto the readers' adversary, framing vigilance. Because the lion-predator image recurs across the laments (Ps 7:2; 10:9; 17:12; 22:13; 35:17), **no single oracle** is uniquely in view; the entry marks the imagery, not a quotation (5:8 note; Rule 13). The DEBATED label marks this as an echo — imagery borrowed and applied — carrying no fulfillment-claim and no explicit citation. **[comparative parallel — POSSIBLE; a Psalter predator-image, no single source-verse]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ps 22:13 → 1 Pet 5:8).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Psalm 22 is a lament of a sufferer encircled by enemies, who "open their mouths… like a ravening and roaring lion" (22:13) — the lion is a standard image of violent, devouring foes across the Psalter (Ps 7; 10; 17; 35). It is read within Israel's prayer in distress; its application to a singular spiritual adversary (the *diabolos*) is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The roaring lion is read as the imagery of the devil's threat against the believer, drawn from the Psalter's predator-figure; 1 Peter 5:8 is treated as a call to sober vigilance and steadfast resistance "firm in the faith" (5:9) against the adversary — the Psalter's encircling lion now naming the spiritual danger. [DOCUMENTED]

## Babylon / the exile-empire (Isaiah; Jeremiah; Daniel) — "she in Babylon" (1 Peter 5:13)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 5:13
**Text says:** "She *who is* in Babylon (Bavel), chosen together *with you*, greets you, and Markos (Mark) my son."
**Context:** The epistolary close (5:12–14): the sending greeting — "she… in Babylon, chosen-together" (*syneklektē*, the co-elect church, feminine) and "Mark my son." "Babylon" as the sending location carries a **typological identity-echo** of the exile-empire; it is a place-name in the greeting, not a citation.
**Subject:** Babylon as the exile-empire — and the readers' diaspora identity. **ἡ ἐν Βαβυλῶνι συνεκλεκτή** (*hē en Babylōni syneklektē*, "she in Babylon, chosen-together") names the sending church's location, but **"Babylon" is widely read as a cipher for Rome** — the great exile-empire that destroyed Jerusalem and deported Judah (**2 Kings 25; Isaiah 13–14; 21; 47**, the oracles against Babylon; **Jeremiah 50–51**, the fall of Babylon; **Daniel**, the exile under Babylon and its kings). In the Second-Temple and early-Christian imagination, "Babylon" had become the symbol of the world-power hostile to God's people — most explicitly in **Revelation 17–18** ("Babylon the great," the imperial city). 1 Peter's whole frame casts the readers as "**resident-aliens and sojourners/exiles**" (*paroikoi kai parepidēmoi*, 1:1, 17; 2:11) — a diaspora living out their faith in exile — so "Babylon" fits the letter's identity-theology: the church writes from within the exile-world. This is an **identity-echo / typology**, not a quotation of any prophetic oracle (5:13 note; Babylon = Rome, Tier-2; Companion §B).
**Fulfillment status:** DEBATED *(mode: echo — typological place-name)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **typological echo by place-name**, not a citation and not a prophecy — 1 Peter names "Babylon" in a greeting, with no formula and no quoted oracle; the entry is included as **comparative-parallel** because the name carries the exile-empire symbolism of the prophets (Isa 13–14; 47; Jer 50–51; Dan) and the early-Christian "Babylon = the hostile world-power" tradition (Rev 17–18). The TT keeps "Babylon" in the text and flags the widely-held reading that it is a **cipher for Rome** (the place of Peter's later ministry; the exile-empire of the readers' world) without resolving the question literally (5:13 note; Tier-2; Rule 13 / Rule 3). **Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Babylon is the historical empire of the exile — the destroyer of the First Temple and the place of Judah's captivity (2 Kgs 25; Ps 137, "by the rivers of Babylon"); the prophets' oracles against Babylon (Isa 13; 47; Jer 50–51) promise its fall and Israel's return. "Babylon" becomes a byword for exile and for the arrogant world-power judged by God; its use as a cipher for Rome by a Christian writer is recognized as the early-Christian appropriation of that symbolism, not part of the Jewish reading of the prophets. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: "Babylon" in 1 Peter 5:13 is widely read as a cryptic name for Rome — the imperial city, hostile world-power, and place of Peter's ministry — consonant with the same usage in Revelation 17–18; it underscores the letter's portrait of the church as exiles and sojourners in a Babylon-like world awaiting deliverance. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: The Quran does not develop the Babylon-exile typology; Babylon (*Bābil*) appears in connection with the angels Harut and Marut (Surah 2:102), not as a symbol of an exile-empire or of Rome; the specific "Babylon = the hostile world-power / Rome" identity-echo is not part of the Quranic presentation.
