## Note on 1 Peter's mode of allusion (an epistle, not a narrative)

1 Peter is a **letter** — *argument and exhortation* in the apostle's own voice, not a narrative of events fulfilling prediction. So its use of the Hebrew Bible is not "prophecy → fulfillment" in the gospel-and-Acts sense. The letter **deploys** the Hebrew Bible *paraenetically and typologically*: it **quotes** it to ground a claim (the stone-catena of 2:6–8 — Isaiah 28:16, Psalm 118:22, Isaiah 8:14 — and the Psalm-34 "taste that the Lord is good" of 2:3), it **applies type-scenes and titles** of Israel's story to the readers (Israel's covenant titles of Exodus 19 / Isaiah 43 and Hosea's "not a people → people of God," 2:9–10), and it **echoes** the Servant Song of Isaiah 53 (interwoven, not formula-cited, through 2:22–25). The dual-label and citation-vs-allusion system carries over unchanged from the gospel companions; what shifts is how to read the **status line**. Here "fulfillment status" should be read as **mode of deployment**:

- **CLAIMED** ≈ **explicit quotation** — the letter cites the OT with a citation marker ("because it is contained in scripture," 2:6) or carries it verbatim and presses it as authoritative for the readers (Ps 34:8 at 2:3; the stone-catena, 2:6–8; Hosea at 2:10).
- **PARTIAL** ≈ **typological application** — the letter reads an Israel institution or title as the pattern now realized in the readers (the covenant titles of Ex 19 / Isa 43 transferred to the church, 2:9), without pressing a verse as predictive.
- **DEBATED** ≈ **echo** — the diction of an OT scene is interiorized into the exhortation (the "on a tree" of Deut 21:23, 2:24), without citation or fulfillment-claim.

Because 1 Peter quotes the OT in the *third* person about God as often as it carries God's *first*-person word, **speech-marking (Rule 30 / A-004)** is selective: the letter is the apostle's **human** exhortation throughout and is not marked; only where the *source verse* is God's own first-person word does the quotation carry `@@…@@`. In 1 Peter 2 that is the **single** marked saying — "@@Behold, I lay in Zion a stone…@@" (2:6, Isaiah 28:16, where the speaker is God, "I lay"). The other stone-texts (Psalm 118:22, the *psalmist's* voice; Isaiah 8:14, the *prophet's* voice), the Psalm-34 citation (2:3, third person "the Lord *is* good"), and the Isaiah-53 weave (2:22–25, the prophet *about* the Servant) are **not** marked. Where κύριος in a citation stands for YHWH, the entries note it per **Option C** (the main text renders "the Lord" + Tier-2; the source-referent is YHWH; P-006/§ κύριος Option C in `1-peter.md`).

## Psalm 34:8 (LXX 33:9) — "taste that the Lord is good" (1 Peter 2:3)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 2:3
**Text says:** "if *indeed* you tasted that the Lord *is* good."
**Context:** The close of the milk-and-growth exhortation (2:1–3): having put away the vices (2:1) and longed for the pure spiritual milk (2:2), the readers are reminded — with a conditional "if *indeed*" (*ei*) that assumes the fact — that they have already "tasted that the Lord *is* good." The line is the **psalmist's** voice (third person, "the Lord *is* good"), so it is **not** divine-marked (Rule 30).
**Subject:** The goodness of YHWH, tasted. 1 Peter quotes **Psalm 34:8** (LXX 33:9) — "taste and see that the Lord (YHWH) is good." The Greek **ἐγεύσασθε ὅτι χρηστὸς ὁ κύριος** (*egeusasthe hoti chrēstos ho kyrios*, "you tasted that the Lord *is* good") follows the LXX wording (with the psalm's imperative "taste" recast as the indicative "you tasted"). In the psalm the speaker is David, and *kyrios* renders the Hebrew **YHWH** (*ṭaʿămû ûrĕʾû kî-ṭôb YHWH*) — so κύριος here = **YHWH** (Option C). 1 Peter lifts the psalm's invitation and applies it to the readers' experience of "the Lord" — and in the very next verse (2:4) "the Lord" is the Messiah, the living stone, so the letter lets the psalm's κύριος (YHWH) flow into the Messiah-referent without comment (a κύριος referent-flow the TT keeps "the Lord" and notes). Note too the likely **wordplay**: **χρηστός** (*chrēstos*, "good/kind") sounds all but identical to **Χριστός** (*Christos*, "Messiah") — "you tasted that the Lord is *chrēstos*" hovers over "…that the Lord is *Christos*."
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED *(mode: explicit quotation)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is a **verbatim quotation** (LXX Ps 33:9), though 1 Peter carries it without a formula — the conditional "if *indeed* you tasted…" simply absorbs the psalm's words into the exhortation, their authority assumed (and the catchword bridge is the milk/tasting imagery of 2:2). The deployment is **paraenetic**, not predictive: 1 Peter does not say a prophecy was fulfilled; it **applies** the psalm's call to taste YHWH's goodness to the readers' present life in "the Lord." Two features carry the TT's restraint: (1) the **κύριος referent-flow** — in the psalm κύριος = YHWH (Option C); 1 Peter slides it toward the Messiah of 2:4 without resolving the identification, which the TT renders "the Lord" and notes (2:3 note); (2) the **chrēstos/Christos pun** — likely intended but unprovable, flagged not pressed (Rule 13). The same Psalm 34 returns at 1 Peter 3:10–12 (Ps 34:12–16), a sign the letter holds the whole psalm in view. **[textual — VERIFIED (citation); the κύριος = Messiah referent-flow — CLAIMED; the chrēstos/Christos pun — POSSIBLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ps 34:8 → 1 Pet 2:3) in `content/en/1-peter/CHAPTER-2.md`.
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Psalm 34 is a thanksgiving and wisdom psalm of David — "taste and see that YHWH is good; happy is the one who takes refuge in him" — an invitation to know God's goodness through experience and to live in the fear of YHWH (the psalm's second half is wisdom instruction). It is read within Israel's worship and piety; the goodness tasted is YHWH's, and the redirection of that tasting to a Messiah identified with "the Lord" is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The verse is read as the believer's experiential knowledge of the Lord's goodness — and, with 2:4's "living stone," of the Messiah as the "Lord" whose goodness is tasted; the *chrēstos/Christos* assonance is often heard as a deliberate pointer to the Messiah. 1 Peter 2:3 is treated as the apostolic application of the psalm to the church's growth in salvation (2:2). [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam affirms the goodness and kindness of God — al-Barr ("the Source of Goodness"), ar-Raḥmān, ar-Raḥīm are among the divine names — and the call to take refuge in God and live in awe of him; the specific Psalm-34 invitation and its application to a Messiah identified as "the Lord" are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Isaiah 28:16 — "behold, I lay in Zion a cornerstone" (1 Peter 2:6)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 2:6
**Text says:** "Because it is contained in scripture: @@'Behold, I lay in Zion (Tsiyon) a stone, a chosen, precious cornerstone, and the *one* believing in him will never be put to shame.'@@"
**Context:** The first text of the **living-stone catena** (2:6–8). Having called the Messiah a "living stone — rejected by people but chosen and precious before God" (2:4) and the readers "living stones" built into a spiritual house (2:5), the letter grounds the rejection/election contrast in three scriptures, introduced with a formal citation marker ("because it is contained in scripture," *dioti periechei en graphē*). The quoted line is **God's direct first-person word** ("I lay"), and is therefore marked `@@…@@` (Rule 30 / A-004) — the chapter's one divine-marked saying.
**Subject:** The cornerstone God lays in Zion. 1 Peter quotes **Isaiah 28:16** (LXX) — God's word against the scoffers of Jerusalem who have made "a covenant with death": "Behold, I lay (*tithēmi*) in Zion a stone… a precious cornerstone… and the one who believes will not be put to shame." The Greek **ἰδοὺ τίθημι ἐν Σιὼν λίθον ἀκρογωνιαῖον ἐκλεκτὸν ἔντιμον, καὶ ὁ πιστεύων ἐπ' αὐτῷ οὐ μὴ καταισχυνθῇ** (*idou tithēmi en Siōn lithon akrogōniaion eklekton entimon, kai ho pisteuōn ep' autō ou mē kataischynthē*) follows the LXX, which reads "will not be put to shame" (*ou mē kataischynthē*) where the Hebrew MT reads "will not be in haste / will not panic" (*lōʾ yāḥîš*) — a notable LXX/MT divergence the letter follows the LXX in. **ἀκρογωνιαῖος** = "cornerstone / capstone." In Isaiah the speaker is God (YHWH), laying the sure foundation-stone in Zion; 1 Peter reads that stone as the Messiah, "chosen and precious" (matching 2:4's *eklekton entimon*), and the believer who trusts him as the one who "will never be put to shame."
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED *(mode: explicit quotation)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This **is a formal, marked citation** — introduced "because it is contained in scripture" (*dioti periechei en graphē*) — and because the source verse is God's own first-person word ("**I** lay in Zion"), it is marked `@@…@@` (Rule 30 / A-004; the only such marking in the chapter). The deployment is **paraenetic-and-applicative**, not predictive: 1 Peter does not claim Isaiah foretold a datable event; it **applies** Isaiah's sure-foundation oracle to the Messiah and grounds the readers' security ("never be put to shame") in trusting him. Two features carry the TT's restraint: (1) the **LXX/MT divergence** — "put to shame" (LXX) vs. "panic/be in haste" (MT) — the letter follows the LXX, and the TT renders the citation as 1 Peter gives it and notes the divergence (2:6 note); (2) the **stone's referent** — that the cornerstone is the Messiah is the letter's reading (consonant with the gospels' use of the same stone-text, Mt 21:42 par.; Rom 9:33 fuses Isa 28:16 with Isa 8:14), and is presented as the letter's appropriation. **[textual — VERIFIED (citation); the LXX/MT divergence — DOCUMENTED; the cornerstone-as-Messiah reading — CLAIMED]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Isa 28:16 → 1 Pet 2:6). For the same stone-texts applied to the Messiah, see the gospel PROPHECY companions (Ps 118:22 / Isa 8:14 → the rejected-stone parable).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Isaiah 28:16 is God's word of judgment-and-assurance against the leaders of Jerusalem: while their "covenant with death" will be swept away, God lays in Zion a tested, precious foundation-stone, and the one who trusts (MT: "will not panic") stands secure. The stone is read variously — the Davidic kingship, the temple/Zion, the faithful remnant, or God's own reliable purpose — within Israel's history; its identification with a crucified Messiah is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Isaiah 28:16 is read as a foundational messianic stone-text — the Messiah the sure cornerstone God lays in Zion, on whom faith rests without shame; 1 Peter 2:6 (with the catena of 2:7–8) is treated as the apostolic gathering of the OT stone-prophecies onto the Messiah (cf. Rom 9:33; Eph 2:20; the gospels' rejected-stone). [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam affirms God as the sure foundation and refuge of those who trust him, and the theme of the secure believer who "will not grieve" (cf. Surah 2:62; 10:62); the specific Zion-cornerstone oracle of Isaiah 28 and its application to the Messiah are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Psalm 118:22 (LXX 117:22) — "the stone the builders rejected" (1 Peter 2:7)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 2:7
**Text says:** "To you therefore who believe *is* the preciousness, but to those disbelieving, '*the* stone which the builders rejected — this *one* became *the* head of *the* corner,'"
**Context:** The second text of the catena (2:7), drawn in by the believe/disbelieve contrast: to believers the stone is "preciousness" (picking up *entimon*, 2:4, 6); to the disbelieving it is the rejected-then-vindicated stone. The line is the **psalmist's** voice (third person, narrating what the builders and God did), so it is **not** divine-marked (Rule 30).
**Subject:** The rejected stone become the chief corner. 1 Peter quotes **Psalm 118:22** (LXX 117:22) — "the stone which the builders rejected has become the head of the corner." The Greek **λίθος ὃν ἀπεδοκίμασαν οἱ οἰκοδομοῦντες, οὗτος ἐγενήθη εἰς κεφαλὴν γωνίας** (*lithos hon apedokimasan hoi oikodomountes, houtos egenēthē eis kephalēn gōnias*) follows the LXX verbatim. In the psalm — a thanksgiving liturgy for deliverance, sung at the festivals — the "rejected stone" is the rescued one (the king, or Israel) whom God has vindicated against those who despised him. 1 Peter reads it of the **Messiah**: rejected (*apodokimazō*, the verb already at 2:4, "rejected by people") by the human "builders," made by God the "head of the corner." This is **the same stone-text the gospels apply to the Messiah** — the climax of the parable of the tenants (Mark 12:10–11 // Matthew 21:42 // Luke 20:17), and Kefa's own word before the council (Acts 4:11, "the stone rejected by you the builders").
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED *(mode: explicit quotation)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is a **verbatim quotation** (LXX Ps 117:22), carried without a fresh formula (it shares the "it is contained in scripture" of 2:6) and welded to the catena by the catchword *apodokimazō* ("reject," 2:4 → 2:7) and the stone/cornerstone field. The deployment is **applicative**, not predictive: 1 Peter does not say the psalm foretold a datable event; it **applies** the psalm's rejected-and-vindicated stone to the Messiah, exactly as the gospel tradition does (the rejected-stone is one of the most widely shared early-church stone-texts). The "head of the corner" (*kephalē gōnias*) — whether a foundation-corner stone or a coping/capstone — is left as the psalm has it (Rule 13). The identification of the stone with the Messiah is the letter's reading, here shared with the wider tradition (Mk 12:10 par.; Acts 4:11). **[textual — VERIFIED (citation); the stone-as-Messiah reading — CLAIMED]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ps 118:22 → 1 Pet 2:7). For the same psalm in the gospels, see the rejected-stone parable companions (Ps 118:22 → Mk 12:10 par.).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Psalm 118 is a festival thanksgiving (part of the Hallel, sung at Passover and the pilgrim feasts) — the rejected stone become the chief corner is read as the deliverance of the king, or of Israel, despised by the nations yet exalted by God; "this is YHWH's doing, marvelous in our eyes" (118:23). It belongs to Israel's worship and the vindication of the righteous/the people; its application to a crucified-and-raised Messiah is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Psalm 118:22 is read as a prime stone-prophecy of the Messiah — rejected by the leaders (the "builders"), made by God the cornerstone in resurrection; 1 Peter 2:7 (with Acts 4:11 and the gospel parable, Mk 12:10 par.) is treated as the apostolic application of the psalm to the Messiah's rejection and vindication. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam knows the theme of prophets and messengers rejected by their own people yet vindicated by God (a recurring Quranic pattern, e.g. Surah 23; the rejected-then-honored servant of God); the specific Psalm-118 stone-text and its application to the Messiah's death and resurrection are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Isaiah 8:14 — "a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense" (1 Peter 2:8)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 2:8
**Text says:** "and 'a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense'; *those* who stumble *at* the word, disbelieving — to which also they were appointed."
**Context:** The third text of the catena (2:8), the dark counterpart to the cornerstone: the same stone that is "precious" to believers is, to the disbelieving, a stone they trip over. The clause "to which also they were appointed" (*eis ho kai etethēsan*) attaches the appointing to the stumbling — election/destiny language the chapter leaves unresolved. The line is the **prophet's** voice (Isaiah's words about the LORD's action), so it is **not** divine-marked (Rule 30).
**Subject:** The stone that makes Israel stumble. 1 Peter quotes **Isaiah 8:14** — in its setting, YHWH himself "will become a sanctuary, but a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense to both houses of Israel." The Greek **λίθος προσκόμματος καὶ πέτρα σκανδάλου** (*lithos proskommatos kai petra skandalou*, "a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense") follows a reading closer to the Hebrew MT and the later Greek versions (Aquila/Symmachus/Theodotion) than to the main LXX of Isaiah 8:14 — the LXX softens "stone of stumbling" — so 1 Peter (like Paul at Rom 9:33) draws on the **MT-shaped** "stumbling-stone" tradition. In Isaiah the stumbling-stone is **YHWH** (to those who do not sanctify him); 1 Peter, having identified the cornerstone with the Messiah (2:6–7), reads the stumbling-stone of Isaiah 8:14 of the **same** stone — honor to those who believe, an obstacle to those who do not. The clause **εἰς ὃ καὶ ἐτέθησαν** ("to which also they were appointed/set," *tithēmi* — the same verb as Isaiah's "I lay," 2:6) plays on the stone-laying: God "lays" the stone (2:6) and "lays/appoints" the stumblers to their stumbling.
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED *(mode: explicit quotation)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is a **quotation** (Isa 8:14, MT-shaped "stumbling-stone" tradition rather than the main LXX), carried into the catena without a fresh formula and joined to it by the stone-word-field and the *tithēmi*/"appointed" wordplay. The deployment is **applicative**, not predictive: 1 Peter does not claim Isaiah foretold a datable event; it **applies** the stumbling-stone oracle to the disbelieving, the negative pole of the cornerstone the believing trust. Three features carry the TT's restraint: (1) the **textual base** — 1 Peter (with Rom 9:33) follows the Hebrew-shaped "stone of stumbling," not the softened main LXX, and the TT renders the citation as 1 Peter gives it and notes the textual point (2:8 note); (2) the **referent-shift** — in Isaiah the stumbling-stone is YHWH; 1 Peter reads it of the Messiah-stone, an interpretive move presented as the letter's; (3) the **"appointed" clause** — whether *etethēsan* appoints them to *stumbling* or to *disbelief*, and how far the "appointing" reaches (destiny / consequence), is the live theological crux the TT renders without resolving (Rule 13; 2:8 note; editorial-log "2:8 ἐτέθησαν"). **[textual — VERIFIED (citation); the MT-shaped textual base — DOCUMENTED; the YHWH→Messiah-stone reading — CLAIMED; the scope of "appointed" — POSSIBLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Isa 8:14 → 1 Pet 2:8). For the same stumbling-stone, cf. Rom 9:33 (Isa 28:16 + 8:14 fused).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Isaiah 8:14 is part of the Immanuel cycle: YHWH, the one to be feared and sanctified, "will become a sanctuary" for those who trust him "but a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense to both houses of Israel" — those who reject God's word stumble over God's own action. The stumbling-stone is YHWH himself; the reading belongs to Isaiah's eighth-century crisis and to the standing warning that rejecting God's counsel brings ruin. Its application to a Messiah-stone is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Isaiah 8:14 is read as a stone-prophecy of the Messiah's twofold effect — salvation to those who believe, stumbling to those who reject him; 1 Peter 2:8 (with Rom 9:33, where it is fused with Isa 28:16) is treated as the apostolic application of the stumbling-stone to unbelief, the negative pole of the cornerstone. The "appointed to stumble" is read variously (foreordination; the consequence of unbelief). [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam knows the theme that God's signs and revelation guide the receptive but increase the rejecters in loss — "He leads astray many by it and guides many by it" (Surah 2:26; cf. 17:82, the Quran "a healing… but it increases the wrongdoers only in loss"); the specific Isaiah stumbling-stone and its application to the Messiah are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Exodus 19:6 + Isaiah 43:20–21 — "a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation" (1 Peter 2:9)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 2:9
**Text says:** "But you *are* a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for *God's* possession, so that you may declare the excellencies of the *One* who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light;"
**Context:** The turn from the disbelieving stumblers (2:7–8) to the believing readers (2:9–10): over against those who reject the stone, "**you**" are given Israel's own titles, with a purpose — to "declare the excellencies" of the God who called you. The titles are **applied**, not formula-cited (no "it is written"), so this is typological/applicative deployment.
**Subject:** Israel's covenant titles applied to the readers. 1 Peter strings together titles from two HB sources. **γένος ἐκλεκτόν** ("a chosen race") and "a people for *God's* possession" + "declare the excellencies" draw on **Isaiah 43:20–21** (LXX) — "my chosen race (*to genos mou to eklekton*)… the people I formed for myself (*laon mou hon periepoiēsamēn*) to **declare my excellencies** (*tas aretas mou diēgeisthai*)." **βασίλειον ἱεράτευμα, ἔθνος ἅγιον** ("a royal priesthood, a holy nation") quotes **Exodus 19:6** (LXX) — at Sinai, before the covenant, YHWH's word to Israel: "you shall be to me a royal priesthood (*basileion hierateuma*) and a holy nation (*ethnos hagion*)." "A people for *possession*" (*laos eis peripoiēsin*) echoes the covenant-people language (Ex 19:5, *laos periousios*; Isa 43:21; cf. Mal 3:17). 1 Peter takes the titles God gave **Israel** — chosen, priestly, holy, God's own possession — and applies them to "the elect sojourners of the dispersion" (1:1), the believing community gathered at the cornerstone (2:4–6). The "called you out of darkness into his marvelous light" carries the exodus/new-creation note of summons.
**Fulfillment status:** PARTIAL *(mode: typological application)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **typological application**, not a formula citation — 1 Peter introduces no "it is written" and quotes no single verse continuously; it **gathers** Israel's titles (Ex 19:6 + Isa 43:20–21, with the covenant-possession language) and **applies** them to the readers. The connection is verbally exact (the LXX phrases *basileion hierateuma, ethnos hagion, genos eklekton, declare the excellencies* are reproduced), which is why the entry is labeled PARTIAL rather than a mere echo — but the move is **transfer**, not prediction-fulfillment. The central interpretive question is the **Rule-3 restraint**: 1 Peter applies Israel's titles to the church **without** saying Israel has lost them — the letter draws no supersessionist conclusion, and the TT renders the transfer **as the Greek has it**, importing no replacement-theology (Rule 3; 2:9 note). Whether the application implies continuity, expansion, or replacement of Israel is the very point the letter does not argue and the TT does not resolve (the Readings below mark the divide). **[comparative parallel — VERIFIED (the title-source is Ex 19:6 / Isa 43); the transfer to the church — CLAIMED; whether it implies replacement of Israel — DEBATED (not resolved, Rule 3)]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ex 19:6; Isa 43:20–21 → 1 Pet 2:9). Cf. the same priesthood-of-the-people theme at 1 Pet 2:5 ("a holy priesthood").
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Exodus 19:6 is the heart of Israel's vocation — at Sinai, before the covenant is cut, YHWH names Israel "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation," a people set apart to mediate God's presence among the nations; Isaiah 43:20–21 reaffirms "my chosen people… whom I formed for myself to declare my praise" to exiled Israel. These titles are Israel's covenant identity; their transfer to a (largely gentile) church as the people of God is not part of the Jewish reading, which holds Israel's election to be irrevocable. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: 1 Peter 2:9 is read as the church's identity in the Messiah — the believing community, Jew and gentile, given Israel's titles as the people of God gathered at the cornerstone; readings differ on whether this means the church has **replaced** Israel (a supersessionist reading), **expanded** Israel's election, or shares it without displacing the Jewish people (a post-Holocaust emphasis on the irrevocable election of Israel, Rom 11:29). 1 Peter itself draws no explicit conclusion. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam affirms that God chose Israel and favored them above the nations in their time (cf. Surah 2:47; 45:16), and presents the Muslim community as "the best community brought forth for humankind" (Surah 3:110) commissioned to enjoin good; the specific transfer of Israel's Sinai titles to a Christian church is not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Hosea 1:9–10; 2:23 (MT 2:1, 2:25) — "not a people → people of God" (1 Peter 2:10)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 2:10
**Text says:** "*you* who once *were* not a people but now *are* a people of God, the *ones* not having received mercy but now having received mercy."
**Context:** The completion of the identity statement (2:9–10): the titles of 2:9 are sealed with Hosea's reversal — the readers were "not a people," now they are "a people of God"; once "not pitied," now "pitied." The line is the **prophet's** voice (Hosea about God's reversal, third person), so it is **not** divine-marked (Rule 30).
**Subject:** The "not my people → my people" reversal. 1 Peter draws on **Hosea** — the prophet's sign-children named **Lo-Ammi** ("not my people," **Hosea 1:9**) and **Lo-Ruhamah** ("not pitied," 1:6) — and the promised reversal: "in the place where it was said to them, 'you are not my people,' they will be called 'sons of the living God'" (**Hosea 1:10** [MT 2:1]), and "I will have mercy on Lo-Ruhamah… and I will say to Lo-Ammi, 'you are my people'" (**Hosea 2:23** [MT 2:25]). The Greek **οἵ ποτε οὐ λαὸς νῦν δὲ λαὸς θεοῦ, οἱ οὐκ ἠλεημένοι νῦν δὲ ἐλεηθέντες** (*hoi pote ou laos nyn de laos theou…*, "who once *were* not a people but now *are* a people of God…") condenses Hosea's "not a people"/"not pitied" → "people of God"/"pitied" into a compact then/now antithesis (rather than quoting Hosea continuously). In Hosea the reversal is for **unfaithful Israel** — the northern kingdom under judgment, promised restoration to covenant; 1 Peter applies the "not a people → people of God" word to the readers' inclusion into the people of God. (Paul applies the same Hosea texts at Rom 9:25–26.)
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED *(mode: explicit quotation / applied)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **quotation-by-application** — 1 Peter does not introduce Hosea with a formula but reproduces Hosea's distinctive "not a people / people of God" and "not pitied / pitied" antitheses, pressing them on the readers; it is more than an echo (the diction is Hosea's), so it is labeled CLAIMED, but the mode is **applicative**, not prediction-fulfillment. The interpretive move is the **transfer**: Hosea's promise to restore **apostate Israel** is read of the readers' coming-to-be the people of God — an appropriation the letter shares with Paul (Rom 9:25–26, where the Hosea texts are explicitly cited of the gentiles' inclusion). Whether 1 Peter intends the readers as gentiles brought in, or as the (Jewish-and-gentile) renewed people, is not specified; the TT renders the then/now reversal as 1 Peter gives it and flags the application (2:10 note; Rule 13). As at 2:9, the letter draws no explicit conclusion about Israel's standing (Rule 3). **[textual — VERIFIED (the source is Hosea 1–2); the application to the readers — CLAIMED; the gentile-vs-renewed-Israel referent — POSSIBLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Hosea 1:9–10; 2:23 → 1 Pet 2:10). Cf. Rom 9:25–26 (the same Hosea texts).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Hosea's sign-children Lo-Ammi ("not my people") and Lo-Ruhamah ("not pitied") dramatize the broken covenant of the northern kingdom; the reversal — "you are my people" / "I will have mercy" (Hos 2:23 [MT 2:25]; 1:10 [MT 2:1]) — is God's promise to **restore Israel** to covenant after judgment, a word of hope for the people who had become "not a people." It is read of Israel's own restoration; its application to a church as the new "people of God" is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Hosea 1:10; 2:23 are read as the prophetic ground for the inclusion of those who were "not a people" — in Paul (Rom 9:25–26) explicitly of the gentiles — into the people of God through the Messiah; 1 Peter 2:10 is treated as the apostolic application of Hosea's reversal to the readers' new identity in mercy. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam affirms God's mercy (*raḥma*) as central — every chapter but one opens "in the name of God, the Most Merciful, the Most Compassionate" — and that God turns to and restores those who turn to him; the specific Hosea "not my people → my people" reversal and its application to a Christian community are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Isaiah 53:9, 4–6, 12 — the Servant woven through (1 Peter 2:22–25)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 2:22–25
**Text says:** "who committed no sin, nor was deceit found in his mouth; who, being reviled, did not revile in return; suffering, he did not threaten, but handed *himself* over to the *One* judging justly; who himself bore up our sins in his body on the tree, so that, having died to sins, we might live to righteousness; by whose wound you were healed. For you were straying like sheep, but now you returned to the shepherd and overseer of your souls."
**Context:** The ground of the household-code's first panel (2:18–25): household servants are to endure unjust suffering because the Messiah suffered unjustly, leaving them a *hypogrammos* ("writing-copy to trace," 2:21). To fill out that example, the letter **weaves** the fourth Servant Song through four verses — the densest sustained use of Isaiah 53 in the New Testament. The Servant-portrait is the prophet's voice *about* the Servant (third person), so it is **not** divine-marked (Rule 30).
**Subject:** The Servant of Isaiah 53 as the pattern of innocent, sin-bearing suffering. 1 Peter threads multiple lines of **Isaiah 53** (LXX) into the passage, none formula-cited:
- **2:22** "who committed no sin, nor was deceit found in his mouth" ← **Isaiah 53:9** ("he had done no lawlessness, nor was deceit found in his mouth," LXX *oude heurethē dolos en tō stomati autou* — reproduced near-verbatim).
- **2:23** "being reviled, did not revile in return… handed *himself* over" ← the **silence** of the Servant, **Isaiah 53:7** ("like a lamb… he did not open his mouth"), recast as non-retaliation and entrusting judgment to God.
- **2:24** "who himself bore up our sins in his body… by whose wound you were healed" ← **Isaiah 53:4, 12** ("he bears our sins," "he bore the sins of many," LXX *anenenken*) and **Isaiah 53:5** ("by his bruise (*mōlōps*) we were healed" → "by whose wound (*mōlōps*) you were healed" — Isaiah's "we" turned to "you").
- **2:25** "you were straying like sheep, but now you returned" ← **Isaiah 53:6** ("all we like sheep went astray," LXX *planēthēmen*), with the "return to the shepherd and overseer" added (Ezek 34 shepherd-imagery in the background).

The Greek vocabulary tracks the LXX of Isaiah 53 closely (*hamartian ouk epoiēsen / dolos / anēnenken / mōlōps / planōmenoi*). This is the **same Servant** read in Acts 3 (the *pais* "glorified," Isa 52:13–53:12 → Acts 3:13, 26) — there an echo in a sermon, here woven into exhortation. The "on the **tree**" of 2:24 (*epi to xylon*) carries Deuteronomy 21:23 (treated separately below).
**Fulfillment status:** PARTIAL *(mode: typological echo / sustained allusion)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **sustained allusion / woven echo**, not a formula citation — 1 Peter says no "it is written," names no source, and quotes no continuous block; instead it **interweaves** the diction of Isaiah 53 (the sinless mouth, the silence, the sin-bearing, the healing bruise, the straying sheep) into its own portrait of the suffering Messiah as the servants' model. The connection is deliberate and lexically anchored (especially 2:22 ≈ Isa 53:9 verbatim, and the rare *mōlōps* "bruise/wound" of 2:24 ≈ Isa 53:5) — the most concentrated NT use of the fourth Servant Song. The PARTIAL label marks this as typological application (the Servant-pattern read as realized in the Messiah and held out as the model for the readers), not an explicit quotation and not a fulfilled prediction. The central crux — flagged not resolved (Rule 13) — is the **vicarious, sin-bearing reading**: 1 Peter reads "he bore *our* sins… by his wound *you* were healed" as the innocent Servant suffering in the place of the guilty, a reading the Jewish and Christian traditions divide over (the Readings below). The TT renders the woven Isaiah-53 language as 1 Peter gives it and flags the interpretive divide (2:22–25 notes). **[textual — PROBABLE (sustained allusion; 2:22 ≈ Isa 53:9 near-verbatim); the vicarious sin-bearing reading — CLAIMED; the Servant's identity (Israel / remnant / individual / Messiah) — POSSIBLE/DEBATED]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Isa 53:4–6, 9, 12 → 1 Pet 2:22–25). For the same Servant in the apostolic preaching, see `content/en/acts/study/CHAPTER-3-PROPHECY.md` (Isa 52:13–53:12 → Acts 3:13, 26).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Isaiah 52:13–53:12 is the fourth Servant Song; in the dominant Jewish reading the Servant is **collective Israel** (or a faithful remnant) — innocent in the eyes of the nations, suffering among them and ultimately vindicated — and in some traditions an individual righteous sufferer; the "he bore our sins… by his bruise we were healed" is read of the nations' astonished recognition of Israel's vicarious-seeming suffering, or of the righteous one's affliction, **not** as a Messiah's substitutionary atonement. The application to a crucified-and-raised Messiah dying for sins is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Isaiah 53 is read as the foremost prophecy of the suffering Messiah, fulfilled in Yeshua — the sinless one who "bore our sins in his body," whose "wound" heals, the straying sheep returned to the shepherd; 1 Peter 2:22–25 is treated as the apostolic, vicarious-atonement reading of the Servant Song, the Messiah suffering innocently in the place of the guilty as both substitute and *hypogrammos* (example, 2:21). [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam honors Isa (Jesus) as Messiah and prophet but rejects the crucifixion as a real atoning death (Surah 4:157, "they did not kill him… but it was made to appear so"); a sinless Servant bearing the sins of others is therefore not part of the Islamic reading, and Isaiah 53 is not engaged as a substitutionary-atonement prophecy. Islam affirms that no soul bears the burden of another (Surah 6:164), which stands directly against a vicarious sin-bearing. [DOCUMENTED]

## Deuteronomy 21:23 — "on a tree" (*xylon*) (1 Peter 2:24)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 2:24
**Text says:** "who himself bore up our sins in his body on the tree, so that, having died to sins, we might live to righteousness; by whose wound you were healed."
**Context:** The climax of the Isaiah-53 weave (2:24): the Servant who "bore our sins" did so "in his body **on the tree**" — naming the cross with the word *xylon* ("wood/tree"). The "tree" is an **echo** of the law of the hanged criminal; no verse is cited.
**Subject:** The "tree" of the executed body. **ἐπὶ τὸ ξύλον** (*epi to xylon*, "on the tree/wood") names the cross with the LXX term *xylon* — the same word used in **Deuteronomy 21:22–23** for the body of the executed criminal "hung on a tree (*epi xylou*)," who "is cursed by God" and must be taken down by nightfall lest the land be defiled. The early proclamation repeatedly calls the cross "the tree" (*xylon*) — Acts 5:30; 10:39; 13:29; and Paul cites Deut 21:23 explicitly (Gal 3:13, "cursed is everyone hanged on a tree"). 1 Peter does **not** cite Deuteronomy or develop a curse-theology; it uses *xylon* as the vivid, scripture-colored word for the cross on which the Servant "bore our sins." The choice of *xylon* (over *stauros*, "cross") quietly draws the crucifixion into the field of the Deuteronomic "hanged on a tree."
**Fulfillment status:** DEBATED *(mode: echo)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion / echo**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse, names no source, and builds no argument from Deuteronomy 21:23 (unlike Paul at Gal 3:13); it simply **uses the word** *xylon* ("tree/wood") for the cross, a usage that carries the Deuteronomic "hanged on a tree" coloring and is standard in the early proclamation (Acts 5:30; 10:39; 13:29). The connection is lexical and atmospheric, not asserted: 1 Peter does not invoke the "cursed by God" clause or read the Messiah's death through the curse-of-the-law (that is Paul's move, Gal 3:13). The DEBATED label marks this as an echo — a scripture-colored word borrowed — carrying no explicit citation and no fulfillment-claim (Rule 13). **[comparative parallel — POSSIBLE; a lexical echo (*xylon*), no cited verse]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Deut 21:23 → 1 Pet 2:24). For the explicit curse-of-the-tree argument, cf. Gal 3:13; for "the tree" in the apostolic preaching, cf. Acts 5:30; 10:39.
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Deuteronomy 21:22–23 commands that the body of an executed criminal hung "on a tree" be taken down by nightfall and not left overnight, "for a hanged man is cursed by God, and you shall not defile your land"; the law concerns the dishonor and ritual defilement of the exposed corpse. It is read within Israel's law of execution and the holiness of the land; the application of "hanged on a tree" to the Messiah's cross — and any curse-bearing reading of it — is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: 1 Peter's "on the tree" (*xylon*) is read as the scripture-colored naming of the cross, the place where the Messiah "bore our sins"; the wider tradition (Paul, Gal 3:13) reads Deut 21:23 as the Messiah becoming "a curse" in the place of the guilty, though 1 Peter itself does not develop the curse-theme — its "tree" simply locates the sin-bearing of the Servant on the cross. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam rejects the crucifixion as a real event (Surah 4:157) and so does not read the cross-as-tree at all; the Deuteronomic "hanged on a tree" and its application to the Messiah's death are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]
