## Note on 1 Peter's mode of allusion (an epistle, not a narrative)

1 Peter is a **letter** — *argument and exhortation* in the apostle's own voice, not a narrative of events fulfilling prediction. So its use of the Hebrew Bible is not "prophecy → fulfillment" in the gospel-and-Acts sense. The letter **deploys** the Hebrew Bible *paraenetically and typologically*: it **quotes** it to ground a command (the wisdom-maxims of Proverbs — "love covers a multitude of sins," 4:8; "if the righteous is scarcely saved," 4:18), it **applies type-scenes** of Israel's worship and judgment to the readers (the judgment that begins at the sanctuary, Ezek 9 / Mal 3, deployed at 4:17), and it **echoes** prophetic and creation diction onto its persecuted, scattered audience (the resting spirit of Isa 11:2 at 4:14; the faithful Creator of Genesis 1 / Isaiah 40–45 at 4:19). The dual-label and citation-vs-allusion system carries over unchanged from the gospel companions; what shifts is how to read the **status line**. Here "fulfillment status" should be read as **mode of deployment**:

- **CLAIMED** ≈ **explicit quotation** — the letter cites the OT (the Proverbs maxims of 4:8, 18) and presses it as authoritative for the readers.
- **PARTIAL** ≈ **typological application** — the letter reads an Israel scene (the judgment beginning at God's house) as the pattern realized in the readers' present trial, without a formula citation.
- **DEBATED** ≈ **echo** — the diction of an OT scene (the resting spirit; the faithful Creator) is interiorized into the exhortation, without citation or fulfillment-claim.

**Speech-marking (Rule 30 / A-004) in this chapter is empty.** Chapter 4 carries **no narrated divine speech and no divine-marked saying** (`@@…@@`). The two formal quotations are both from **Proverbs** — the **sage's** human voice, not God's first-person word — so neither is divine-marked; the echoes (Isa 11:2; Gen 1 / Isa 40–45) are the letter's own diction and likewise unmarked. Where κύριος in a *source* verse stands for YHWH, the entries note it per **Option C** (the main text renders "the Lord" + Tier-2; the source-referent is YHWH; P-006 / § κύριος Option C in `1-peter.md`); in 1 Peter 4 the κύριος-referent surfaces only behind the Malachi "Lord coming to his temple" echo (4:17).

## Proverbs 10:12 — "love covers a multitude of sins" (1 Peter 4:8)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 4:8
**Text says:** "above all *things*, having earnest love among yourselves, because love covers a multitude of sins;"
**Context:** The heart of the end-time ethics (4:7–11): because "the end of all things has drawn near" (4:7), the community is summoned to sobriety, fervent mutual love, hospitality, and gift-stewardship. The love-command is grounded with a wisdom-maxim — love "covers a multitude of sins" — set "above all things" (*pro pantōn*) as the community's first priority. The quoted line is the **sage's** human voice (a proverb about love), not God's first-person word, and is therefore **not** divine-marked (Rule 30).
**Subject:** Love as that which covers sins. 1 Peter quotes **Proverbs 10:12** — Hebrew MT: "hatred stirs up strifes, but love **covers** (*tĕkhasseh*) all transgressions (*kol-pĕshaʿîm*)." The Greek **ἀγάπη καλύπτει πλῆθος ἁμαρτιῶν** (*agapē kalyptei plēthos hamartiōn*, "love covers a multitude of sins") follows the **Hebrew sense**, not the LXX wording: the **LXX of Prov 10:12b** reads quite differently — "but upon all who do not love strife, friendship covers" (*pantas de tous mē philoneikountas kalyptei philia*), which lacks the "multitude of sins" object. 1 Peter's phrasing ("covers a **multitude of sins**," *plēthos hamartiōn*) renders the **MT's** "covers all transgressions" directly — independent of the divergent LXX — and matches the same proverb's reuse in James 5:20 ("covers a multitude of sins"). The "covering" is the proverb's own image of love that does not expose or multiply wrongs; 1 Peter applies it to the mutual love that holds a pressured community together before the end.
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED *(mode: explicit quotation)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is a **quotation/application** of a wisdom-maxim — 1 Peter introduces it without a formula ("because [*hoti*] love covers…"), folding the proverb into the exhortation as a known saying rather than a cited proof-text. The deployment is **paraenetic**, not predictive: 1 Peter does not say a prophecy was fulfilled; it **grounds the love-command** in the proverb. The notable textual feature — flagged in the main text (4:8 note) — is that 1 Peter follows the **Hebrew sense of Prov 10:12 ("covers all transgressions"), not the LXX**, whose Greek diverges entirely from "a multitude of sins"; the TT renders the citation as 1 Peter gives it and flags the Hebrew-not-LXX source (Rule 13). Because it is the **sage's** voice (a proverb), it carries **no** `@@…@@` marking (Rule 30). The interpretive question of *whose* sins love covers (the sins of others, forgiven and not multiplied; or one's own, by love's practice) is left open as the proverb leaves it. **[textual — VERIFIED (quotation; Hebrew sense, not LXX); the referent of the "covered" sins — POSSIBLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Prov 10:12 → 1 Pet 4:8) in `content/en/1-peter/CHAPTER-4.md`.
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Proverbs 10:12 is a wisdom-saying in the antithetic style of the Solomonic collection — hatred stirs up quarrels, but love "covers" (overlooks, does not broadcast) all transgressions; in the rabbinic tradition love and the overlooking of others' faults are prized (cf. the value placed on not shaming and on covering another's failing). It is read within Israel's wisdom on human relations; its application as a maxim for a Christian community's mutual love is 1 Peter's deployment. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The verse is read as the scriptural ground for forgiving, non-exposing love within the church — love that does not parade or multiply others' sins; 1 Peter 4:8 (with James 5:20) is treated as the apostolic application of the proverb, and "covering" is read of love's restraint (and, in some traditions, of the sins that love's intercession leads to being forgiven). [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam strongly commends concealing the faults of others (*satr*) — covering a believer's fault is itself meritorious in the prophetic tradition — and prizes love and reconciliation; the specific Proverbs 10:12 wording and its application to a church's mutual love are not part of the Quranic presentation, though the underlying ethic of covering faults is shared ground. [DOCUMENTED]

## Isaiah 11:2 — "the spirit of glory and of God rests upon you" (1 Peter 4:14)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 4:14
**Text says:** "If you are reviled in *the* name of *the* Messiah, *you are* blessed, because the {a:wind/spirit} of glory and of God rests upon you."
**Context:** The beatitude at the center of the suffering-section (4:12–19): the readers are not to be surprised at the "burning trial" (4:12) but to rejoice as sharers in the Messiah's sufferings (4:13); and being reviled "in the name of the Messiah" is declared a blessing — "because the {a:wind/spirit} of glory and of God **rests upon** you." The resting-spirit language is an **echo** of Isaiah's messianic oracle, reapplied; no verse is cited.
**Subject:** The spirit of YHWH resting, reapplied to the persecuted. **τὸ τῆς δόξης καὶ τὸ τοῦ θεοῦ πνεῦμα… ἀναπαύεται** (*to tēs doxēs kai to tou theou pneuma… anapauetai*, "the {a:wind/spirit} of glory and of God rests") echoes **Isaiah 11:2** — "and the **spirit** of YHWH shall **rest upon** him (*wĕnāchâ ʿālāyw rûach YHWH*), the spirit of wisdom and understanding…" — the sevenfold spirit settling on the messianic shoot from the stump of Yishai (Jesse). The Greek verb *anapauomai* ("rest upon") matches the LXX of Isa 11:2 (*anapausetai epʼ auton pneuma tou theou*). 1 Peter takes the spirit that, in Isaiah, **rests on the Messiah** and declares it now **rests on the reviled believers** — those who suffer "in the name of the Messiah" share in the resting of his own spirit (the {a:wind/spirit} slash kept; *pneuma* could be the divine Spirit or the spirit of glory resting, and the TT keeps the openness). The phrase "of glory and of God" is grammatically dense (two articles, one *pneuma*); the TT renders it without resolving whether one spirit or two aspects are in view (4:14 note).
**Fulfillment status:** DEBATED *(mode: echo)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion / echo**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse and uses no formula; the "rests upon (you)" diction (*anapauetai*) recognizably recalls Isaiah 11:2's spirit "resting" on the messianic figure, here **transferred** to the persecuted community. The connection is deployment, not prediction: the letter does not say Isaiah 11 was fulfilled; it **echoes** the resting-spirit and reapplies it as the believers' present honor under reviling, consonant with the share-in-the-Messiah's-sufferings logic of 4:13. The DEBATED label marks this as an echo — diction borrowed and applied — carrying no fulfillment-claim and no explicit citation (Rule 13); the strength of the Isa-11:2 allusion (versus a generic "spirit resting" idiom) and the one-spirit-or-two reading of "of glory and of God" are both held open. The {a:wind/spirit} slash is retained per the locked GS convention. **[comparative parallel — PROBABLE (echo, not citation); the one-spirit-vs-two-aspects reading — POSSIBLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Isa 11:2 → 1 Pet 4:14). For the same Isaianic spirit-anointing tradition, cf. the gospel baptismal-descent companions (Isa 11:2 / 42:1 → the spirit on the Messiah).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Isaiah 11:2 is read as the endowment of the ideal Davidic king — the "shoot from the stump of Yishai" (Jesse) on whom the spirit of YHWH rests, the spirit of wisdom, counsel, might, knowledge, and the fear of YHWH; it is a vision of righteous, spirit-empowered rule, a live messianic hope. Its application to persecuted believers as the resting-place of that spirit is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Isaiah 11:2 is read as fulfilled in the Messiah on whom the Spirit rests (cf. the baptismal descent), and 1 Peter 4:14 as the apostolic extension of that resting spirit to those who suffer in his name — the "spirit of glory" resting on the reviled as a present sign of their blessedness. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam affirms God's strengthening of his prophets and the righteous (cf. the support of the "holy spirit," *rūḥ al-qudus*, given to Isa, Surah 2:87, 253); the specific Isaiah-11 resting-spirit oracle and its application to persecuted believers are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Ezekiel 9:6 — "judgment begins from the house of God" (1 Peter 4:17)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 4:17
**Text says:** "Because *it is* the time for the judgment to begin from the house of God; and if first from us, what *is* the end of those disobeying the good news of God?"
**Context:** The interpretive key to the whole suffering-section (4:17–19): the readers' present "burning trial" is reframed as the **judgment beginning at God's household** — and if it begins "first from us," the fate of the disobedient is far worse (the lesser-to-greater argument continued in 4:18). The "house of God" / judgment-beginning language is an **echo/typology** of Ezekiel's temple vision; no verse is cited.
**Subject:** Judgment beginning at the sanctuary. **ἄρξασθαι τὸ κρίμα ἀπὸ τοῦ οἴκου τοῦ θεοῦ** (*arxasthai to krima apo tou oikou tou theou*, "the judgment to begin from the house of God") echoes **Ezekiel 9:6** — in the vision of the slayers sent through Jerusalem, YHWH commands "**begin from my sanctuary**" (MT *ûmimmiqdāšî tāchēllû*; LXX *kai apo tōn hagiōn mou arxasthe*), and "they began from the elders who were before the house." The principle — that judgment falls **first** on God's own house/sanctuary before reaching the wider populace — is the pattern 1 Peter deploys: the believers (the "house of God," tied to the "spiritual house" of 2:5) undergo the purifying judgment first; "those disobeying the good news" face its end. The Greek "begin from" (*arxasthai apo*) tracks the Ezekiel command's "begin from [my sanctuary]."
**Fulfillment status:** PARTIAL *(mode: typological application)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **typology / echo**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse and names no source; it **deploys** the Ezekiel-9 principle (judgment beginning at the sanctuary) as the pattern realized in the readers' present trial. The connection is conceptually and lexically anchored: the "begin from the house of God" structure mirrors Ezekiel's "begin from my sanctuary" / "began from… before the house," and 1 Peter's "house of God" gathers the letter's own "spiritual house" temple-imagery (2:5). The PARTIAL label marks this as typological application (the Ezekiel pattern read as enacted in the believers' suffering), not an explicit quotation and not a fulfilled prediction (Rule 13). This entry overlaps with the Malachi 3 echo below — both feed the single "judgment begins at God's household" move of 4:17; the relative weight of Ezekiel 9 versus Malachi 3 behind the verse is not resolved here. **[comparative parallel — PROBABLE (typology, not citation); Ezek 9 vs. the broader judgment-begins-at-the-sanctuary tradition — POSSIBLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ezek 9:6 → 1 Pet 4:17). Linked to the "spiritual house" of 2:5 (the believers as temple).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Ezekiel 9 is read within the temple-judgment visions of chapters 8–11 — the glory departing the defiled sanctuary, the marking of the faithful (the *taw* on the foreheads), and the slaughter that "begins from my sanctuary," from the elders before the house: God's judgment falling first and most severely on his own polluted house and its leaders. It belongs to the prophecy of Jerusalem's fall; its application to a Christian community as the "house of God" undergoing purifying judgment is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Ezekiel 9:6 is read as establishing the principle that God's judgment begins with his own people, and 1 Peter 4:17 as its application to the church — the present suffering as the refining judgment that starts at God's household (the "spiritual house," 2:5), with a graver reckoning awaiting those who disobey the gospel. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam affirms that God's judgment is just and that the people of revelation are held to account for their faithfulness; the specific Ezekiel-9 temple-judgment vision and its application to a Christian "house of God" are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Malachi 3:1–5 — the Lord coming to refine his temple (1 Peter 4:17)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 4:17
**Text says:** "Because *it is* the time for the judgment to begin from the house of God; and if first from us, what *is* the end of those disobeying the good news of God?"
**Context:** The same verse (4:17). Behind the "judgment beginning at God's house" lies not only Ezekiel's slaying-vision but also the **refining**-judgment of Malachi — the Lord coming suddenly to his temple to purify. The diction is an **echo**; no verse is cited. (This entry sits alongside the Ezekiel 9:6 entry above; both stand behind the one verse.)
**Subject:** The purifying judgment at God's household. The "time for the judgment to begin from the house of God" also echoes **Malachi 3:1–5** — "the Lord (*hāʾādôn* / κύριος) whom you seek will **suddenly come to his temple**… But who can endure the day of his coming?… for he is like a **refiner's fire** (*kĕʾēš mĕṣārēp̄*)… he will purify the sons of Levi" — the coming of the Lord *to his own sanctuary first*, as a refining and purging judgment that begins among the priesthood before it reaches the wider wrongdoers (the sorcerers, adulterers, false-swearers, oppressors of 3:5). In Malachi, "the Lord" (κύριος in the LXX, *hāʾādôn*/the messenger-of-the-covenant in the MT) coming to his temple = the divine visitation; the referent stands behind 1 Peter's "house of God" judgment (Option C: the source-referent is YHWH / the Lord coming, though 1 Peter does not quote and names no κύριος here). The Malachi pattern reinforces the Ezekiel one: judgment that **starts with God's own house** and refines it.
**Fulfillment status:** DEBATED *(mode: echo)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion / echo**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse and uses no formula; the "judgment beginning at God's house" carries the **refining-temple-visitation** motif of Malachi 3 alongside the Ezekiel-9 sanctuary principle. The connection is thematic rather than verbal: 1 Peter shares no distinctive Malachi *wording* (unlike the closer Ezekiel "begin from" structure), so this is the **weaker** of the two echoes behind 4:17 — included because the chapter's "burning trial" (4:12, *pyrōsis*) as a **refining** ordeal (cf. the gold-tried-by-fire of 1:7) resonates strongly with Malachi's "refiner's fire" purifying God's house. On the divine name: the "Lord coming to his temple" of Malachi is YHWH / the Lord's visitation (Option C); 1 Peter neither quotes nor uses κύριος here, so the referent is background, not a rendered name. The DEBATED label marks this as an echo — a motif gathered into the verse — carrying no fulfillment-claim and no citation (Rule 13). **[comparative parallel — POSSIBLE; a refining-temple motif, no distinctive wording]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Mal 3:1–5 → 1 Pet 4:17). For Malachi's "messenger" and refining coming in the gospel-Acts tradition, cf. the Lukan/Markan companions (Mal 3:1 → the forerunner).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Malachi 3:1–5 is read as the promise of the Lord's coming to his temple, preceded by his messenger, as a day of refining and purifying judgment — the refiner's fire purging the sons of Levi and the fuller's soap cleansing, then judgment on the sorcerers, adulterers, and oppressors; it is a live expectation of divine visitation and purification of worship. Its application to a Christian community's present suffering as that refining judgment is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Malachi 3:1–5 is read as the coming of the Lord to refine and judge, beginning with his own; 1 Peter 4:17 is treated as reflecting that refining-judgment-at-God's-house pattern, the believers' "burning trial" (4:12) as the purifying fire that starts with God's household before the reckoning of the disobedient. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam affirms God's just judgment and the purification of the faithful through trial; the specific Malachi temple-refining oracle and its application to a Christian household are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Proverbs 11:31 (LXX) — "if the righteous is scarcely saved…" (1 Peter 4:18)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 4:18
**Text says:** "And if the righteous *is* scarcely saved, where will the ungodly and *the* sinner appear?"
**Context:** The continuation of the judgment-at-God's-house argument (4:17–18): having said judgment begins "first from us," 1 Peter clinches the lesser-to-greater logic with a quoted proverb — if even the **righteous** are "scarcely saved," what of the ungodly and the sinner? The quoted line is the **sage's** human voice (a wisdom-saying), not God's first-person word, and is therefore **not** divine-marked (Rule 30).
**Subject:** The righteous barely saved versus the fate of the ungodly. 1 Peter quotes **Proverbs 11:31 — in its LXX form** — almost verbatim: **εἰ ὁ μὲν δίκαιος μόλις σῴζεται, ὁ ἀσεβὴς καὶ ἁμαρτωλὸς ποῦ φανεῖται** ("if the righteous is **scarcely saved** [*molis sōzetai*], where will the ungodly and the sinner appear?"). This is **decisively the LXX, not the Hebrew MT**: the MT of Proverbs 11:31 reads "Behold, the righteous is **recompensed** (*yĕšullām*) on the earth (*bāʾāreṣ*); how much more the wicked and the sinner!" — a statement about *requital in this life*. The LXX translators evidently read or rendered the Hebrew *yĕšullām bāʾāreṣ* ("repaid on earth") as *molis sōzetai* ("scarcely / with difficulty is saved") — a markedly different sense (a soteriological "barely rescued" rather than a this-worldly "repaid"), and turned the MT's flat "how much more" into the rhetorical "**where** will [they] **appear**?" 1 Peter follows this **LXX reading entirely** — the "scarcely saved" sense exists *only* in the Greek; the proverb's MT does not support 1 Peter's wording. The TT renders the citation as 1 Peter gives it (the LXX) and flags the LXX/MT divergence (4:18 note; Rule 13).
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED *(mode: explicit quotation)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is a **near-verbatim quotation** of **LXX Proverbs 11:31**, introduced without a formula ("And if [*kai ei*] the righteous…"), folded into the argument as a known saying. The deployment is **paraenetic-and-hortatory**, not predictive: 1 Peter does not claim a prophecy was fulfilled; it **applies** the proverb's lesser-to-greater logic to drive home 4:17 — if the judgment that begins at God's house leaves even the righteous "scarcely saved," the disobedient face far worse. **The key textual point — flagged in the main text and here — is the LXX/MT divergence:** the **"scarcely saved" reading is LXX-specific**; the **Hebrew MT** reads "the righteous is **repaid on the earth**," a this-worldly requital, *not* a "barely rescued" soteriology. 1 Peter quotes the **Greek**, and the TT renders that, noting that the proverb's Hebrew says something materially different (Rule 13). Because it is the **sage's** voice (a proverb), it carries **no** `@@…@@` marking (Rule 30 — the chapter's speech-marking note confirms this is unmarked). **[textual — VERIFIED (quotation, LXX form); the LXX-vs-MT divergence (MT "repaid on earth" ≠ LXX "scarcely saved") — VERIFIED]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Prov 11:31 LXX → 1 Pet 4:18) in `content/en/1-peter/CHAPTER-4.md`.
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Proverbs 11:31 in the **Hebrew (MT)** reads "the righteous is **recompensed on the earth** — how much more the wicked and the sinner": a wisdom-saying that even the righteous receive their due requital in this life (and so, *a fortiori*, the wicked will not escape) — read within the Proverbs theme of just recompense in the present world. The "scarcely saved" sense of 1 Peter's quotation is the **Greek (LXX)** rendering, not the Hebrew; the Jewish reading follows the MT's requital-on-earth meaning. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: 1 Peter 4:18 is read (following the LXX it quotes) as teaching the difficulty even of the righteous being saved — and so, *a fortiori*, the graver peril of the ungodly and the sinner — reinforcing the call to faithful endurance under judgment that begins at God's house; the verse is a standard text on the costliness of salvation and the seriousness of final judgment. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam teaches that the righteous are saved by God's mercy and the wicked face a severe reckoning, and that even the devout depend on God's grace; the specific Proverbs 11:31 saying (in either its Hebrew or Greek form) is not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Genesis 1; Isaiah 40–45 — "a faithful Creator" (1 Peter 4:19)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 4:19
**Text says:** "So then, *let* also those suffering according to *the* will of God entrust their souls to a faithful Creator in doing good."
**Context:** The resolution of the suffering-section (4:19): those who suffer "according to the will of God" are to **entrust their souls** to "a faithful Creator" while "doing good." The title "Creator" (*ktistēs*, only here in the GS) grounds endurance in God's creative faithfulness. The phrase is an **echo** of the creation-faithfulness theme of the Hebrew Bible; no verse is cited.
**Subject:** God the faithful Creator. **πιστῷ κτίστῃ** (*pistō ktistē*, "to a faithful Creator") reaches back to the foundational claim that God **created** (Hebrew *bārāʾ*; LXX *epoiēsen / ektisen*) the heavens and the earth — **Genesis 1:1** and the whole creation account — and to the prophetic consolation that ties God's **creative power** to his **covenant faithfulness**, paradigmatically **Isaiah 40–45**: "the everlasting God, YHWH, the **Creator** (*bôrēʾ*) of the ends of the earth, does not faint or grow weary" (Isa 40:28); the repeated self-identification "I am YHWH, who **made** all things… your Creator" (cf. Isa 42:5; 43:1, 15; 44:24; 45:7, 12, 18) — the Creator who, *because* he made and sustains the world, can be trusted to keep his people. 1 Peter joins the noun *ktistēs* ("Creator") to *pistos* ("faithful"): the God who creates is the God who keeps faith, and so the suffering can safely "entrust" (*paratithēmi*, deposit) their souls to him. The verb *paratithēmi* also echoes the Messiah's dying word, "into your hands I entrust my spirit" (Lk 23:46 / Ps 31:5).
**Fulfillment status:** DEBATED *(mode: echo)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion / echo**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse and names no source; it **deploys** the creation-faithfulness theme (God as Creator, therefore trustworthy) as the ground of entrusting the soul. The connection is thematic rather than verbal: "faithful Creator" gathers the Genesis-1 creation claim and the Isaiah-40–45 linkage of *creative power* to *covenant fidelity* (the Creator "who does not faint," who "formed" and "will carry" his people) — but 1 Peter coins its own compact title (*pistos ktistēs*) rather than quoting any one verse. The DEBATED label marks this as an echo — a theme gathered into a title — carrying no fulfillment-claim and no citation (Rule 13); which strand is foremost (Genesis-1 creation vs. the Isaiah Creator-redeemer) is not specified and is not resolved here. The *paratithēmi* ("entrust") verb additionally carries the Psalm-31 / Luke-23:46 deposit-of-the-soul resonance, noted in the main text (4:19 note). **[comparative parallel — PROBABLE (echo, not citation); the foremost strand (Gen 1 vs. Isa 40–45) — POSSIBLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Gen 1; Isa 40–45 → 1 Pet 4:19). The "entrust the soul" verb echoes the gospel passion (Lk 23:46 / Ps 31:5).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: God as **Creator** (*bôrēʾ*) of heaven and earth is foundational — Genesis 1 and the Isaian prophecies of comfort, where YHWH's creative power (the "Creator of the ends of the earth" who does not weary, Isa 40:28) grounds Israel's confidence that he will keep his covenant and restore his people. Entrusting oneself to the faithful Creator is consonant with the trust in God the maker and sustainer expressed throughout the Psalms and Prophets; the specific 1 Peter title and its application to suffering believers is the letter's own deployment. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: "A faithful Creator" is read as grounding Christian endurance under suffering in God's creative faithfulness — the Maker who keeps his word can be trusted with the soul (cf. the *paratithēmi* of Lk 23:46); 1 Peter 4:19 is treated as the apostolic application of the Creator-faithfulness theme (Genesis 1; Isaiah 40–45) to the persecuted. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam centrally affirms God as **al-Khāliq**, the Creator (and al-Bāriʾ, the Maker), faithful to his promise (cf. the divine names; the frequent Quranic insistence that God created the heavens and the earth and never breaks his word); entrusting oneself to God (*tawakkul*) in trial is a core devotional posture. The specific "faithful Creator" phrase of 1 Peter and its Genesis-1 / Isaiah-40–45 backdrop are not part of the Quranic presentation, though the underlying conviction (the trustworthy Creator) is shared ground. [DOCUMENTED]
