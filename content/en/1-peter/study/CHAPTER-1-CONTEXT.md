## HOW TO USE THIS COMPANION

This file contains background material organized by topic. Each entry carries **two labels:**

**What kind of claim?** (tells you the source of the information)
- **TEXTUAL** — derived directly from the Greek text
- **STRONG INFERENCE** — logically entailed by the text, not stated explicitly
- **POSSIBLE INFERENCE** — one legitimate reading among others
- **COMPARATIVE PARALLEL** — attested in another ancient source
- **LATER RECEPTION** — post-biblical reading
- **HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL** — based on material or historical evidence
- **SPECULATION** — no strong textual or evidential basis

**How certain?** (tells you the confidence level)
- **VERIFIED** — strong evidence, wide agreement
- **PROBABLE** — well-supported but debated
- **POSSIBLE** — one reasonable reading among others
- **UNCERTAIN** — genuinely unclear
- **SPECULATIVE** — must be rare and clearly flagged
- **DOCUMENTED** — historically attested reception (for LATER RECEPTION entries)

**Reading principle:** Start with TEXTUAL entries — those come from the Greek itself. Then explore outward to parallels, reception, and comparison. The companion presents; you assess.

## A. Source Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. The epistolary salutation — sender, recipients, greeting
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

1 Peter opens in the fixed form of the ancient letter: sender in the nominative (**Πέτρος ἀπόστολος Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ**, *Petros apostolos Iēsou Christou*, "Kefa [Peter], an apostle of Yeshua *the* Messiah"), recipients in the dative (**ἐκλεκτοῖς παρεπιδήμοις διασπορᾶς**, *eklektois parepidēmois diasporas*, "to *the* elect sojourners of *the* dispersion"), and a greeting (1:2). The TT preserves the case-structure that carries the form. Unlike the Greco-Roman *chairein* ("greetings"), the greeting here is **χάρις… καὶ εἰρήνη πληθυνθείη** ("may grace and peace be multiplied"), an optative health-wish that fuses the Greek *charis* with the Hebrew *shalom* ("peace") — a recognizably Jewish-Christian reshaping of the convention (cf. Rom 1:7; 1 Cor 1:3). The verb *plēthynthēnai* ("be multiplied") echoes the salutation of Daniel 4:1 / 6:25 LXX, where the same word greets a dispersed people.

### A2. The triune-shaped election, rendered without later dogma (Rule 3)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the three phrases); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the relations among them)]**

The election of 1:2 is governed by three prepositional phrases naming Father, {a:wind/spirit}, and the blood of Yeshua: **κατὰ πρόγνωσιν θεοῦ πατρός** ("according to *the* foreknowledge of God *the* Father"), **ἐν ἁγιασμῷ πνεύματος** ("in sanctification of *the* {a:wind/spirit}"), **εἰς ὑπακοὴν καὶ ῥαντισμὸν αἵματος Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ** ("for obedience and sprinkling of *the* blood of Yeshua *the* Messiah"). The TT renders the Father/spirit/blood structure exactly as the Greek gives it and **does not** import the later vocabulary "the Trinity" or specify the relations among the three (Rule 3; editorial log P-008).

**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN]** Whether the three phrases assign distinct functions (the Father foreknowing, the spirit sanctifying, the blood ratifying) or simply pile up a single act of grace is not settled by the syntax; the prepositions (*kata* / *en* / *eis*) differ, but their force is debated. The TT keeps the threefold shape visible and leaves the systematic question to the reader. → see editorial log P-008.

### A3. "Sprinkling of blood" — the covenant ritual behind the phrase
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexeme); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE (for the Sinai referent)]**

The phrase **ῥαντισμὸν αἵματος** (*rhantismon haimatos*, "sprinkling of blood," 1:2) draws on the blood-sprinkling rituals of the Torah. The noun *rhantismos* and its verb *rhantizō* render the Hebrew *zaraq* / *nazah* ("dash / sprinkle") used at the covenant-ratification of Sinai, where Moshe (Moses) "took the blood and sprinkled *it* on the people" (Exod 24:8), and in the consecration and purification rites (Lev 14; Num 19). **[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE]** that the Sinai covenant-ratification is the primary echo — the readers are constituted a covenant people by the blood — though the consecration and purification senses are also in the field. The TT keeps "sprinkling of *the* blood" rather than paraphrasing ("cleansing by Christ's death"), so the ritual image stays open.

### A4. *Anagennaō* — "new birth," a near-unique verb
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The blessing of 1:3 turns on **ἀναγεννήσας** (*anagennēsas*, "having begotten again / given new birth"), a compound of *ana* ("again / anew / from above") + *gennaō* ("beget, give birth"). The verb occurs in the entire Greek New Testament only here and at 1:23 — both in this chapter — making it a 1 Peter signature. Its sense-range is genuinely triple: "again," "anew," and "from above," and the prefix *ana-* does not decide among them. The TT renders "new birth / born again" and flags the range. The image governs the chapter's frame: the readers are "begotten again into a living hope" (1:3) and "begotten again… through *the* living and abiding word" (1:23), bracketing the central exhortation. The cognate noun-idea overlaps the Johannine "born from above / again" (*gennēthēnai anōthen*, John 3:3-7), but the verb itself is distinct.

### A5. The single periodic sentence of the blessing (1:3-12)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Verses 3-12 are, in Greek, **one continuous sentence** — a periodic *berakah* (blessing) opening with **εὐλογητός** ("Blessed *be*") and running, through a chain of relative clauses and participles, to "things angels long to look into" (1:12). The TT preserves the suspended, subordinated structure rather than breaking it into short modern sentences, so the reader feels the single breath of the blessing. The form imitates the Hebrew *barukh* benediction ("Blessed be YHWH, who…") and the Greek *eulogētos* benedictions of Second Temple Judaism; the same opening recurs at 2 Corinthians 1:3 and Ephesians 1:3. The piling of clauses is not loose grammar but the genre's own shape — praise that accumulates rather than concludes.

### A6. Three rhyming alpha-privatives — the inheritance "kept in the heavens"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The inheritance of 1:4 is described by three adjectives that all begin with the negating alpha and rhyme in *-on*: **ἄφθαρτον καὶ ἀμίαντον καὶ ἀμάραντον** (*aphtharton kai amianton kai amaranton*) — "imperishable and undefiled and unfading." The sound-pattern is deliberate, a rhetorical triad the TT keeps as a triad rather than smoothing to a single epithet. The noun is **κληρονομία** (*klēronomia*, "inheritance / allotted portion"), the LXX's word for Israel's inherited **land** (Num 26:53-56; Deut 4:21). Where Israel's inheritance was the land — perishable, defilable, and lost in exile — this one is "kept in *the* heavens," set beyond the three liabilities the adjectives name. The TT renders the land-word "inheritance" so the contrast with Israel's *nachalah* stays audible. → see §A8 on the *parepidēmos / paroikia* exile keyword.

### A7. "Tested faith… like gold through fire" — the assay image
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

At 1:7 the **δοκίμιον** (*dokimion*) of the readers' faith — "the testing / the proven-genuineness" — is set against "gold that perishes, *yet* being tested (**δοκιμαζομένου**, *dokimazomenou*) through fire." Both words belong to the metallurgical vocabulary of assaying: *dokimazō* is to test a metal for purity, *dokimion* the test or its result. The image is drawn from the HB wisdom and prophetic tradition — "the crucible is for silver and the furnace for gold, but YHWH tests hearts" (Prov 17:3; cf. Ps 66:10; Zech 13:9; Mal 3:3; Job 23:10; and especially Wisdom 3:6, "like gold in the furnace he tested them"). The point turns on a paradox the TT keeps visible: the perishable thing (gold) is what gets refined by literal fire, yet the faith — which does not perish — is "more precious" and is proven by the fire of trial.

### A8. *Parepidēmos / diaspora / paroikia* — the exile-identity keyword
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The letter's controlling self-designation is a cluster of "stranger" words the TT keeps literal across the chapter. **παρεπίδημος** (*parepidēmos*, 1:1; cf. 2:11) = a "resident foreigner / sojourner / one staying alongside a people not one's own"; **διασπορά** (*diaspora*, 1:1) = the "scattering," the technical term for Israel dispersed among the nations (Deut 28:25 LXX; John 7:35; James 1:1); **παροικία** (*paroikia*, 1:17) = "sojourning / resident-alien status," with its cognate *paroikos* ("resident alien"). The three together cast the readers in Israel's exile identity: chosen yet not at home. The TT keeps "sojourners… dispersion… sojourning" rather than flattening to "Christians scattered abroad," because the social-and-theological force of the keyword — alienation as the frame of the whole letter — depends on the vocabulary staying intact. → see §C2 on the social reality of *paroikoi* in Asia Minor.

### A9. The prophets "searched out" — and "the spirit of the Messiah in them" (1:10-12)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexemes); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the scope of the claim)]**

Verses 10-12 make a dense claim about the HB prophets in three loaded terms. They **ἐξεζήτησαν καὶ ἐξηραύνησαν** (*exezētēsan kai exēraunēsan*, "searched out and investigated"), an emphatic compound doublet — the prophets as inquirers, not merely mouthpieces. What was at work in them is **τὸ ἐν αὐτοῖς πνεῦμα Χριστοῦ** ("the {a:wind/spirit} of *the* Messiah in them"), which was **προμαρτυρόμενον** ("testifying beforehand") to **τὰ εἰς Χριστὸν παθήματα καὶ τὰς μετὰ ταῦτα δόξας** ("the sufferings *destined* for the Messiah and the glories after these"). The TT keeps the wind/spirit slash and renders the claim as the Greek has it.

**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN]** Whether "the *pneuma* of the Messiah in them" asserts the Messiah's pre-existent activity in the prophets, or means "the spirit that pointed to the Messiah," or "the spirit later given by the Messiah," is exegetically contested; the genitive *Christou* is grammatically open. The TT does not resolve it (editorial log; chapter note at 1:11). The suffering-then-glory pattern stated here is the same read of the prophets found at Luke 24:26 and Acts 3:18. → see §B2 on Second Temple ideas of the prophetic spirit.

### A10. "Gird the loins of your mind" — the Exodus posture made interior
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The first main imperative of the letter (1:13) is **ἀναζωσάμενοι τὰς ὀσφύας τῆς διανοίας ὑμῶν** (*anazōsamenoi tas osphyas tēs dianoias hymōn*), literally "having girded up the loins of your mind." The verb is rare (*anazōnnymi*, to tuck up a long robe under the belt for action), and the idiom is the Exodus image of readiness — eating the Passover "with your loins girded" for departure (Exod 12:11; cf. the prophetic "gird your loins" of 1 Kgs 18:46; Jer 1:17). The TT keeps the bodily metaphor literal rather than rendering "prepare your minds," because the letter deliberately relocates a physical Exodus posture *into the mind*. The Passover-departure echo is reinforced by the ransom-by-a-lamb imagery a few verses later (§B1, on 1:18-19).

### A11. The Leviticus citation as God's first-person word (Rule 30)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

At 1:16 the call to holiness is grounded in a citation: **Ἅγιοι ἔσεσθε, ὅτι ἐγὼ ἅγιος** ("Be holy, because I *am* holy"), from Leviticus 11:44-45; 19:2; 20:7. Because the source is **God's own first-person speech** (the LORD speaking "I am holy"), the TT marks it `@@…@@` per Rule 30 (the A-004 precedent; editorial log). This is the only divine-speech marking in the chapter: the surrounding exhortation is the apostle's own voice and is unmarked, and the other citations (Isa 40 at 1:24-25) are the prophet's human voice and are likewise unmarked. The TT thus does not flatten the distinction between Kefa exhorting and God speaking inside the quotation. The copula "*am*" is supplied in italics, since the Greek (and the Hebrew behind it) is verbless. → see §A12.

### A12. *Anastrophē* — "conduct" as a 1 Peter keyword
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The noun **ἀναστροφή** (*anastrophē*, "way of life, conduct, manner of living") is one of the letter's signature terms, appearing at 1:15, 1:18, 2:12, 3:1, 3:2, and 3:16. It denotes not isolated acts but a whole pattern of life "turned about" among others (the word's root is *anastrephō*, "to move about, conduct oneself"). The TT renders it consistently "conduct / way of life" so the keyword's recurrence stays trackable. In 1:15 holiness is to mark "all *your* conduct"; in 1:18 the readers were ransomed from a "futile *anastrophē* handed down from *the* fathers" — the same word for the life left behind and the life taken up, the contrast carried by one repeated noun.

## B. Ancient Near Eastern and Ancient World Parallels

### B1. The lamb "without blemish" — Passover and the Servant of Isaiah 53
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

The ransom imagery of 1:18-19 — "not with perishable *things*, silver or gold… but with *the* precious blood, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot (**ἀμνοῦ ἀμώμου καὶ ἀσπίλου**, *amnou amōmou kai aspilou*)" — draws on two strands of HB sacrificial language. **ἄμωμος** (*amōmos*, "without blemish") is the LXX's standard requirement for an acceptable sacrificial animal (Exod 12:5 of the Passover lamb; Lev 1:3; 22:19-21; Num 28:3), and **ἀμνός** (*amnos*, "lamb") is the Passover-and-offering word. The combination evokes the Passover lamb whose blood marked Israel for deliverance (Exod 12), now reread as the price of the readers' redemption. The "lamb… led to slaughter" of Isaiah 53:7 (LXX *amnos*) stands alongside, supplying the *suffering-servant* resonance that the chapter's "sufferings of the Messiah" (1:11) has already named.

The parallel establishes a shared scriptural symbolism — an unblemished lamb's blood as the instrument of deliverance — not a claim that the verse depends on any single text. The TT keeps "lamb without blemish and without spot" literal so both the Passover and the Servant echoes stay audible. → see §A3 on the related blood-sprinkling, §A10 on the Exodus posture; for the citation mechanics see the PROPHECY companion (Ex 12 / Isa 53 → 1 Pet 1:19).

**Source:** Jobes, K.H., *1 Peter*, Baker Exegetical Commentary, Baker Academic, 2005, pp. 117-124; Beale, G.K. and Carson, D.A. (eds.), *Commentary on the New Testament Use of the Old Testament*, Baker Academic, 2007, pp. 1023-1027 (on 1 Peter 1).

### B2. The prophetic spirit and the "mystery searched out" in Second Temple thought
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The picture of prophets "searching out" a salvation meant for a later generation (1:10-12) has analogues in Second Temple Judaism's reflection on prophecy. The Qumran *pesharim* (commentaries) present the prophets as having spoken mysteries (*razim*) whose true meaning was hidden from them and disclosed only to the interpreter of the last days — the Habakkuk Commentary (1QpHab 7:1-5) states explicitly that God told Habakkuk to write but "did not make known to him the consummation of the age," which was revealed to the Teacher of Righteousness. The idea of an active "spirit of prophecy" speaking through the prophets is widespread in the Targums and rabbinic tradition (*ruach ha-qodesh*). These parallels show that the *structure* of 1 Peter's claim — prophets as inspired inquirers into a future they served but did not fully grasp — was at home in the period; they do not establish dependence, and 1 Peter's specific identification of the agent as "the spirit of the Messiah" (§A9) is its own.

**Source:** Vermes, G., *The Complete Dead Sea Scrolls in English*, rev. ed., Penguin, 2011 (1QpHab); Bauckham, R., *The Jewish World around the New Testament*, Mohr Siebeck, 2008, pp. 233-260.

### B3. The diaspora letter as a genre
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

A letter addressed from a center to scattered Jewish communities — instructing, encouraging, and binding them to a shared identity — was an established Second Temple form. Jeremiah's letter to the exiles (Jer 29 [36 LXX], "to the elders of the exile… build houses, seek the peace of the city") is the foundational instance: a word to *paroikoi* on how to live faithfully as residents in a land not their own. Later diaspora letters appear in 2 Maccabees 1:1-9 (Jerusalem to the Jews of Egypt) and the *Epistle of Jeremiah*, and the form continues in James 1:1 ("to the twelve tribes in the dispersion"). 1 Peter, "to *the* elect sojourners of *the* dispersion" (1:1), takes up exactly this genre — a circular letter casting its readers as exiles to be sustained in a hostile environment. The parallel illuminates the letter's shape and its instruction to live well as resident-aliens (cf. 2:11-12); it does not make 1 Peter literarily dependent on any one of these.

**Source:** Doering, L., *Ancient Jewish Letters and the Beginnings of Christian Epistolography*, Mohr Siebeck, 2012, pp. 429-484; Tsuji, M., "The Diaspora Letter," in studies of James and 1 Peter (as surveyed in Doering, above).

## C. Historical Context

### C1. The five provinces — Roman Asia Minor and the letter-carrier's circuit
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED (for the provinces); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the circuit)]**

The address names five regions of Asia Minor in order: **Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia** (1:1). By the mid-first century all were under Roman administration — Asia and Bithynia-Pontus as senatorial provinces, Galatia and Cappadocia as imperial provinces (Cappadocia annexed under Tiberius in 17 CE) — covering most of Anatolia north of the Taurus, an area of mixed Greek cities, indigenous populations, and Jewish diaspora communities (cf. the overlap with the pilgrim list of Acts 2:9-10). The unusual order — Pontus first, Bithynia last, though the two formed one province — has long been read as the **route of the bearer**, a circular itinerary beginning and ending at the Black Sea coast and looping inland through the interior. This is a reasonable inference from the sequence, not a stated fact. The detail locates the readers in the specific social world of provincial Anatolia, where the rest of the letter's concerns (alienation, slander, the household, civic authority) belong.

**Source:** Hemer, C.J., "The Address of 1 Peter," *Expository Times* 89 (1978), pp. 239-243; Mitchell, S., *Anatolia: Land, Men, and Gods in Asia Minor*, 2 vols., Oxford University Press, 1993, vol. 1, pp. 27-58, 149-159.

### C2. *Paroikoi* — resident-aliens as a real social class
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The letter's exile vocabulary (§A8) was not only theological metaphor: *paroikos* and *parepidēmos* named **actual legal-social statuses** in the Greco-Roman city. A *paroikos* was a free non-citizen resident — settled in a city but lacking citizen rights, often a metic-like figure subject to special taxes, excluded from full civic participation, and socially vulnerable. Some scholars argue the addressees included literal resident-aliens and displaced persons, so the spiritual designation traded on a felt social reality; others read the language as primarily a religious self-understanding adopted by people of varied legal status. Either way, the terms would have evoked the experience of being present-but-not-belonging — taxed, watched, and disadvantaged — which is precisely the situation the letter addresses (slander, suffering "for the name," 3:14-17; 4:12-16). The TT keeps the literal "sojourners," allowing both the social and the theological force to register.

**Source:** Elliott, J.H., *A Home for the Homeless: A Sociological Exegesis of 1 Peter*, Fortress Press, 1981, pp. 21-49; Feldmeier, R., *The First Letter of Peter*, Baylor University Press, 2008, pp. 8-13, 56-63.

### C3. The Greco-Roman household and honor culture
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The readers lived within the *oikos* (household), the basic unit of Greco-Roman society — a hierarchy of husband over wife, father over children, master over slaves, ordered by the widely-discussed ideal of *household management* (*oikonomia*; cf. Aristotle, *Politics* 1.1253b; the Stoic and later "household-code" tradition in Seneca and others). Social standing turned on **honor and shame**: a person's worth was a public reckoning, conferred or withdrawn by the community, and deviation from expected roles — including refusing the household's and city's gods — exposed a person to *shame*, slander, and reprisal. 1 Peter 1 sets up this background: the readers' new identity ("elect," "born again," holy) is asserted *against* their inherited social world ("the futile *anastrophē* handed down from *the* fathers," 1:18), and the call to "conduct yourselves in fear *during* the time of your sojourning" (1:17) anticipates the household instructions and the suffering-amid-slander that fill the later chapters. The first chapter establishes the alienated social position that the rest of the letter pastorally addresses.

**Source:** Balch, D.L., *Let Wives Be Submissive: The Domestic Code in 1 Peter*, Scholars Press, 1981, pp. 1-62; deSilva, D.A., *Honor, Patronage, Kinship and Purity*, InterVarsity Press, 2000, pp. 23-93.

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. *Eklektos* + *prognōsis* — "elect" and "foreknowledge"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Two election-words frame the salutation. **ἐκλεκτός** (*eklektos*, "chosen, elect," 1:1) is the LXX's term for Israel as God's chosen (Isa 43:20; 45:4; Ps 105:6), here transferred to the scattered readers and then reapplied to the Messiah himself as the "chosen" stone (2:4, 6). **πρόγνωσις** (*prognōsis*, 1:2) is "foreknowledge / prior knowing," from *pro* ("before") + *gnōsis* ("knowing"); its verb *proginōskō* describes the Messiah "foreknown (**προεγνωσμένου**) before *the* foundation of *the* world" (1:20) — the same root binding the readers' election (1:2) to the Messiah's pre-temporal designation (1:20). In the LXX and Semitic background "to know" (*yada*) often carries the relational sense of "acknowledge, choose, set in relationship," not bare cognition; so *prognōsis* leans toward "prior choosing / prior relationship" rather than mere advance information. The TT renders "foreknowledge" plainly and lets the relational range stand. → on the election's structure see §A2.

**Source:** Liddell, H.G. and Scott, R., *Greek-English Lexicon*, 9th ed., rev. Jones, 1940, s.v. ἐκλεκτός, πρόγνωσις; Bauer, W., *A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament* (BDAG), 3rd ed., rev. Danker, University of Chicago Press, 2000, s.v. πρόγνωσις, προγινώσκω.

### D2. *Lytroō* — "ransom / redeem" and the price image
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

At 1:18 the readers "were ransomed" (**ἐλυτρώθητε**, *elytrōthēte*, from *lytroō*) — the verb of paying a *lytron* ("ransom-price") to release a captive or slave. In the LXX *lytroō* renders the Hebrew *padah* and *gaal*, the language of **redemption**: God "redeemed" Israel from Egypt (Deut 7:8; 13:5; Exod 6:6) and the *goel* ("kinsman-redeemer") buys back an enslaved relative or forfeited land (Lev 25:25-55; Ruth 4). The cognate noun *lytrōsis* names the hoped-for "redemption" of Israel (Luke 2:38). Here the word is set in a deliberate contrast: redemption "not with perishable *things* — silver or gold" (the usual currency of a ransom) "but with *the* precious blood… of *the* Messiah" (1:18-19). The TT renders "ransomed" to keep the price-image — a real cost paid for release — rather than the flatter "saved," and the Exodus-redemption background frames the readers' deliverance as a new exodus.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. λυτρόω, λύτρον; Marshall, I.H., *Aspects of the Atonement*, Paternoster, 2007, pp. 1-33 (on the redemption word-group); Bauer, *BDAG*, s.v. λυτρόω.

### D3. *Pneuma* — the wind/spirit field across the chapter (1:2, 11, 12)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexeme); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE (for the breadth retained)]**

**πνεῦμα** (*pneuma*) carries the live range "wind / spirit / breath," and the TT keeps the locked GS slash {a:wind/spirit} at each of its three occurrences in the chapter: the "sanctification of *the* {a:wind/spirit}" in the election (1:2), the "{a:wind/spirit} of *the* Messiah in them" active in the prophets (1:11), and "*the* holy {a:wind/spirit} sent from heaven" through whom the gospel was preached (1:12). The slash is not a claim that "wind" is the meaning in each case but a refusal to collapse the field prematurely (Rule 2). **[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE]** that 1:12's "sent from heaven" leans toward the personal-spirit sense, while the breath/wind resonance remains available — as it does throughout the corpus where *pneuma* appears. The TT marks all three uniformly so the reader can weigh each in context. → on 1:11's contested genitive (*pneuma Christou*) see §A9.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. πνεῦμα; Bauer, *BDAG*, s.v. πνεῦμα.

### D4. *Psychē* — "soul" as the whole self, not a separable part
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

**ψυχή** (*psychē*, 1:9, 22) carries the HB range of *nephesh* — "life, self, person, the living being" — rather than a Greek-philosophical "soul" set against the body. The "salvation of *your* souls" (1:9) is the salvation of the whole persons, and "having purified your *souls*" (1:22) means the readers themselves, not an immaterial component. The same letter uses *psychē* for whole persons elsewhere ("eight souls were saved through water," 3:20, = eight people). The TT renders "soul" to keep the traditional term but the note signals the Semitic sense, guarding against importing a body/soul dualism the Greek does not assert here. The *telos* ("outcome / end / goal," 1:9) of faith is thus the deliverance of the entire self, present and future.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. ψυχή; Wolff, H.W., *Anthropology of the Old Testament*, Fortress Press, 1974, pp. 10-25 (on *nephesh*); Bauer, *BDAG*, s.v. ψυχή.

### D5. *Phroureō* — "guarded / garrisoned" by God's power
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

At 1:5 the readers are **φρουρουμένους** (*phrouroumenous*), "being guarded," by God's power. *Phroureō* is a **military** term — to keep watch over, garrison, hold under guard with a *phroura* (a garrison or sentry-post). The same word is used of a city held by a guard (2 Cor 11:32, of Damascus) and of being "kept in custody" (Gal 3:23). The image is of God's power as a protecting garrison stationed around the believers "for a salvation ready to be revealed" — and it sits in pointed tension with the readers' actual social exposure (§C2): outwardly vulnerable resident-aliens, they are inwardly "garrisoned" by divine power. The TT renders "guarded" with the note flagging the garrison-image, rather than the weaker "protected," so the military metaphor stays visible.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. φρουρέω; Bauer, *BDAG*, s.v. φρουρέω; Michaels, J.R., *1 Peter*, Word Biblical Commentary 49, Word, 1988, pp. 22-23.

## F. Later Reception in Other Traditions

### F1. "Born again" / *anagennaō* and baptismal reading
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The new-birth language of 1:3 and 1:23 (§A4), together with the chapter's purification and obedience motifs and the explicit baptism passage later in the letter (3:21), led many early and modern interpreters to read 1 Peter as shaped by **baptismal instruction or a baptismal liturgy**. From the 20th century, scholars such as Preisker, Cross, and Boismard proposed that parts of 1 Peter preserve a baptismal homily or even a paschal liturgy; the "born again through the living word" of 1:23 was tied to baptismal regeneration in later sacramental theology, and the phrase entered Christian devotional and revivalist vocabulary ("born again") far beyond its context. These are reception-history developments and scholarly reconstructions, not data of the Greek, which speaks of new birth "through *the* living and abiding word of God" (1:23) without naming a rite. The TT renders the verb's range (§A4) and leaves the baptismal application to its history; the liturgical-reconstruction theories are now widely regarded as unproven.

**Source:** Cross, F.L., *1 Peter: A Paschal Liturgy*, Mowbray, 1954 (the reconstruction proposal); Elliott, J.H., *1 Peter*, Anchor Bible 37B, Doubleday, 2000, pp. 7-12 (surveying and critiquing the liturgical theories).

### F2. The "spirit of the Messiah in the prophets" and pre-existence debates
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The claim of 1:11 that "the {a:wind/spirit} of *the* Messiah" was active "in" the HB prophets (§A9) became a proof-text in later Christian debates about the Messiah's pre-existence and the inspiration of the Old Testament. Patristic writers (e.g. Augustine, and earlier the broader tradition that the same Spirit/Word spoke through the prophets and the apostles) cited it for the unity of the two Testaments and the activity of Christ before the incarnation; the verse remained prominent in later doctrines of the inspiration and Christological reading of Scripture. These are documented doctrinal receptions built on the verse; the Greek genitive *pneuma Christou* is itself open (§A9), and the TT renders it without adjudicating the pre-existence question (Rule 3).

**Source:** Kelly, J.N.D., *Early Christian Doctrines*, 5th ed., A&C Black, 1977, pp. 138-145; Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, Fortress Press, 1996, pp. 109-112 (on the history of interpretation of 1:10-12).

### F3. "Resident aliens" as an ecclesial self-image
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The *paroikoi / parepidēmoi* designation (§A8, §C2) became a durable model for how Christian communities pictured themselves in the world. The second-century *Epistle to Diognetus* (5.5) elaborates the theme almost as a commentary on 1 Peter — Christians "live in their own countries, but only as resident aliens (*paroikoi*)… every foreign land is their homeland, and every homeland a foreign land." The Greek *paroikia* ("sojourning," 1:17) is also the etymological root of "parish" (via Latin *parochia*), a community of sojourners. In modern theology the image was revived as a lens on the church's relation to its culture (e.g. the "resident aliens" motif of late-20th-century ecclesiology). These are documented receptions of the keyword; the TT keeps the literal "sojourning" so the source-image behind the long tradition stays visible.

**Source:** *Epistle to Diognetus* 5, in Holmes, M.W. (ed.), *The Apostolic Fathers*, 3rd ed., Baker Academic, 2007; Elliott, J.H., *A Home for the Homeless*, Fortress Press, 1981, pp. 21-49, 129-132.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. "You rejoice" — indicative or imperative? (1:6, 8)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the data); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the mood)]**

The verb **ἀγαλλιᾶσθε** (*agalliasthe*, "you rejoice / rejoice!," 1:6, 8) is morphologically identical in the indicative and the imperative, so the Greek does not tell us whether Kefa **describes** the readers' present joy amid trial ("in this you rejoice") or **commands** it ("rejoice!"). The choice subtly shifts the tone — a statement of fact about the suffering community, or an exhortation to it. The TT reads the indicative (their present joy), as the surrounding participles and the logic of vv.6-9 favor, but the imperative reading is grammatically available and the ambiguity is real.

### G2. "Joy inexpressible and glorified" — a unique pairing
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The phrase **χαρᾷ ἀνεκλαλήτῳ καὶ δεδοξασμένῃ** (*chara aneklalētō kai dedoxasmenē*, "joy inexpressible and glorified," 1:8) joins two unusual adjectives. **ἀνεκλάλητος** (*aneklalētos*, "inexpressible, unutterable") is a New Testament *hapax legomenon* — it occurs nowhere else in the NT. **δεδοξασμένη** ("glorified," a perfect passive participle) applied to *joy* is striking: joy already touched by the coming glory. The pairing — joy that cannot be spoken and is already glory-laden — describes the readers loving and believing an **unseen** Lord (1:8; cf. John 20:29). The TT keeps both adjectives rather than smoothing to "indescribable joy," because the second word ("glorified") carries the chapter's revelation-and-glory theme into the readers' present experience.

### G3. The grass-and-flower of Isaiah 40 — the longer or shorter form?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the data); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the source-form)]**

The citation of Isaiah 40:6-8 at 1:24-25 follows the **LXX** closely ("all flesh *is* like grass… the word of *the* Lord endures into the age") but is **abbreviated**: it omits the middle clauses of the Isaiah passage (the repeated "the grass withers, the flower fades, because the breath of YHWH blows on it") and ends on the enduring word, which 1 Peter then identifies with "the word… preached as good news to you" (1:25). Whether the author quotes from memory, uses a shortened testimony-form, or deliberately trims to land on "the word endures" is not recoverable, but the selective citation is itself a feature — the abbreviation foregrounds exactly the contrast (transient flesh / enduring word) the argument needs. The TT renders the citation as it stands in the letter, not as the fuller Isaiah text. → see the PROPHECY companion (Isa 40:6-8 → 1 Pet 1:24-25).

### G4. *Rhēma* and *logos* — two "word"-words for one enduring word
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The chapter ends on a quiet lexical move. The new birth comes "through *the* living and abiding **λόγος** (*logos*) of God" (1:23); the Isaiah citation says "the **ῥῆμα** (*rhēma*) of *the* Lord endures" (1:25); and then the same verse closes, "this is the *rhēma* that was preached as good news to you." Two different Greek "word"-words (*logos*, the broader "word / message / reason"; *rhēma*, "spoken word / utterance / saying") are used for what is finally the same thing — the enduring word of God, now identified with the gospel preached. The TT renders both "word" so the identification (the living *logos* of v.23 = the enduring *rhēma* of v.25 = the gospel) is allowed to stand, while a reader who wishes can note that the source uses two terms.

## I. The World at the Time

> **A letter, not a narrative.** Because 1 Peter is an epistle, this section describes the **recipients' social world** — the lived setting of the "elect sojourners of *the* dispersion" addressed in 1:1 — rather than a narrated scene. The readers are scattered across five provinces of Roman Asia Minor in the mid-first century CE; the entries below sketch the political, social, religious, and household world against which the letter's exile-identity, its call to holiness, and its anticipation of slander and suffering must be read. The two scenarios bracket the most-discussed dating window for the letter (a Petrine/Neronian setting in the early 60s CE versus a later first-century setting), since the social pressures on the readers register somewhat differently in each.

---

### SCENARIO A — Mid-first century, Neronian era (c. 60-65 CE)

#### IA-1. Political — Roman Asia Minor under the early Principate
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The five named regions (1:1) lay within the Roman provincial system of Anatolia under the Julio-Claudian and early Flavian emperors. Asia (the wealthy western seaboard, with Ephesus, Pergamon, Smyrna) and Bithynia-Pontus (the northern coast) were senatorial provinces governed by proconsuls; Galatia and Cappadocia were imperial provinces under the emperor's legates. Roman roads, the provincial assemblies (*koina*), and the imperial cult bound the cities to Rome, while local civic elites competed for honor through benefaction. For the letter's readers — scattered, mostly non-elite, and now refusing the customary civic and household religion — this was a world in which standing depended on conformity, and conspicuous non-participation invited suspicion. The letter's concern with "doing good" before the authorities and being slandered "as evildoers" (2:12-15; cf. 1:17's "fear" in sojourning) presupposes exactly this provincial setting.

**Source:** Mitchell, S., *Anatolia: Land, Men, and Gods in Asia Minor*, Oxford University Press, 1993, vol. 1; Magie, D., *Roman Rule in Asia Minor*, Princeton University Press, 1950.

#### IA-2. Social Structure — Resident-aliens, status, and honor culture
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Greco-Roman provincial society was steeply stratified — citizens, resident-aliens (*paroikoi*), freedpersons, and slaves — and ordered by an all-pervading economy of **honor and shame**, in which worth was a public verdict rendered by one's peers and community (§C3). A *paroikos* or *parepidēmos* (§C2) occupied a precarious middle: free but without citizen rights, present but not belonging, vulnerable to special burdens and to the contempt of those above. 1 Peter takes this exact social location as its readers' literal-or-felt condition and reframes it: they are "sojourners" not only in the city but in the world, their true citizenship and inheritance "kept in *the* heavens" (1:4). For the first audience, the letter's exile-language named both their real marginality and a reinterpretation of it as election.

**Source:** Elliott, J.H., *A Home for the Homeless*, Fortress Press, 1981; deSilva, D.A., *Honor, Patronage, Kinship and Purity*, InterVarsity Press, 2000.

#### IA-3. Daily Life — The household (*oikos*) as the unit of life and faith
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Life was organized around the *oikos* — the extended household of kin, slaves, freedpersons, and dependents under a male head — which was simultaneously the economic, social, and religious unit (its gods were the household's gods, its honor the members' honor). Conversion that cut across the household's religion was therefore socially disruptive at the most basic level: a wife, slave, or son who would not worship the household and civic gods threatened its cohesion and its standing. The opening chapter prepares for the household instructions that follow (2:18-3:7) by grounding the readers' identity in a new birth and a new "father" — "if you call upon *as* Father" the impartial judge (1:17) — that relativizes "the futile *anastrophē* handed down from *the* fathers" (1:18). The household world is the immediate stage on which the letter's holiness and conduct (*anastrophē*) must be lived.

**Source:** Balch, D.L., *Let Wives Be Submissive*, Scholars Press, 1981; Osiek, C. and Balch, D.L., *Families in the New Testament World*, Westminster John Knox, 1997, pp. 5-47.

#### IA-4. Religion — Civic cult, the diaspora synagogue, and a new movement
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The religious landscape of Asia Minor was densely populated: the traditional Greek and indigenous Anatolian deities, the mystery cults, and the rising **imperial cult** (especially strong in the province of Asia), alongside well-established **Jewish diaspora communities** with their synagogues across the region (attested epigraphically and by Josephus). The readers — whether of Jewish or, more likely in much of this territory, gentile background — are addressed in thoroughly Jewish terms ("dispersion," "elect," "be holy as I am holy," redemption by a Passover-like lamb), suggesting a movement that understood itself in continuity with Israel's story while standing apart from both the civic cults and the synagogue. The letter's silence about participation in civic religion, combined with its insistence on a single holy God and a redemptive lamb, marks the social fault-line: the readers' withdrawal from the customary worship of their cities is precisely what exposed them to the slander and suffering the letter addresses.

**Source:** Trebilco, P., *Jewish Communities in Asia Minor*, Cambridge University Press, 1991; Price, S.R.F., *Rituals and Power: The Roman Imperial Cult in Asia Minor*, Cambridge University Press, 1984.

---

### SCENARIO B — Later first century (post-70 CE setting)

#### IB-1. Social Structure — Reading the exile-identity after the war
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

On the view that 1 Peter reached (or was composed for) its Anatolian readers in the later first century — after the Jewish war and the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE — the "dispersion" and "sojourning" language would carry an added weight. For Jewish and Jewish-adjacent communities across the diaspora, exile was no longer only a metaphor or an ancestral memory but a freshly intensified reality: the cultic center was gone, and the diaspora was now the whole of Jewish life. A movement that called its scattered members "elect sojourners of *the* dispersion" and located their inheritance "in *the* heavens" (1:4) addressed people for whom homelessness — religious and sometimes literal — was a live condition, and offered a securely "kept" inheritance precisely where the earthly one had proven losable.

**Source:** Goodman, M., *Rome and Jerusalem: The Clash of Ancient Civilizations*, Allen Lane, 2007; Williams, M.H., *The Jews among the Greeks and Romans: A Diasporan Sourcebook*, Johns Hopkins University Press, 1998.

#### IB-2. Religion — Suffering "for the name" and the pressure on a marginal movement
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

By the later first century, the followers of Yeshua in Asia Minor were an increasingly visible and distinct group, no longer simply shelterable under the legal recognition extended to Judaism, and subject to local hostility — social slander, denunciation, and sporadic official action rather than empire-wide persecution. The famous correspondence of Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia-Pontus c. 111-113 CE (one of the very regions of 1:1), documents trials of Christians "for the name" itself, prompted by local accusations — a later but geographically exact window onto the kind of pressure 1 Peter anticipates ("if *anyone suffers* as a Christian," 4:16; "the fiery trial," 4:12). For a later-first-century audience, the letter's insistence on holy conduct, doing good, and a hope that outlasts trial spoke directly to a community learning to live as a slandered minority. The first chapter lays the foundation: an identity, a hope, and a holiness that do not depend on the surrounding society's approval.

**Source:** Pliny the Younger, *Letters* 10.96-97, Loeb Classical Library; Horrell, D.G., *Becoming Christian: Essays on 1 Peter and the Making of Christian Identity*, T&T Clark, 2013, pp. 164-210.
