## Note on 1 Peter's mode of allusion (an epistle, not a narrative)

1 Peter is a **letter** — *argument and exhortation* in the apostle's own voice, not a narrative of events fulfilling prediction. So its use of the Hebrew Bible is not "prophecy → fulfillment" in the gospel-and-Acts sense. The letter **deploys** the Hebrew Bible *paraenetically and typologically*: it **quotes** it to ground a command (Psalm 34, vv.10–12, grounding non-retaliation; Isaiah 8, vv.14–15, grounding the fearless apologia), it **applies type-scenes** of Israel's story to the readers (Noah, the ark, the eight saved through water as the baptism antitype, vv.20–21), and it **echoes** its diction and exempla (Sarah "calling him lord," v.6; "fear no terror," v.6; the exalted one "at the right hand," v.22). The dual-label and citation-vs-allusion system carries over unchanged from the gospel companions; what shifts is how to read the **status line**. Here "fulfillment status" should be read as **mode of deployment**:

- **CLAIMED** ≈ **explicit quotation** — the letter cites the OT (the verbatim Psalm 34 of vv.10–12; the Isaiah 8 of vv.14–15) and presses it as authoritative for the readers.
- **PARTIAL** ≈ **typological application** — the letter reads an Israel event (the flood, the ark, the eight saved) as the pattern realized in the readers' baptism, without claiming a prediction fulfilled.
- **DEBATED** ≈ **echo** — the diction or an exemplum of an OT scene is interiorized into the exhortation (Sarah's deference; "fear no terror"; the right-hand exaltation), without citation or fulfillment-claim.

Because 1 Peter quotes the OT in the *third* person about God as often as it carries God's *first*-person word, **speech-marking (Rule 30 / A-004)** is selective: the letter is the apostle's **human** exhortation throughout and is not marked; only where the *source verse* is God's own first-person word would the quotation carry `@@…@@`. **In 1 Peter 3 there is no divine-marked saying at all:** the Psalm 34 citation (vv.10–12) is the **psalmist's** human voice (third person, "the eyes of *the Lord*…"), and the Isaiah 8 material (vv.14–15) is the **prophet's** human voice — neither is marked. Where κύριος in a citation stands for YHWH, the entries note it per **Option C** (the main text renders "the Lord" + Tier-2; the source-referent is YHWH; P-006/§ κύριος Option C in `1-peter.md`). The single *kyrios* of v.6 (Sarah's "calling him **lord**") is a **human honorific** — lowercase, not the divine name.

One entry below registers a **Second-Temple** source (1 Enoch) rather than a Hebrew-Bible passage: it is included only because it is a live *reading* of "the spirits in prison" (3:19) and is presented strictly as **one option among several, resolving none** (P-006; Rule 13) — not as a fulfillment claim and not as the TT's reading.

## Psalm 34:12–16 (LXX 33:13–17) — "keep your tongue from evil… the eyes of the Lord are on the righteous" (1 Peter 3:10–12)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 3:10–12
**Text says:** "For 'the *one* desiring to love life and to see good days, let him stop his tongue from evil and *his* lips from speaking deceit, and let him turn away from evil and do good, let him seek peace and pursue it. Because *the* eyes of *the* Lord *are* on *the* righteous, and his ears to their petition, but *the* face of *the* Lord *is* against *those* doing evil.'"
**Context:** The scriptural ground of the non-retaliation ethic (3:8–12). Having called the readers not to repay "evil for evil or reviling for reviling, but on the contrary blessing" (3:9), the letter seals the command with the **longest OT citation in the letter** — a wisdom exhortation from Psalm 34. The quoted words are the **psalmist's** human voice *about* the Lord (third person, "the eyes of *the* Lord… his ears… the face of *the* Lord"), so they are **not** divine-marked (Rule 30).
**Subject:** The Lord who attends the righteous and opposes evildoers. 1 Peter quotes **Psalm 34:12–16** (LXX **33:13–17**) — the wisdom call to "keep your tongue from evil" and "seek peace and pursue it," with its assurance that "the eyes of YHWH are toward the righteous." The Greek **ὁ… θέλων ζωὴν ἀγαπᾶν… παυσάτω τὴν γλῶσσαν ἀπὸ κακοῦ… ζητησάτω εἰρήνην καὶ διωξάτω αὐτήν… ὀφθαλμοὶ κυρίου ἐπὶ δικαίους** (*ho… thelōn zōēn agapan… pausatō tēn glōssan apo kakou… zētēsatō eirēnēn kai diōxatō autēn… ophthalmoi kyriou epi dikaious*) follows the LXX closely. In the Psalm the speaker is the psalmist (David, per the superscription) teaching "the fear of YHWH" to his hearers; **κύριος = YHWH** (Option C). 1 Peter lifts the wisdom exhortation and presses it directly on the diaspora readers as the rationale for blessing rather than retaliating. This **continues the Psalm-34 thread** already opened at 2:3 ("you have tasted that the Lord is good/kind," Ps 34:8).
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED *(mode: explicit quotation)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is the **letter's longest, verbatim citation** (LXX Ps 33:13–17), introduced without a formula ("For 'the *one* desiring…'," *gar* + the citation) — its authority is assumed and it grounds the immediately preceding command (3:9). The deployment is **paraenetic**, not predictive: 1 Peter does not claim the Psalm foretold an event now fulfilled; it **applies** the wisdom assurance (God attends the righteous, opposes evildoers) to motivate the readers' non-retaliation. On the divine name: in the Psalm, κύριος = YHWH (Option C); the TT renders "the Lord" in the main text and notes the YHWH referent (3:12 note). Because the citation is the psalmist's voice *about* the Lord (third person), it is **not** marked `@@…@@` (Rule 30) — there is no divine-marked saying in this chapter. **[textual — VERIFIED (citation); the application to the readers' non-retaliation — CLAIMED]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ps 34:12–16 → 1 Pet 3:10–12) in `content/en/1-peter/CHAPTER-3.md`. For the start of the Psalm-34 thread, see the chapter-2 companion (Ps 34:8 → 1 Pet 2:3).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Psalm 34 is a wisdom psalm (acrostic) of thanksgiving and instruction — "come, children, listen to me; I will teach you the fear of YHWH" — urging the guarding of the tongue, the turn from evil, the pursuit of peace, with the assurance that YHWH's eyes are toward the righteous and hears their cry. It is read within Israel's wisdom and liturgical tradition (a psalm of David, recited in worship); its mobilization to ground a Christian community's non-retaliation toward outsiders is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Psalm 34:12–16 is read as the wisdom ground of the gospel ethic — the guarded tongue, the active peacemaking, the God who watches over the righteous — and 1 Peter 3:10–12 as the apostolic application of that assurance to the suffering church, motivating blessing-not-retaliation (cf. the Sermon's "blessed are the peacemakers," Mt 5:9; Rom 12:17–21). [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam affirms the guarding of the tongue, the turn from evil to good, the pursuit of peace, and that God sees and hears the righteous (cf. the frequent Quranic "God is hearing, seeing," *as-Samīʿ al-Baṣīr*); the specific Psalm-34 wording and its application to the Christian community's conduct are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Isaiah 8:12–13 — "do not fear their fear… sanctify the Lord" (1 Peter 3:14–15)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 3:14–15
**Text says:** "But even if you should suffer for righteousness, *you are* blessed. And do not fear their fear, nor be troubled, but sanctify *the* Messiah as Lord in your hearts, *being* always ready for a defense to everyone asking you a word concerning the hope in you,"
**Context:** The turn from non-retaliation to **suffering for righteousness** (3:13–17): even if harm comes, the readers are "blessed" (the beatitude form, cf. Mt 5:10); they are not to fear what their accusers fear, but to "sanctify the Messiah as Lord" and be "always ready for a defense" (*apologia*). The Isaiah words are the **prophet's** human voice, not God's first person, so they are **not** divine-marked (Rule 30).
**Subject:** The sanctified Lord, not the dreaded threat. 1 Peter quotes **Isaiah 8:12–13** (LXX) — the prophet's word to the faithful in a time of conspiracy and dread: "do not fear what this people fears, and do not be troubled… YHWH of hosts — **him** you shall regard as holy (sanctify), and **he** *shall be* your fear." The Greek **τὸν δὲ φόβον αὐτῶν μὴ φοβηθῆτε μηδὲ ταραχθῆτε** (*ton de phobon autōn mē phobēthēte mēde tarachthēte*, "do not fear their fear nor be troubled," v.14) follows Isa 8:12 closely; **κύριον δὲ τὸν Χριστὸν ἁγιάσατε** (*kyrion de ton Christon hagiasate*, "sanctify *the* Messiah as Lord," v.15) takes the imperative of Isa 8:13 ("**the Lord** [κύριον] sanctify") but **substitutes "the Messiah" for the object**. This is the chapter's key theological move: in Isaiah the one to be "sanctified" is **YHWH of hosts** (κύριος = YHWH, Option C); 1 Peter applies that YHWH-text to the Messiah — a **κύριος referent-shift** (cross-ref the same move at Acts 2:36, "God made him both Lord and Messiah").
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED *(mode: explicit quotation, with a referent-shift)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is a **verbatim quotation** of Isa 8:12 (the "do not fear their fear" clause) **plus a modified citation** of Isa 8:13 (the "sanctify the Lord" imperative with the object changed). The deployment is **paraenetic**, not predictive: 1 Peter does not claim Isaiah foretold an event now fulfilled; it **applies** Isaiah's call to fearless reverence to the readers' situation of suffering for righteousness. The interpretive crux is the **referent-shift**: where Isaiah's object of "sanctify" is **YHWH of hosts**, 1 Peter writes "sanctify **the Messiah** as Lord" — applying the YHWH-text directly to Christ (Rule 13 + Tier-2; 3:15 note). The TT renders the substitution as 1 Peter gives it and flags the move; it does not adjudicate the christological inference the substitution carries. On the divine name: in Isaiah, κύριος = YHWH (Option C); the source referent is noted (3:15 note). Because the citation is the prophet's voice (not God's first person), it is **not** marked `@@…@@` (Rule 30). **[textual — VERIFIED (citation: Isa 8:12 verbatim, 8:13 modified); the κύριος referent-shift / christological application — CLAIMED]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Isa 8:12–13 → 1 Pet 3:14–15) in `content/en/1-peter/CHAPTER-3.md`. For the parallel "Lord = Messiah" identification in the apostolic preaching, see `content/en/acts/study/CHAPTER-2-PROPHECY.md` (Acts 2:36).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Isaiah 8:12–13 is the prophet's word during the Syro-Ephraimite crisis — do not call "conspiracy" what the people call conspiracy, and do not fear what they fear; rather "YHWH of hosts, **him** sanctify, and let **him** be your fear and dread." The one to be hallowed is YHWH alone; the text grounds the fear of God over the fear of human threats. Its reapplication so that "the Messiah" stands as the object of "sanctify" is not part of the Jewish reading, which holds the object to be YHWH. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Isaiah 8:12–13 is read as the call to fearless reverence under threat, and 1 Peter 3:14–15 as the apostolic application that **identifies the Messiah with the Lord to be sanctified** — a high-christological reading in which the YHWH-text is referred to Christ (cf. the same κύριος-application at Acts 2:36; Rom 10:13 / Joel 2:32); the readers' fearless apologia flows from hallowing him in the heart. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam affirms that God alone is to be feared and held holy (*al-Quddūs*), and that the believer should not fear human threats but trust in God (cf. Surah 3:175, "do not fear them, but fear Me"); the Isaiah-8 oracle and especially its application of the "sanctify the Lord" command to the Messiah are not part of the Quranic presentation, which does not regard Isa (Jesus) as the divine Lord to be hallowed. [DOCUMENTED]

## Genesis 6–8 — Noah, the ark, the eight saved through water (1 Peter 3:20–21)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 3:20–21
**Text says:** "who once disobeyed, when the patience of God was waiting in *the* days of Noach (Noah), *while* an ark was being prepared, into which a few — that is, eight souls — were brought safely through water. Which also, *as* an antitype, now saves you — immersion, not a removal of dirt of *the* flesh but an appeal of a good conscience toward God — through *the* resurrection of Yeshua *the* Messiah (Jesus),"
**Context:** The flood-and-ark exemplum within the letter's storm-center (3:18–22): the Messiah "made alive in spirit" went and proclaimed to "the spirits in prison" (3:19, see the next entry), who "once disobeyed… in the days of Noah" while the ark was built and "eight souls were brought safely through water" (3:20) — and that water is read as the **type** whose **antitype** is baptism (3:21). The flood-narrative is **typological application**, not a quotation: no verse is cited.
**Subject:** The flood and the eight saved, read as the baptism type. The exemplum draws on **Genesis 6–8** — the building of the ark (Gen 6:14–22), the **patience of God** that "waited" before the deluge (cf. Gen 6:3), the flood that judged "the days of Noah," and the **eight souls** (Noach, his wife, his three sons, and their wives — Gen 7:7, 13) "brought safely **through water**." The Greek **ἐν ἡμέραις Νῶε κατασκευαζομένης κιβωτοῦ… ὀκτὼ ψυχαί, διεσώθησαν δι' ὕδατος** (*en hēmerais Nōe kataskeuazomenēs kibōtou… oktō psychai, diesōthēsan di' hydatos*, "in *the* days of Noach, *while* an ark was being prepared… eight souls were brought safely through water") uses *kibōtos*, the LXX's word for the ark. The flood **both judged and rescued**: the same water that destroyed the disobedient was, for the eight, the medium of deliverance — and 1 Peter makes that double valence the bridge to baptism, "which also, *as* an antitype (*antitypon*), now saves you" (3:21), immediately qualified as "not a removal of dirt of the flesh but an appeal of a good conscience toward God… through *the* resurrection." 1 Peter does not quote Genesis; it **applies** the flood/ark type-scene as the pattern realized in the readers' baptism.
**Fulfillment status:** PARTIAL *(mode: typological application)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **typology / allusion**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse and uses no formula; it **deploys** the Genesis flood-narrative (the ark, God's patience, the eight saved through water) as the pattern (*typos*) whose **antitype** (*antitypon*, the only NT use besides Heb 9:24) is baptism. The connection is explicit in the text's own vocabulary: the flood-water is the type, baptism the antitype, and "saved… through water" (3:20) is matched by baptism that "now saves you" (3:21). The TT keeps the typology and the immediate qualification — baptism "not a removal of dirt of the flesh but an appeal/pledge (*eperōtēma*) of a good conscience" — **without importing a sacramental theology**, since the saving is "through *the* resurrection," not the water itself (Rule 3; P-007; 3:21 note). The PARTIAL label marks this as typological application (the Genesis pattern read as realized in the readers' baptism), not an explicit quotation and not a fulfilled prediction. **[comparative parallel — PROBABLE (typology, not citation); the *eperōtēma* sense ("appeal" vs. "pledge") — POSSIBLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Gen 6–8 → 1 Pet 3:20). For the Genesis-6 "watchers" reading attached to the *adjacent* clause (3:19), see the next entry.
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Genesis 6–8 is the flood narrative — the corruption of "the days of Noah," God's grief, the ark built at God's command, and Noah's household (the eight) preserved while the world is judged by the deluge; in later tradition Noah is the righteous man of his generation and the flood a paradigm of judgment-and-mercy. These belong to Israel's primeval history; the reading of the flood-water as a type of a Christian baptism that "now saves" is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The flood and the eight saved through water are read as the type of baptism — the water that judges and rescues prefiguring the water that "now saves you… through the resurrection" (1 Pet 3:21); the ark/household becomes an image of the saved community, and the Noah type recurs in early Christian flood-and-baptism typology. The qualification (an appeal of a good conscience, not the washing itself) is read as guarding against a merely external rite. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Nuh (Noah) is a major prophet in the Quran — he builds the ark by God's command and inspiration, warns his people, and is saved with the believers while the rest are drowned (cf. Surah 11:36–48; 71); the flood is a sign of judgment on those who rejected the messenger. The specific reading of the flood-water as a type of Christian baptism is not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## 1 Enoch 6–21 (Second-Temple) — the imprisoned "watchers" of Genesis 6, a live reading of "the spirits in prison" (1 Peter 3:19)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 3:19
**Text says:** "in which also, having gone, he proclaimed to the spirits in prison,"
**Context:** The single most debated clause in the letter, set between the Messiah "made alive in spirit" (3:18) and the disobedient "in the days of Noah" (3:20). The Messiah "having gone, proclaimed (*ekēryxen*) to the spirits in prison" — the descent/proclamation crux. No OT (or Enochic) verse is *cited*; this entry registers a Second-Temple text only as one **reading** of the clause, presented strictly as an option (P-006; Rule 13).
**Subject:** "The spirits in prison" and the readings they admit. The Greek **τοῖς ἐν φυλακῇ πνεύμασιν πορευθεὶς ἐκήρυξεν** (*tois en phylakē pneumasin poreutheis ekēryxen*, "having gone, he proclaimed to the spirits in prison") is irreducibly open. One major reading interprets "the spirits in prison" through the **Second-Temple Enochic tradition** — **1 Enoch 6–21** (the "Book of the Watchers"), which expands **Genesis 6:1–4** (the "sons of God," the *bĕnê hāʾĕlōhîm*) into a narrative of rebel **angels/"watchers"** who descended, corrupted humanity, and were **bound and imprisoned** under the earth to await judgment. On this reading, the "imprisoned spirits" are those bound angels, and "in the days of Noah" (3:20) locates their disobedience in the same primeval episode (cf. **2 Peter 2:4**, "God did not spare the angels who sinned but cast them into Tartarus… kept for judgment"; **Jude 6**, "angels who… left their proper dwelling, he has kept in eternal chains… for the judgment"). But this is **only one** of the live readings, and the TT **resolves none**:
- **(a)** the **disobedient human dead of Noah's generation** — "the spirits" are the people who "once disobeyed… in the days of Noah" (3:20), to whom the Messiah proclaimed (in a descent, or who heard the warning in their lifetime);
- **(b)** the **disobedient angels/"watchers" of Genesis 6**, imprisoned per the Enochic tradition (1 Enoch 6–21; cf. 2 Pet 2:4; Jude 6) — this entry's classified parallel;
- **(c)** the **pre-incarnate Messiah proclaiming through Noah** to that disobedient generation while the ark was built.
The content of the proclamation (*ekēryxen*, "proclaimed/heralded") is **not specified** — good news vs. a herald of judgment is itself disputed. (This entry classifies the **Enochic/Genesis-6 watchers** reading; the human-dead and pre-incarnate-proclamation readings are noted but not separately entried.)
**Fulfillment status:** DEBATED *(mode: comparative parallel / echo — a reading, not a fulfillment claim)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **not a citation and not a fulfillment claim** — 1 Peter quotes neither Genesis nor 1 Enoch, and the Enochic tradition is **post-biblical Second-Temple literature** (not Hebrew Bible), registered here only because it is a **live reading** of an otherwise opaque clause. It is classified as a **comparative parallel / echo**, not a typology or quotation: the Enochic "imprisoned watchers" supply *one* possible referent for "the spirits in prison," and the kinship of 2 Peter 2:4 / Jude 6 (which clearly draw on that tradition) lends it weight. **Per the chapter's instruction and P-006, all three major readings are laid out (a/b/c above) and none is adopted** — the TT renders the clause literally ("the spirits in prison," "proclaimed," content unspecified) and resolves nothing (Rule 13, Rule 3; 3:19 note; Companion §A; §F on the reception, the "harrowing of hell"). The DEBATED label marks this as a contested reading carrying no fulfillment-claim and no citation. **[comparative parallel — POSSIBLE (one reading among several; Second-Temple, not HB); the referent of "the spirits in prison" — Uncertain]** → See editorial-log **P-006** (`docs/editorial-log/1-peter.md`) and Cross-Chapter Tracking (1 Enoch 6–21 → 1 Pet 3:19) in `content/en/1-peter/CHAPTER-3.md`.
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Genesis 6:1–4 (the "sons of God" who took human wives) is read variously — as angelic beings, or as the lines of Seth and Cain, or as dynastic "great ones"; the **Enochic** expansion (1 Enoch's "Watchers" bound under the earth for their rebellion) is a Second-Temple development, influential in some streams but not part of the rabbinic mainstream, which largely set Enoch aside. The "imprisoned spirits" of a Christian text proclaimed to by a risen Messiah are not part of the Jewish reading; the underlying Genesis-6 episode and its angelic interpretation are. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: 1 Peter 3:19 is among the most debated verses in the NT. The major readings — (a) Christ proclaiming to the human dead of Noah's day (linked to the "harrowing of hell" / the descensus); (b) Christ proclaiming **triumph or judgment to the imprisoned fallen angels** of Genesis 6 read through the Enochic tradition (the reading favored by many modern commentators, tied to 2 Pet 2:4; Jude 6, and to the v.22 "powers subjected"); (c) the pre-incarnate Christ preaching through Noah — coexist without consensus. The creeds' "he descended" is variously connected. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: The Quran narrates rebellious and bound spirits/jinn and the fall of Iblis (cf. Surah 7:11–18; the chained devils, 38:37–38), and Genesis-6-style angel narratives appear in the Harut-and-Marut episode (Surah 2:102); but the specific 1 Enoch "imprisoned watchers" framework and its application to "the spirits in prison" of 1 Peter are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Genesis 18:12 — Sarah "calling him lord" (1 Peter 3:6)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 3:6
**Text says:** "as Sarah (Sara) obeyed Avraham (Abraham), calling him lord; of whom you became children, doing good and not fearing any terror."
**Context:** The matriarchal precedent within the household code (3:5–6): "the holy women of old" adorned themselves with the inner *kosmos* and submitted to their husbands — Sarah named as the exemplar, whose "children" the readers become "by doing good and not fearing any terror." The Sarah exemplum is an **echo / application** of a Genesis scene, not a quotation; no verse is cited.
**Subject:** Sarah's deference, the matriarch as exemplar. **ὡς Σάρρα ὑπήκουσεν τῷ Ἀβραάμ, κύριον αὐτὸν καλοῦσα** (*hōs Sarra hypēkousen tō Abraam, kyrion auton kalousa*, "as Sarah obeyed Avraham, calling him lord") draws on **Genesis 18:12**, where Sarah, overhearing the promise of a son, laughs within herself: "after I have grown old, shall I have pleasure, **my lord** (*ʾădonî*; LXX **ὁ… κύριός μου**) *being* old also?" The Hebrew *ʾădonî* / Greek *kyrios* there is a **human honorific** — Sarah's deferential reference to Avraham as "my lord" — **not** the divine name. 1 Peter takes that detail (Sarah inwardly "calling him lord") as the type of the wife's reverent conduct and makes the readers "her children" by good-doing and fearlessness. The TT renders *kyrios* here **lowercase "lord"** (human honorific), distinct from κύριος = YHWH elsewhere in the chapter (vv.12, 15) and from "the Messiah as Lord" (v.15).
**Fulfillment status:** DEBATED *(mode: echo / exemplum)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion / echo by exemplum**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse and uses no formula; it lifts the **detail** of Genesis 18:12 (Sarah's inward "my lord") as the matriarchal pattern for the wife's conduct. The connection is deployment, not prediction: the letter reads Sarah's deference as the precedent the readers join ("of whom you became children"). The interpretive weight falls on the **household code** (high-restraint, P-009): the TT renders the Greek — Sarah "calling him lord," the readers "her children" — and **flags the reception history in Tier-2 / the companion, taking no position** (Rule 3; 3:6 note; Companion §F). On the divine name: *kyrios* here is a **human** honorific (lowercase), expressly **not** YHWH — a deliberate contrast with the κύριος = YHWH of vv.12 and 15. The DEBATED label marks this as an echo/exemplum carrying no fulfillment-claim and no explicit citation (Rule 13). **[comparative parallel — PROBABLE (exemplum drawn from Gen 18:12); the *kyrios* = human honorific (not divine) — VERIFIED]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Gen 18:12 → 1 Pet 3:6) in `content/en/1-peter/CHAPTER-3.md`; editorial-log P-009 (the household code).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Genesis 18:12 records Sarah's inward laughter and her deferential reference to Avraham as "my lord" (*ʾădonî*) at the annunciation of Yitschaq (Isaac); the matriarchs are honored, and Sarah's words are read within the Genesis narrative of the promised son and Sarah's own dignity (cf. God's instruction to Avraham, "in all that Sarah says to you, listen to her voice," Gen 21:12). Her use as a paradigm of wifely submission for a later community is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Sarah "calling him lord" is read as the matriarchal model held up in the household code — the holy women of old whose adornment was the inner self and whose conduct was reverent; 1 Peter 3:6 makes the believing women "Sarah's children" by doing good and fearlessness. The passage's reception (its use to ground wifely submission) is much discussed; the TT flags this and takes no position (P-009). [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Sarah (Sārah) is honored as the wife of Ibrahim and mother of Ishaq, and the annunciation of a son to her in old age is narrated (cf. Surah 11:71–73; 51:29–30); the specific "calling him lord" detail and its use as a model of wifely conduct are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Proverbs 3:25 — "not fearing any terror" (1 Peter 3:6)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 3:6
**Text says:** "…of whom you became children, doing good and not fearing any terror."
**Context:** The close of the Sarah exemplum (3:6): the readers are Sarah's "children" not only by doing good but by "not fearing any terror" — a clause that echoes a wisdom assurance. The phrase is an **echo**, interiorized into the exhortation; no verse is cited.
**Subject:** Fearlessness before sudden terror. **μὴ φοβούμεναι μηδεμίαν πτόησιν** (*mē phoboumenai mēdemian ptoēsin*, "not fearing any terror") echoes **Proverbs 3:25** LXX — "do not be afraid of **sudden terror** (*ptoēsin*)… for YHWH will be your confidence." The noun **πτόησις** (*ptoēsis*, "terror, fright, alarm") is rare, and its appearance here, in the wisdom register, points to the Proverbs assurance that the one who trusts YHWH need not dread sudden calamity. 1 Peter applies the wisdom fearlessness to the believing women (and, by extension, the readers under pressure), pairing it with "doing good." The echo also anticipates the "do not fear their fear" of v.14 (Isaiah 8) — fearlessness is the chapter's connective thread.
**Fulfillment status:** DEBATED *(mode: echo)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion / echo**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse and uses no formula; the rare noun *ptoēsis* ("terror") is the recognizable lexical link to Proverbs 3:25 LXX, the wisdom assurance against "sudden terror." The connection is deployment, not prediction: the letter borrows the wisdom posture of fearlessness-grounded-in-trust and applies it to the readers' conduct under threat — anticipating the explicit Isaiah-8 citation of "do not fear their fear" (v.14). **Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Proverbs 3:25 belongs to the wisdom instruction of trust — "do not fear sudden terror or the ruin of the wicked when it comes, for YHWH will be your confidence" — the assurance that the one who walks in wisdom and the fear of YHWH need not dread calamity. It is read within Israel's wisdom tradition; its application to the conduct of believing women in a later community is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The "not fearing any terror" is read as the wisdom assurance carried into the gospel ethic of courage under suffering — the believer (here, the believing woman as Sarah's child) freed from dread by trust in God; it dovetails with the Isaiah-8 "do not fear their fear" of v.14 to make fearlessness the mark of those who hope in the Messiah. - **Islamic**: Islam affirms that the one who trusts in God need not fear, and that "no fear shall be upon them, nor shall they grieve" is the recurring assurance to the faithful (cf. ## Psalm 110:1 — "at the right hand of God… powers subjected" (1 Peter 3:22)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 3:22
**Text says:** "who is at *the* right *hand* of God, having gone into heaven, *with* angels and authorities and powers having been subjected to him."
**Context:** The climax of the chapter's storm-center (3:18–22): the Messiah who suffered, was made alive, and proclaimed to the spirits in prison is now exalted "at the right hand of God," with "angels and authorities and powers" subjected to him. The exaltation language is an **echo** of the most-cited HB verse; no verse is formally cited.
**Subject:** The Messiah enthroned at the right hand, all powers subjected. **ὅς ἐστιν ἐν δεξιᾷ θεοῦ… ὑποταγέντων αὐτῷ ἀγγέλων καὶ ἐξουσιῶν καὶ δυνάμεων** (*hos estin en dexia theou… hypotagentōn autō angelōn kai exousiōn kai dynameōn*, "who is at *the* right *hand* of God… *with* angels and authorities and powers having been subjected to him") echoes **Psalm 110:1** (LXX 109:1) — "YHWH said to my lord, 'Sit at my **right hand** until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet.'" Psalm 110:1 is the **most-cited Hebrew-Bible verse in the New Testament** (the right-hand session + the subjection of enemies); 1 Peter draws on its right-hand exaltation and, with the "subjection" (*hypotagentōn*) of all spirit-powers, on its "until I make your enemies a footstool." The verb of subjection (*hypotassō*) is the **household-code verb** of this very chapter (3:1, 5) and ties the cosmic subjection of the powers back to the "spirits in prison" of v.19: the Messiah who proclaimed to them now reigns over every power. 1 Peter does not quote Psalm 110; it **echoes** its enthronement language. (Cross-ref: the explicit Psalm 110:1 citation in the apostolic preaching, Acts 2:34–35.)
**Fulfillment status:** DEBATED *(mode: echo)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion / echo**, not a formula citation — 1 Peter does not say "as it is written" or quote Psalm 110 verbatim; it weaves the psalm's right-hand exaltation and subjection-of-enemies into the chapter's closing affirmation. The connection is deliberate and recognizable: "at the right hand of God" is the standing NT shorthand for Psalm 110:1, and "powers… subjected" (*hypotagentōn*) renders the psalm's "footstool" subjection into the spirit-realm — pointedly using the chapter's own household-code verb (*hypotassō*) to answer the "spirits in prison" (v.19). The DEBATED label marks this as an echo — diction and motif borrowed and applied — carrying no formula and no explicit fulfillment-claim (Rule 13). On the divine name: Psalm 110:1's "YHWH said to my lord" stands behind the exaltation, though 1 Peter cites no κύριος line here; the right-hand session is referred to "God" (3:22). **[comparative parallel — PROBABLE (the right-hand session = Ps 110:1, the NT's most-cited verse); the explicit citation form — not present here (echo)]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ps 110:1 → 1 Pet 3:22) in `content/en/1-peter/CHAPTER-3.md`. For the formal citation of Psalm 110:1, see `content/en/acts/study/CHAPTER-2-PROPHECY.md` (Ps 110:1 → Acts 2:34–35).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Psalm 110 is a royal psalm — "YHWH said to my lord, 'Sit at my right hand'" — read of the Davidic king enthroned under God's protection, with his enemies subdued; the "my lord" (*ladonî*) is the king, and the psalm celebrates the king's God-given victory and (v.4) priestly status "after the order of Melchizedek." Its application to a Messiah exalted in heaven over angelic powers is the Christian reading, not the Jewish one, which keeps the psalm within the royal/Davidic frame. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Psalm 110:1 is read as the foremost prophecy of the Messiah's exaltation — the risen Christ seated at God's right hand until all enemies are subdued (cited at Mt 22:44; Acts 2:34–35; 1 Cor 15:25; Heb 1:13); 1 Peter 3:22 is treated as the affirmation of that enthronement, with "angels and authorities and powers" subjected — the cosmic victory completing the descent-and-proclamation of vv.18–19. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam affirms God's supreme sovereignty and that all creation, including the angels, is subject to God's command (cf. the angels who prostrate, Surah 2:34); but the enthronement of the Messiah at God's "right hand" with the powers subjected to him is not part of the Quranic presentation, which honors Isa (Jesus) as a prophet raised to God (Surah 4:158), not as the cosmic Lord enthroned. [DOCUMENTED]
