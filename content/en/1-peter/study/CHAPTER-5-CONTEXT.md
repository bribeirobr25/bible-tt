## A. Source Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. "Fellow-elder" — the author's self-lowering
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The author addresses the elders not from above but alongside, calling himself **συμπρεσβύτερος** (*sympresbyteros*, "fellow-elder," 5:1) — a compound (*syn-* "with" + *presbyteros* "elder") that places him in the same office he is exhorting. The same verse stacks two further self-descriptions, **μάρτυς** (*martys*, "witness") of the Messiah's sufferings and **κοινωνός** (*koinōnos*, "sharer / partner") of the coming glory, so that authority is expressed as shared experience rather than rank. The TT keeps "fellow-elder" rather than smoothing to "elder" or "apostle," so the self-leveling — which the chapter then turns into its theme of humility (v.5) — stays visible.

### A2. "Overseeing" inside the elder-charge — the fluid leadership vocabulary
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The charge to the **πρεσβύτεροι** (*presbyteroi*, "elders," 5:1) is to "shepherd the flock… **ἐπισκοποῦντες** (*episkopountes*, 'overseeing')" (5:2), placing the *presbyteros* word-group and the *episkopos* ("overseer") word-group in one breath, describing one task. The participle *episkopountes* is itself textually contested — it is bracketed in NA28, absent from some major witnesses (see §G2) — but where read, it shows the two later-distinct titles ("presbyter / priest" and "bishop / overseer") functioning here as overlapping descriptions of the same persons. The TT renders "elders… overseeing" rather than importing the developed offices, keeping the first-century fluidity (§I1) on the surface of the text.

### A3. The triple "not… but" of the pastoral charge
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The charge of 5:2-3 is built as three matched contrasts, each rejecting a motive and naming its opposite: not **ἀναγκαστῶς** ("by compulsion") but **ἑκουσίως** ("willingly"); not **αἰσχροκερδῶς** ("for shameful gain") but **προθύμως** ("eagerly"); not **κατακυριεύοντες** ("lording it over") the charges but **τύποι γινόμενοι** ("becoming examples"). The rhetorical symmetry frames eldership entirely by inner disposition rather than by the powers of the office. The TT preserves the three-fold parallel rather than collapsing it into a single exhortation, so the deliberate structure stays audible.

### A4. "Those allotted to you" — *klēroi*, the same root the office-vocabulary carries
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexeme); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the precise referent)]**

At 5:3 the elders are told not to lord it over **τῶν κλήρων** (*tōn klērōn*, "the *klēroi*"), the plural of *klēros*, "lot / portion / allotment" — the same word that carries the office-and-lot wordplay elsewhere in the corpus (cf. Acts 1:17, 25-26). Here it most plausibly means "those allotted to your care," the congregations apportioned to each elder, though "the portions / charges" assigned to them is also defensible. The TT renders "those allotted *to you*" and marks the supplied pronoun in italics, keeping the allotment-image rather than flattening to "your congregation."

### A5. Four piled-up verbs — "restore, support, strengthen, establish"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The promise of 5:10 stacks four future verbs with one subject (God, emphatic **αὐτός**, "himself"): **καταρτίσει, στηρίξει, σθενώσει, θεμελιώσει** (*katartisei, stērixei, sthenōsei, themeliōsei*) — "will restore / mend, support, strengthen, establish." *Katartizō* carries a "mend / put in order" sense (used of mending nets, Mark 1:19); *themelioō* is a building-word ("lay a foundation"). The TT keeps all four distinct verbs rather than compressing them into a single "strengthen," so the cumulative weight of the promise — set against "*after you have* suffered a little" — stays on the page.

### A6. The epistolary close and the speech-marking decision (Rule 30)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The chapter's only quoted scripture, Proverbs 3:34 LXX at 5:5 ("God opposes *the* proud, but to *the* humble he gives grace"), is the **sage's** third-person statement *about* God, not God's own first-person speech, and so — consistent with the book's Rule-30 policy (citations *about* the Lord stay unmarked; only God's first-person words take `@@…@@`) — it is left unmarked. The chapter contains no narrated divine speech at all; its voice is the author's exhortation throughout, including the closing greetings (vv.12-14). The TT therefore carries no `@@…@@` marking in this chapter, and the verse-notes record the reasoning rather than leaving the absence unexplained.

## B. Ancient Near Eastern and Ancient World Parallels

### B1. "Babylon" as a cipher for Rome — the exile-empire read onto the present
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The greeting from "she… in **Babylon** (Bavel)" (5:13) is most widely read as a coded name for **Rome**: by the late first century "Babylon" had become a recognizable Jewish and Christian cipher for the imperial power that had destroyed Jerusalem, exactly as the historic Babylon had destroyed the first Temple (so Revelation 17-18; 4 Ezra 3:1-2, 28-31; 2 Baruch 11:1; Sibylline Oracles 5.143, 159). The usage fits the letter's whole framing of its readers as "resident-aliens and sojourners" in dispersion (1:1, 17; 2:11) — Rome becomes the exile-empire, and the church a people living in its "Babylon." A minority of interpreters take it literally (the city of Babylon in Mesopotamia, or the Roman military camp of that name in Egypt), but the symbolic reading best suits both the genre and the period.

**Source:** Hunzinger, C.-H., "Babylon als Deckname für Rom und die Datierung des 1. Petrusbriefes," in *Gottes Wort und Gottes Land* (ed. H.G. Reventlow), Vandenhoeck & Ruprecht, 1965, pp. 67-77; Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, Fortress Press, 1996, pp. 353-354.

### B2. Proverbs 3:34 as a shared early-Christian "humility" testimony
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

The citation at 5:5, **ὁ θεὸς ὑπερηφάνοις ἀντιτάσσεται, ταπεινοῖς δὲ δίδωσιν χάριν** ("God opposes *the* proud, but to *the* humble he gives grace"), is Proverbs 3:34 in the Septuagint form — which reads "God" (*theos*) where the Hebrew has "the LORD" (YHWH) — and the *identical* wording appears at James 4:6, with the proud/humble contrast also echoed at 1 Clement 30:2. The shared, verbatim use of the LXX form by two independent letters points to a common stock of early-Christian moral teaching ("testimonia") drawn on by more than one author, rather than direct literary borrowing between them. The TT renders the citation as the sage's voice (unmarked, §A6) and notes the shared tradition without claiming a direction of dependence.

**Source:** Davids, P.H., *The First Epistle of Peter*, NICNT, Eerdmans, 1990, pp. 184-185; Bauckham, R., *James*, Routledge, 1999, pp. 29-35 (on shared paraenetic tradition).

### B3. The "roaring lion" as a stock image of the menacing adversary
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The adversary "like a **roaring lion** (*leōn ōryomenos*) seeking *someone* to devour" (5:8) draws on a deep biblical image-pool in which the lion stands for a deadly, encircling threat: Psalm 22:13 ("they opened their mouths against me, a ravening and roaring lion"), Psalm 7:2, Ezekiel 22:25, and the lions' den of Daniel 6. Second-Temple and early-Christian texts likewise picture hostile or satanic powers as devouring beasts (cf. the imagery of *Testament of Naphtali* 8:4-6; 2 Timothy 4:17). The image conveys menace and appetite, not a specific zoological claim; the TT keeps "roaring lion" so the Psalm-22 resonance and the predator-symbolism stay audible.

**Source:** Strawn, B.A., *What Is Stronger than a Lion? Leonine Image and Metaphor in the Hebrew Bible and the Ancient Near East*, Academic Press Fribourg / Vandenhoeck & Ruprecht, 2005, pp. 58-65; Michaels, J.R., *1 Peter*, Word Biblical Commentary 49, Word, 1988, pp. 297-299.

## C. Historical Context

### C1. Elders and the household-shaped early congregation
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The "elders" addressed in 5:1-4 reflect a leadership form drawn from both the synagogue and the Greco-Roman household: the Jewish *zĕqēnim* / *presbyteroi* who governed local communities, and the *paterfamilias*-shaped authority of the house-church meeting in a domestic setting. In the mid-first century these assemblies were small, met in homes, and had no formalized clergy, no buildings, and no fixed titles of the later kind; "elder" named senior, responsible men rather than an ordained order. Against this setting, a charge to shepherd "willingly… not for shameful gain" (5:2) addresses real local leaders whose authority was personal and easily abused, which is why the letter frames it by motive (§A3) rather than by office.

**Source:** Elliott, J.H., *1 Peter*, Anchor Bible 37B, Doubleday, 2000, pp. 815-841; Campbell, R.A., *The Elders: Seniority within Earliest Christianity*, T&T Clark, 1994, pp. 97-119.

### C2. The five provinces, dispersion, and the suffering "in the world"
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The "same sufferings… being accomplished for your brotherhood in the world" (5:9) presupposes the letter's recipients — believers scattered across Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia (1:1), the provinces of northern and central Asia Minor. The trouble the letter addresses is most plausibly local, social hostility — slander, ostracism, and sporadic accusation against a suspect new group (cf. 2:12; 3:16; 4:14-16) — rather than an empire-wide, state-organized persecution, for which there is no evidence at this period. The detail of a "brotherhood in the world" (*adelphotēs en kosmō*) sharing the same trials presents these dispersed congregations as a single, trans-regional community, an early sense of the church as a worldwide body.

**Source:** Horrell, D.G., *1 Peter*, New Testament Guides, T&T Clark, 2008, pp. 41-58; Williams, T.B., *Persecution in 1 Peter: Differentiating and Contextualizing Early Christian Suffering*, Brill, 2012, pp. 179-236.

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. *Tapeinophrosynē* — "humility," a word the wider culture despised
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexeme); COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE (for the cultural valuation)]**

At 5:5 all are told to "**clothe yourselves with humility** (*tēn tapeinophrosynēn enkombōsasthe*)," using **ταπεινοφροσύνη** (*tapeinophrosynē*) and the rare verb *enkombōomai* ("tie on" a garment, as a slave's apron). The word-group *tapeino-* in classical and Hellenistic Greek usually carried a *negative* charge — "low, base, servile, mean-spirited" — so what later Christian usage made a virtue would have struck many Greco-Roman ears as the disposition of a slave, not a value to cultivate (§I2). The TT renders "humility" and the verse-note flags the counter-cultural force; the choice of *enkombōsasthe* ("tie on like an apron") may further recall a servant girding to serve (cf. John 13:4-5), reinforcing the lowliness the surrounding honor-culture scorned.

**Source:** Liddell, H.G. and Scott, R., *Greek-English Lexicon*, 9th ed., rev. Jones, 1940, s.v. ταπεινός, ταπεινοφροσύνη; Spicq, C., *Theological Lexicon of the New Testament*, trans. J.D. Ernest, Hendrickson, 1994, vol. 3, pp. 369-371.

### D2. *Antidikos* and *diabolos* — the legal opponent and the slanderer
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexemes); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE (for the satan-figure referent)]**

The adversary of 5:8 is named twice: **ἀντίδικος** (*antidikos*, literally "opponent-at-law," the other party in a lawsuit) and **διάβολος** (*diabolos*, "slanderer / accuser," from *diaballō*, "to throw across, accuse"). Both terms map onto the Second-Temple development of "the *satan*" (Hebrew *śāṭān*, "accuser / adversary") into a personalized accusing figure — the courtroom prosecutor of Job 1-2 and Zechariah 3:1, rendered *diabolos* in the LXX, and the tester/accuser of later Jewish thought. The TT renders the meaning, "*the* slanderer," noting the familiar association behind the loanword "devil," and keeps "adversary" for *antidikos* so the legal-opponent image is not lost.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. ἀντίδικος, διάβολος; Bauer, W., *A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament* (BDAG), 3rd ed., rev. Danker, University of Chicago Press, 2000, s.v. διάβολος; Day, P.L., *An Adversary in Heaven: śāṭān in the Hebrew Bible*, Scholars Press, 1988, pp. 25-43.

### D3. *Archipoimēn* and *amarantinos* — the chief Shepherd and the unfading crown
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Verse 4 coins two rare words. **ἀρχιποίμην** (*archipoimēn*, "chief Shepherd") is otherwise unattested in the Greek Bible (a NT *hapax legomenon*), formed like other *archi-* compounds (*archiereus*, "chief priest"); it sets the Messiah over the elder-"shepherds" of v.2, tying back to "the shepherd and overseer of your souls" (2:25). **ἀμαράντινος** (*amarantinos*, "unfading," 5:4) echoes the inheritance described as **ἀμάραντος** (*amaranton*, "unfading," 1:4) — the *amaranton* being the legendary never-wilting flower — so the elders' "crown of glory" is marked as imperishable in contrast to a withering victor's wreath. The TT keeps both rare terms in their literal force ("chief Shepherd," "unfading crown") rather than naturalizing them, preserving the link to 1:4 and 2:25.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. ποιμήν, ἀμάραντος; Bauer, *BDAG*, s.v. ἀρχιποίμην, ἀμαράντινος.

### D4. *Krataia cheir* — the "mighty hand" of the Exodus
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexeme); COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE (for the Exodus idiom)]**

"Humble yourselves under the **mighty hand** (*krataia cheir*) of God" (5:6) uses a phrase that is the Septuagint's standing idiom for God's saving and judging power in the Exodus — "with a mighty hand" recurs of the deliverance from Egypt (Exod 3:19; 6:1; Deut 9:26; 26:8). The image holds together two directions at once: the hand that humbles is the same hand that "lifts up in *due* time" (*hypsōsē en kairō*, 5:6), so submission is to a power that both disciplines and delivers. The TT keeps "mighty hand" literally rather than paraphrasing ("God's power"), so the Exodus resonance and the humble-now / exalted-then pattern stay visible.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. κραταιός, χείρ; Hatch, E. and Redpath, H.A., *A Concordance to the Septuagint*, 2nd ed., Baker, 1998, s.v. κραταιός; Elliott, J.H., *1 Peter*, Anchor Bible 37B, 2000, pp. 850-852.

## F. Later Reception in Other Traditions

### F1. "Through Silvanus" and the secretary/authorship debate
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The phrase **διὰ Σιλουανοῦ… ἔγραψα** ("**through Silvanus** I wrote," 5:12) has been read in two main ways across the history of interpretation: traditionally, as naming Silvanus (Silas) the **amanuensis (secretary)** who composed the polished Greek on Peter's behalf — a reading used to reconcile Petrine authorship with the letter's accomplished style; and, in much modern scholarship, as the conventional formula identifying the **letter-bearer / courier** (the idiom *graphein dia* + person regularly designates the carrier, e.g. in Ignatius and the papyri), which leaves the composition question untouched. The matter connects to the wider debate over authorship (apostolic, secretarial, or pseudonymous) set out in the Introduction (§B); the TT renders "through Silvanus" without deciding the role and flags the options in Tier-2. These are interpretive and reception-historical positions, not data the verse itself settles.

**Source:** Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, Fortress Press, 1996, pp. 349-352; Richards, E.R., *The Secretary in the Letters of Paul*, Mohr Siebeck, 1991, pp. 169-189; Elliott, J.H., *1 Peter*, Anchor Bible 37B, 2000, pp. 872-874.

### F2. "Mark my son," Peter, and the Gospel of Mark
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The greeting from "**Mark** my son" (*Markos ho huios mou*, 5:13) was connected early to John Mark (Acts 12:12, 25) and, through him, to the **Gospel of Mark**: Papias (early 2nd century, in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15) reports that Mark was Peter's "interpreter" (*hermēneutēs*) who recorded Peter's preaching, and later writers (Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria) repeat and elaborate the Peter-Mark link. "Son" here is understood as spiritual kinship (a convert or close associate), not a biological claim — the same figurative use as Paul's "my son" of Timothy (1 Tim 1:2). These are documented patristic traditions about the connection, not statements made by 1 Peter itself, which says only "Mark my son"; the TT renders the plain greeting and leaves the Gospel-of-Mark tradition to its history.

**Source:** Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15, Loeb Classical Library; Hengel, M., *Studies in the Gospel of Mark*, trans. J. Bowden, Fortress Press, 1985, pp. 47-53; Bauckham, R., *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, Eerdmans, 2006, pp. 202-239.

### F3. "Babylon," Peter at Rome, and the topography of his ministry
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

Reading "Babylon" (5:13) as Rome (§B1) dovetailed with the early and widespread tradition that Peter ministered and died at Rome: 1 Clement 5-6 (c. 96 CE) speaks of Peter's martyrdom, Ignatius (*Romans* 4:3) links Peter and Paul to the Roman church, and later writers (Irenaeus, Eusebius) name Rome as the place of his death under Nero. From late antiquity the tradition attached itself to specific sites (the Vatican necropolis; later veneration there), and the Babylon-equals-Rome reading of 5:13 became a standard support for it. These are reception traditions of varying historical weight; the verse itself names only "Babylon," and the TT keeps that name while noting the Rome equivalence as the probable sense (§B1), not asserting the biographical tradition as fact.

**Source:** Bockmuehl, M., *Simon Peter in Scripture and Memory*, Baker Academic, 2012, pp. 114-132; Cullmann, O., *Peter: Disciple, Apostle, Martyr*, 2nd ed., trans. F.V. Filson, SCM Press, 1962, pp. 71-157.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. A "fellow-elder" who was an apostle — title and self-presentation
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the data); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the explanation)]**

The opening (1:1) names the author "apostle of Yeshua *the* Messiah," yet here he addresses the elders as their **fellow**-elder (5:1, §A1), not as their apostolic superior. Whether this is genuine self-effacement enacting the very humility the chapter commands (v.5), a rhetorical strategy to make the charge land among peers, or a trace of the author's actual relation to these communities is debated and not decidable from the text. The TT keeps "fellow-elder" and leaves the motive open; the juxtaposition with "apostle" in 1:1 is itself the curiosity.

### G2. The bracketed "overseeing" — a real text-critical datum
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The participle **ἐπισκοποῦντες** (*episkopountes*, "overseeing," 5:2) is bracketed in NA28: it is read by major witnesses but omitted by others (including Vaticanus and the original hand of Sinaiticus), so its originality is genuinely uncertain. Its presence or absence does not change the sense of the charge (the elders shepherd either way), but it bears on §A2 — whether the verse explicitly overlaps the *presbyteros* and *episkopos* word-groups, or only does so implicitly. The TT follows the bracketed reading and the verse-note records the variant rather than hiding it.

**Source:** Metzger, B.M., *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*, 2nd ed., German Bible Society, 1994, pp. 625-626.

### G3. "She… chosen-together" — a church, a woman, or Peter's wife?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the data); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the referent)]**

The greeter of 5:13 is **ἡ… συνεκλεκτή** ("the *co-elect* [feminine]"), with no noun supplied — most read it as "the (sister-)church" in Babylon (feminine *ekklēsia*, "assembly," cf. the "elect lady" of 2 John 1, 13), but a minority have taken the feminine literally as an individual woman, even Peter's wife. The Greek leaves the noun unexpressed, so the church-reading is an inference (well-supported, given the corporate greeting and the 2 John parallel) rather than a stated fact. The TT supplies "*who is*" in italics and keeps the feminine "She," leaving the referent for the reader to weigh.

## I. The World at the Time

> **Cross-reference:** For the broad early-imperial Greco-Roman world of the letter's recipients — politics, economy, daily life, and religion under the early Principate — see the John and Acts companions' Section I (`en/john/study/CHAPTER-1-CONTEXT.md`; `en/acts/study/CHAPTER-1-CONTEXT.md`). 1 Peter writes to dispersed believers in Asia Minor (Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, Bithynia, 1:1) in the second half of the first century. The entries below address the specific features of that world foregrounded by the closing chapter: early-church leadership structures, the honor-culture against which "humility" stands, the conventions of letter-closing, and "Babylon" as a name for Rome.

---

### I1. Social Structure — Early-church leadership: elders, overseers, and fluid terminology
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

In the mid-to-late first century, leadership in the scattered assemblies was still informal and its vocabulary unfixed: "elders" (*presbyteroi*), "overseers" (*episkopoi*), and "shepherds/pastors" (*poimenes*) named overlapping functions rather than the distinct, ranked offices of the later church, and 1 Peter 5:1-2 places the elder- and overseer-words in a single charge (§A2). The model drew on the synagogue's council of elders and on the patron-and-household structures of Greco-Roman society, with congregations meeting in homes under the senior figures of the local community. The clear separation of "bishop," "presbyter," and "deacon" emerges more sharply only in the early second century (e.g. in Ignatius of Antioch); the letter reflects the earlier, more fluid stage.

**Source:** Campbell, R.A., *The Elders: Seniority within Earliest Christianity*, T&T Clark, 1994, pp. 97-119, 156-179; Elliott, J.H., *1 Peter*, Anchor Bible 37B, Doubleday, 2000, pp. 815-841.

### I2. Social Structure — Honor, patronage, and why "humility" was countercultural
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Greco-Roman society was organized around honor and shame and around webs of patronage, in which status was publicly competed for and displayed, and a patron's superiority over clients was assumed and advertised. In this frame the *tapeino-* word-group ("low, humble, servile") was largely a term of contempt — the bearing of slaves and the defeated, not a quality the honorable cultivated (§D1). For the letter's first hearers, then, a command that elders not "lord it over" their charges (5:3) and that all "clothe themselves with humility" toward one another and "humble" themselves under God (5:5-6) ran directly against the dominant social grammar, redefining a despised trait as the community's central virtue.

**Source:** Malina, B.J., *The New Testament World: Insights from Cultural Anthropology*, 3rd ed., Westminster John Knox, 2001, pp. 27-57; deSilva, D.A., *Honor, Patronage, Kinship & Purity: Unlocking New Testament Culture*, InterVarsity Press, 2000, pp. 23-93.

### I3. Daily Life — Letter-closing conventions: the bearer, greetings, and the holy kiss
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The close of 1 Peter (5:12-14) follows the standard conventions of the ancient letter: a note about the bearer or secretary (the *graphein dia* formula, "through Silvanus," §F1), a statement of the letter's brevity and purpose, exchanged greetings between communities, and a final benediction. The "kiss of love" (5:14) reflects the early-Christian "holy kiss," a greeting used at gatherings and attested across the New Testament letters (Rom 16:16; 1 Cor 16:20; 2 Cor 13:12; 1 Thess 5:26) and in second-century sources (Justin, *First Apology* 65). Greek and Latin letters of the period regularly closed with conveyed greetings, health-wishes, and a farewell formula, and 1 Peter's close maps onto this shared epistolary etiquette while filling it with the community's own content (grace, peace, the kiss of *agapē*).

**Source:** Klauck, H.-J., *Ancient Letters and the New Testament: A Guide to Context and Exegesis*, trans. D.P. Bailey, Baylor University Press, 2006, pp. 17-42; Weima, J.A.D., *Neglected Endings: The Significance of the Pauline Letter Closings*, JSOT Press, 1994, pp. 110-144.

### I4. Religion — "Babylon" and Rome: the empire as the place of exile
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

By the later first century, after Rome's destruction of Jerusalem and its Temple in 70 CE, Jewish and Christian writers reached for "Babylon" — the empire that had destroyed the *first* Temple — as a name for Rome, the new imperial oppressor (cf. Revelation 17-18; 4 Ezra; 2 Baruch; §B1). The usage encodes a whole reading of the present: God's people again live as exiles under a great pagan power, awaiting deliverance, which fits 1 Peter's address to "resident-aliens and sojourners" of the dispersion (1:1, 17; 2:11). For readers in Asia Minor, a greeting "from Babylon" (5:13) located the sending church within that same exile-experience and, on the dominant reading, identified its place as Rome — traditionally the city of Peter's later ministry (§F3).

**Source:** Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, Fortress Press, 1996, pp. 353-354; Goodman, M., *Rome and Jerusalem: The Clash of Ancient Civilizations*, Allen Lane, 2007, pp. 451-468.
