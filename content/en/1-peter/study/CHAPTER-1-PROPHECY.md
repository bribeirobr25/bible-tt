## Note on 1 Peter's mode of allusion (an epistle, not a narrative)

1 Peter is a **letter** — *argument and exhortation* in the apostle's own voice, not a narrative of events fulfilling prediction. So its use of the Hebrew Bible is not "prophecy → fulfillment" in the gospel-and-Acts sense. The letter **deploys** the Hebrew Bible *paraenetically and typologically*: it **quotes** it to ground a command ("be holy, for I am holy," 1:16, from Leviticus; "all flesh is grass… the word of the Lord endures," 1:24–25, from Isaiah 40), it **applies type-scenes** of Israel's story to the readers (the Passover lamb, the Exodus readiness-posture, the covenant-blood sprinkled), and it **echoes** the diaspora/exile identity onto its scattered audience. The dual-label and citation-vs-allusion system carries over unchanged from the gospel companions; what shifts is how to read the **status line**. Here "fulfillment status" should be read as **mode of deployment**:

- **CLAIMED** ≈ **explicit quotation** — the letter cites the OT with a citation marker ("it is written," 1:16; the unmarked-but-verbatim Isaiah of 1:24–25) and presses it as authoritative for the readers.
- **PARTIAL** ≈ **typological application** — the letter reads an Israel institution or event (Passover lamb, covenant blood) as the pattern realized in the Messiah, without quoting a verse.
- **DEBATED** ≈ **echo** — the diction or posture of an OT scene is interiorized into the exhortation (the girded loins of Exodus), without citation or fulfillment-claim.

Because 1 Peter quotes the OT in the *third* person about God as often as it carries God's *first*-person word, **speech-marking (Rule 30 / A-004)** is selective: the letter is the apostle's **human** exhortation throughout and is not marked; only where the *source verse* is God's own first-person word does the quotation carry `@@…@@`. In 1 Peter 1 that is the **single** marked saying — "@@Be holy, because I *am* holy@@" (1:16, Leviticus). The Isaiah 40 citation (1:24–25) is the **prophet's** human voice *about* the Lord and is therefore **not** marked. Where κύριος in a citation stands for YHWH, the entries note it per **Option C** (the main text renders "the Lord" + Tier-2; the source-referent is YHWH; P-006/§ κύριος Option C in `1-peter.md`).

## Leviticus 11:44–45; 19:2 — "Be holy, for I am holy" (1 Peter 1:16)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 1:16
**Text says:** "but according to the holy *One* who called you, you yourselves also be holy in all *your* conduct, because it is written, @@'Be holy, because I *am* holy.'@@"
**Context:** The first imperative-cluster of the letter's body (1:13–21). Having girded the mind for action (1:13) and renounced the former desires (1:14), the readers are summoned to holiness "in all *your* conduct" (*anastrophē*), and the command is grounded by a formal citation of God's own word in the Torah. The quoted line is **God's direct first-person word** in Leviticus, and is therefore marked `@@…@@` (Rule 30 / A-004) — the chapter's one divine-marked saying.
**Subject:** The holiness of God as the ground of the people's holiness. 1 Peter quotes the holiness-code refrain — **Leviticus 19:2** ("you shall be holy, for I YHWH your God *am* holy"), repeated across the code (**Leviticus 11:44–45**; 20:7, 26). The Greek **Ἅγιοι ἔσεσθε, ὅτι ἐγὼ ἅγιος** (*Hagioi esesthe, hoti egō hagios*, "Be holy, because I *am* holy") follows the LXX wording. In Leviticus the speaker is YHWH addressing Israel at Sinai (the refrain that seals the holiness code, "I YHWH your God"); 1 Peter lifts that first-person word and applies it to **the scattered believers** — the holiness once grounding Israel's separated vocation now grounds the conduct of the "elect sojourners of the dispersion" (1:1). The copula *am* is added in italics (the Greek is verbless; Rule 11).
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED *(mode: explicit quotation)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This **is the chapter's one formal, marked citation** — introduced "because it is written" (*dioti gegraptai*) — and because the source verse is YHWH's own first-person word ("I *am* holy"), it is marked `@@…@@` (Rule 30 / A-004; the only such marking in the chapter). The deployment is **paraenetic**, not predictive: 1 Peter does not say a prophecy was fulfilled; it *applies* the Torah's holiness command directly to the readers' present conduct (the imperative *esesthe* read as command). The interpretive move the letter makes — transferring the holiness-code address from Israel-at-Sinai to the diaspora believers — is the letter's own appropriation, consonant with its diaspora-identity framing (1:1, 17; 2:9–10, which gathers Exod 19:6 / Isa "chosen people" language); the TT renders the citation as 1 Peter gives it and flags the application (1:16 note). **[textual — VERIFIED (citation); the application to the believers — CLAIMED]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Lev 11:44–45; 19:2 → 1 Pet 1:16) in `content/en/1-peter/CHAPTER-1.md`.
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Leviticus 19:2 (and the 11:44–45 refrain) is the heart of the holiness code (*Qedoshim*) — "you shall be holy, for I YHWH your God am holy" — Israel's call to imitate God's holiness through the whole life of the community, the ethical and ritual commandments that follow. Holiness is the vocation of Israel as a people set apart; the command is read within the covenant at Sinai. Its transfer to a gentile-and-Jewish diaspora church is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The verse is read as the abiding ground of Christian holiness — the character of the holy God now reproduced in the lives of those "called" in the Messiah; 1 Peter 1:16 is treated as the apostolic application of the Levitical command to the church as the new people of God (cf. the "holy nation," 1 Pet 2:9 / Exod 19:6). [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam affirms the holiness (*quddūs*) of God — al-Quddūs is one of the divine names (cf. Surah 59:23; 62:1) — and the call to purity and righteous conduct; the specific Levitical refrain "be holy, for I am holy" and its application to a church are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Isaiah 40:6–8 — "all flesh is grass… the word of the Lord endures" (1 Peter 1:24–25)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 1:24–25
**Text says:** "having been born again, not from perishable seed but imperishable, through *the* living and abiding word of God. For 'all flesh *is* like grass, and all its glory like *the* flower of grass; the grass withered, and the flower fell off, but the word of *the* Lord endures into the age.' And this is the word that was preached as good news to you."
**Context:** The close of the chapter's exhortation to love (1:22–25). The new birth comes "through *the* living and abiding word of God" (1:23), and that claim is sealed with a citation contrasting the transience of "all flesh" with the permanence of "the word of the Lord" — which 1 Peter then **identifies with the gospel** ("this is the word that was preached as good news to you," 1:25b). The quoted words are the **prophet's** human voice *about* the Lord (third person, "the word of *the* Lord endures"), so they are **not** divine-marked (Rule 30).
**Subject:** The enduring word of YHWH over against the withering of all flesh. 1 Peter quotes **Isaiah 40:6–8** (LXX) — the opening of the prophecies of comfort, where a voice cries that "all flesh *is* grass… the grass withers, the flower fades, but the word of our God stands forever." The Greek **πᾶσα σὰρξ ὡς χόρτος… τὸ δὲ ῥῆμα κυρίου μένει εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα** (*pasa sarx hōs chortos… to de rhēma kyriou menei eis ton aiōna*) follows the LXX, with 1 Peter's "the word of *the* Lord (κυρίου)" where the Hebrew MT reads "the word of our God" (*dĕbar ʾĕlōhênû*) — κύριος here = **YHWH** (Option C). 1 Peter then makes the decisive move: "the word of *the* Lord" that endures **is** "the word that was preached as good news to you" — the gospel is identified with the enduring divine word of Isaiah 40. This is the **same Isaiah 40** whose "voice crying in the wilderness" (40:3) frames Yochanan the Immerser (John the Baptist) in the gospels (cf. the gospel cross-references).
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED *(mode: explicit quotation)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is a **verbatim quotation** (LXX Isa 40:6–8), though 1 Peter introduces it without a formula ("For 'all flesh…'," *dioti* + the citation) — its authority is assumed, and the catchword "word/seed" (*logos / sperma*, 1:23) and "abide/endure" (*menō*) bridge into it. The deployment is **paraenetic-and-applicative**, not predictive: 1 Peter does not claim Isaiah foretold an event now fulfilled; it **applies** Isaiah's contrast (transient flesh vs. enduring word) to the new birth and then **identifies** the enduring "word of the Lord" with the gospel preached to the readers — an interpretive identification the TT renders as 1 Peter gives it (1:25 note). On the divine name: in Isaiah, κύριος = YHWH (Option C); the TT renders "the Lord" in the main text and notes the YHWH referent (1:25 note). Because the citation is the prophet's voice *about* the Lord (third person), it is **not** marked `@@…@@` (Rule 30). **[textual — VERIFIED (citation); the gospel-as-the-enduring-word identification — CLAIMED]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Isa 40:6–8 → 1 Pet 1:24–25). For the same Isaiah 40 behind the Immerser's mission, see the gospel PROPHECY companions (Isa 40:3 → the wilderness voice).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Isaiah 40:6–8 opens *Nachamu* ("comfort") — the consolation prophecies to exiled Israel: human power and glory ("all flesh") wither like grass, but the word of God stands forever, assuring that the promise of restoration will hold. The enduring "word of our God" (MT) is read as God's covenant promise to Israel; the identification of that word with a Christian gospel is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Isaiah 40:6–8 is read as affirming the permanence of God's word against all that is mortal, and 1 Peter 1:24–25 as the apostolic identification of that enduring word with the gospel — the imperishable seed of the new birth (1:23), the same word that comforted exiles now proclaimed to the scattered church. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam affirms the transience of worldly life and the permanence of God's word and decree (cf. the Quran's frequent contrast of the fleeting *dunyā* with the lasting; Surah 18:45, the world's life like withering vegetation); the specific Isaiah-40 oracle and its identification with the Christian gospel are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Exodus 12; Leviticus (sacrifice); cf. Isaiah 53:7 — "a lamb without blemish" (1 Peter 1:18–19)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 1:18–19
**Text says:** "knowing that not with perishable *things* — silver or gold — were you ransomed from your futile way of life handed down from *the* fathers, but with *the* precious blood, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot, of *the* Messiah,"
**Context:** The ground of the call to reverent fear (1:17–21): the readers were "ransomed" (*lytroō*) not by money but by the Messiah's blood — figured as the blood of an unblemished lamb. The lamb-imagery is **typological application**, not a quotation: no verse is cited.
**Subject:** The Passover/sacrificial lamb realized in the Messiah's blood. The phrase **ἀμνοῦ ἀμώμου καὶ ἀσπίλου** (*amnou amōmou kai aspilou*, "a lamb without blemish and without spot") joins **ἀμνός** ("lamb") with **ἄμωμος** — the LXX's standard term for the **unblemished** sacrificial victim required by the cult (**Leviticus** passim; e.g. Lev 1:3, 10; 22:19–21, "without blemish… to be accepted") and specifically the **Passover lamb** of **Exodus 12:5** ("your lamb shall be without blemish [LXX *teleion*/*amōmon* tradition], a male a year old"). The "ransom" verb (*lytroō*, 1:18) draws on the **Exodus redemption** language (YHWH "redeems" Israel from Egypt). The image also resonates with the silent **lamb led to slaughter** of **Isaiah 53:7** (*hōs probaton… amnos*, the Servant). 1 Peter does not quote any of these; it **applies** the lamb-standard typologically: the Messiah is the unblemished victim whose blood is the ransom-price, replacing silver and gold.
**Fulfillment status:** PARTIAL *(mode: typological application)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **typology / allusion**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse and uses no formula; it **deploys** the cultic "without blemish" standard (*amōmos*) and the Passover/redemption type-scene as the pattern realized in the Messiah's blood. The connection is deliberate and lexically anchored: *amnos* + *amōmos* is the sacrificial-Passover vocabulary, and "ransomed… not with silver or gold" (1:18) evokes the Exodus redemption. Which type is foremost — the **Passover** lamb (Ex 12), the **daily/sacrificial** lamb (Lev), or the **Servant** "lamb" of Isaiah 53:7 — is not specified by the text and is not resolved here; 1 Peter blends the sacrificial field rather than citing one passage (1:19 note; Rule 13). The PARTIAL label marks this as typological application (the pattern read as realized in the Messiah), not an explicit quotation and not a fulfilled prediction. **[comparative parallel — PROBABLE (typology, not citation); the foremost type (Passover vs. sacrifice vs. Isa 53) — POSSIBLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ex 12; Lev → 1 Pet 1:18–19). For the Passover/Servant lamb in the gospel, cf. the "lamb of God" tradition (Jn 1:29).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: The Passover lamb (Exodus 12) and the unblemished sacrificial victims of the Levitical cult are central to Israel's worship — the Passover blood marking the houses for deliverance, the *tamim* ("without blemish") standard for acceptable offerings; redemption (*pidyon*) is YHWH's act for Israel. These are read within Israel's covenant worship; the application of the lamb-standard to a crucified Messiah as the ransom-price is not part of the Jewish reading. Isaiah 53's "lamb" is read of the Servant (collectively Israel or a righteous sufferer), not a sacrificial atonement in the Christian sense. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The Messiah is read as the true Passover lamb and the unblemished sacrifice (cf. 1 Cor 5:7, "Christ our Passover"; Jn 1:29, 19:36; Rev 5:6), whose blood ransoms the people — 1 Peter 1:18–19 is treated as the typological realization of Exodus 12 and the sacrificial code, fused with the Servant of Isaiah 53. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam honors Ibrahim's (Abraham's) sacrifice and the ransom of his son by a great offering (Surah 37:107), commemorated at Eid al-Adha, and affirms Isa (Jesus) as prophet and Messiah but **not** as a sacrificial atonement (the crucifixion as a real atoning death is rejected, Surah 4:157); the Passover-lamb / unblemished-sacrifice typology applied to the Messiah's blood is not part of the Islamic reading. [DOCUMENTED]

## Exodus 12:11 — "gird the loins" (1 Peter 1:13)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 1:13
**Text says:** "Therefore, having girded the loins of your mind, being sober, set your hope completely on the grace being brought to you at *the* revelation of Yeshua *the* Messiah (Jesus)."
**Context:** The hinge of the chapter — the first main imperative (1:13), turning the blessing-and-doxology (1:3–12) into exhortation. "Having girded the loins of your mind" sets the posture of readiness for everything that follows. The phrase is an **echo** of the Exodus scene, interiorized; no verse is cited.
**Subject:** The Exodus readiness-posture made interior. **ἀναζωσάμενοι τὰς ὀσφύας τῆς διανοίας ὑμῶν** (*anazōsamenoi tas osphyas tēs dianoias hymōn*, "having girded the loins of your mind") recalls the Passover-night instruction of **Exodus 12:11** — eat the lamb "with your loins girded (*tas osphyas… periezōsmenoi*, LXX), your sandals on your feet, your staff in your hand… in haste" — the posture of a people ready to move at YHWH's word. 1 Peter takes this physical readiness (tucking the long robe into the belt to run or work) and applies it to "the loins of your **mind**" (*dianoia*) — a moral-mental alertness toward "the grace being brought to you" at the Messiah's revelation. The Exodus diction is unmistakable; the application is metaphorical and inward.
**Fulfillment status:** DEBATED *(mode: echo)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion / echo**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse and uses no formula; the "gird the loins" idiom is a recognizable Exodus (and prophetic, cf. Jer 1:17; the "gird your loins" call) readiness-posture, here **interiorized** ("of your *mind*"). The connection is deployment, not prediction: the letter borrows the Exodus posture of a people poised for deliverance and presses it on its own diaspora readers, consonant with their exile-and-pilgrimage identity (1:1, 17). The DEBATED label marks this as an echo — diction borrowed and applied — carrying no fulfillment-claim and no explicit citation (Rule 13). **[comparative parallel — POSSIBLE; an interiorized idiom, no single oracle]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ex 12:11 → 1 Pet 1:13). The same readiness-idiom appears in the gospels (Lk 12:35, "let your loins be girded").
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Exodus 12:11 sets the Passover-night posture — loins girded, sandals on, staff in hand, eaten in haste — the readiness of Israel to depart Egypt at YHWH's command; "girding the loins" becomes a standard idiom for preparedness for action or for a task (cf. the prophets). It belongs to the exodus deliverance and the festival's enactment; its interiorizing application ("loins of the mind") toward a Messiah's revelation is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The phrase is read as a call to spiritual alertness and readiness for the Messiah's return — the Exodus posture of the pilgrim people applied to the church living as sojourners (cf. Lk 12:35; Eph 6:14); 1 Peter 1:13 frames the whole ethical exhortation that follows as the conduct of a people ready to move. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam values readiness, vigilance, and sobriety of mind in devotion and in awaiting the Day of Judgment; the specific Exodus "gird the loins" idiom and its application to the readers' hope in the Messiah's revelation are not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Exodus 24:8 — the blood of the covenant sprinkled (1 Peter 1:2)

**Verse:** 1 Peter 1:2
**Text says:** "according to *the* foreknowledge of God *the* Father, in sanctification of *the* {a:wind/spirit}, for obedience and sprinkling of *the* blood of Yeshua *the* Messiah (Jesus): may grace and peace be multiplied to you."
**Context:** The third clause of the triune-shaped salutation (1:1–2): the readers are elect "for obedience and sprinkling of the blood of Yeshua." The "sprinkling of blood" is an **echo / typology** of the Sinai covenant-ratification ritual; no verse is cited. (For the salutation as a whole, see editorial-log P-008 and Companion §A.)
**Subject:** The covenant-blood sprinkled on the people. **ῥαντισμὸν αἵματος Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ** (*rhantismon haimatos Iēsou Christou*, "sprinkling of *the* blood of Yeshua *the* Messiah") echoes the covenant-ratification at Sinai — **Exodus 24:8**, where Mosheh (Moses) takes the blood and **sprinkles it on the people**, saying "Behold, the blood of the covenant which YHWH has made with you." The Greek *rhantismos* / *rhantizō* is the LXX's sprinkling-language for covenant and purification rites (cf. also the sin-offering blood, Lev; the Day of Atonement). 1 Peter pairs "obedience" with "sprinkling of the blood" — the Sinai sequence (the people pledge "we will obey," then the blood is sprinkled, Ex 24:7–8) read as the pattern of the readers' entry into the new covenant. The blood here is the Messiah's; the type is the covenant-blood of Sinai (and, behind it, the purifying/consecrating sprinkled blood of the cult).
**Fulfillment status:** PARTIAL *(mode: typological application)*
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **typology / echo**, not a citation — 1 Peter quotes no verse and names no source; it **deploys** the covenant-blood sprinkling of Sinai (and the cult's purifying sprinkled blood) as the pattern realized in "the blood of Yeshua." The connection is lexically and structurally anchored: *rhantismos* ("sprinkling") is the covenant/purification term, and the **obedience-then-blood** pairing (1:2) mirrors the Sinai sequence of Exodus 24:7–8. Which sprinkling is foremost — the **covenant-ratification** blood (Ex 24:8) or the **purification/consecration** blood of the cult (Lev) — is not specified, and the salutation's compressed triune-shaped grammar (foreknowledge / sanctification / sprinkling) resists over-precise mapping; the TT renders the Greek without importing later dogma (Rule 3; P-008) and flags the echo (1:2 note). The PARTIAL label marks this as typological application (the Sinai/cult pattern read as realized in the Messiah's blood), not an explicit quotation and not a fulfilled prediction. **[comparative parallel — PROBABLE (typology); the foremost referent (Ex 24 covenant vs. Lev purification) — POSSIBLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Ex 24:8 → 1 Pet 1:2).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Exodus 24:8 is the ratification of the Sinai covenant — Moses sprinkles "the blood of the covenant" on the people after they pledge obedience ("all that YHWH has spoken we will do and obey," 24:7); sprinkled blood also consecrates and purifies in the priestly cult. These belong to Israel's covenant and worship; their application to the blood of a Messiah sealing a new covenant with a diaspora church is not part of the Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The "sprinkling of the blood" is read as the new-covenant counterpart to Sinai — the Messiah's blood ratifying and purifying the people for obedience (cf. Heb 9:18–22; 12:24, "the sprinkled blood"); 1 Peter 1:2 is treated as casting the readers' election in covenant-ratification terms. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Islam affirms God's covenants with the prophets and the people (the *mīthāq*), and the Sinai covenant with Musa and the Israelites is narrated; the specific blood-sprinkling ratification ritual and its application to the Messiah's blood are not part of the Quranic presentation, which does not frame atonement through sprinkled covenant-blood. [DOCUMENTED]
