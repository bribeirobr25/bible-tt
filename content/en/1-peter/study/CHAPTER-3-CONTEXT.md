## HOW TO USE THIS COMPANION

This file contains background material organized by topic. Each entry carries **two labels:**

**What kind of claim?** (tells you the source of the information)
- **TEXTUAL** — derived directly from the Greek text
- **STRONG INFERENCE** — logically entailed by the text, not stated explicitly
- **POSSIBLE INFERENCE** — one legitimate reading among others
- **COMPARATIVE PARALLEL** — attested in another ancient source
- **LATER RECEPTION** — post-biblical reading
- **HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL** — based on material or historical evidence
- **SPECULATION** — no strong textual or evidential basis

**How certain?** (tells you the confidence level)
- **VERIFIED** — strong evidence, wide agreement
- **PROBABLE** — well-supported but debated
- **POSSIBLE** — one reasonable reading among others
- **UNCERTAIN** — genuinely unclear
- **SPECULATIVE** — must be rare and clearly flagged
- **DOCUMENTED** — historically attested reception (for LATER RECEPTION entries)

**Reading principle:** Start with TEXTUAL entries — those come from the Greek itself. Then explore outward to parallels, reception, and comparison. The companion presents; you assess.

> **A note on this chapter's restraint.** 1 Peter 3 is the most interpretively loaded chapter of the letter. Three of its passages — the household code's second panel (vv.1-7), the proclamation to "the spirits in prison" (vv.18-20), and baptism "as antitype" (v.21) — carry centuries of contested reading. The companion's task here is to *lay out the options*, sourced and labeled, and **resolve none of them** (Rule 3; editorial-log P-006, P-007, P-009). Where you see several readings listed, that listing is the finding, not a step toward a verdict.

## A. Source Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. The household code's second panel — "likewise" and the missionary aim
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The chapter opens **ὁμοίως** (*homoiōs*, "likewise," 3:1), the connective that ties the wives-and-husbands material back to the servants of 2:18 and, before that, to the general "submit" of 2:13 — marking vv.1-7 as the second and third panels of a single **household code** (German *Haustafel*; cf. Col 3:18-4:1; Eph 5:21-6:9). The TT renders the code's governing verb **ὑποτάσσω** (*hypotassō*, "submit / be subordinate," 3:1, 5) literally and consistently — the same verb returns at 3:22 for the cosmic powers "subjected" to the Messiah, a deliberate frame the TT keeps visible.

The aim stated in the Greek is missionary, not merely domestic: a husband who "**disobeys the word**" (*apeithousin tō logō*, 3:1 — i.e. is not a believer) "**may be won**" (*kerdēthēsontai*, the commercial/missionary verb "gain," cf. 1 Cor 9:19-22) "**without a word**" (*aneu logou*) — through the wife's *anastrophē* ("conduct, way of life"), not through argument. The wordplay is exact: won *without a word* by conduct, since he disobeys *the word*. The TT renders the Greek and refers the interpretive history to §F, taking no position (Rule 3). → editorial-log P-009; §F1.

### A2. "The hidden person of the heart" and the relocation of *kosmos*
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Verses 3-4 turn on a single word played two ways. **κόσμος** (*kosmos*) means both "adornment / ornamentation" (its everyday sense, the root of "cosmetic") and, elsewhere, "world / ordered whole." Here the outward *kosmos* — the conventional triad of "braiding of hair and wearing of gold and putting on of garments" (3:3) — is contrasted with the true *kosmos*, **ὁ κρυπτὸς τῆς καρδίας ἄνθρωπος** (*ho kryptos tēs kardias anthrōpos*), "the hidden person of the heart" (3:4). The text does not forbid the triad; it *relocates* value from the visible to the interior.

That interior is "**a gentle and quiet spirit**" (*praeos kai hēsychiou pneumatos*), called **ἄφθαρτος** ("imperishable") — the same adjective used of the inheritance (1:4) and the seed (1:23), binding the wife's inner *kosmos* to the letter's eschatological vocabulary. Note that *pneuma* here is rendered **plain "spirit"** (human disposition), **not** the {a:wind/spirit} slash: no "wind" sense is live in a description of temperament. The TT keeps the *kosmos* play and the *aphthartos* echo audible. → For the moralists' adornment-triad that lies behind v.3, see §I3.

### A3. Sarah "calling him lord" — a human *kyrios*, not the divine name
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexeme); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the Genesis referent)]**

At 3:6 the matriarch is the proof-text: **ὡς Σάρρα ὑπήκουσεν τῷ Ἀβραάμ, κύριον αὐτὸν καλοῦσα** = "as Sarah (Sara) obeyed Avraham (Abraham), calling him **lord**." The noun is **κύριος** (*kyrios*), the same word rendered "the Lord" (= YHWH) at 3:12 and "Lord" (of the Messiah) at 3:15 — but here it is a **human honorific**, "lord / master," and the TT renders it lowercase to track the referent-shift across the chapter (contrast §A4, §A5).

The wording draws on **Genesis 18:12 LXX**, where Sarah says inwardly "my **lord** (*ho kyrios mou*) is older" — though 1 Peter reports her as *habitually* "calling him lord," a generalizing gloss the Genesis verse states only once. The readers (addressed as wives) "became **her children**" by "**doing good** (*agathopoiousai*) and not fearing any **terror**" (*ptoēsis*) — the last phrase echoing **Proverbs 3:25 LXX** ("do not fear sudden *ptoēsis*"). The TT renders the human "lord" and leaves the Genesis-and-Proverbs substructure to this note. → §I1 (the wife and the *paterfamilias*); §F1 (reception).

### A4. "Weaker vessel" and "co-heir" — the two claims in one sentence (v.7)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexemes); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the sense of "weaker")]**

Verse 7 addresses husbands and holds two terms in tension that the TT renders both, side by side, without harmonizing. The wife is **ἀσθενέστερον σκεῦος τὸ γυναικεῖον** (*asthenesteron skeuos to gynaikeion*), "the weaker vessel — the feminine one"; and she is **συγκληρονόμος… χάριτος ζωῆς** (*synklēronomos charitos zōēs*), "co-heir of *the* grace of life." *Skeuos* ("vessel, instrument, container") is a common metaphor for the person (cf. 2 Cor 4:7; 1 Thess 4:4); the comparative *asthenesteron* ("weaker") is undetermined by the verse — physical strength, social vulnerability, or a worth-claim are all grammatically possible.

The TT renders "weaker vessel" literally and **declines to fix its sense**, noting that the *same sentence* calls the wife a **co-heir**, an equal-inheritance term (cf. Rom 8:17) that cuts against any reading of "weaker" as lesser worth. The husband is told to "assign honor" (*aponemontes timēn*) and to dwell "according to knowledge" (*kata gnōsin*), with a severe sanction: "**so that your prayers may not be hindered**" (*egkoptesthai*, "be cut off / impeded"). The TT presents both claims and the sanction, and takes no position on how "weaker" is to be weighed (Rule 3). → editorial-log P-009; §F1, §F2.

### A5. "Sanctify the Messiah as Lord" — the κύριος referent-shift of Isaiah 8:13
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

At 3:14-15 the letter quotes **Isaiah 8:12-13**. Verse 14 takes Isaiah 8:12 verbatim from the LXX: "**do not fear their fear nor be troubled**" (*ton de phobon autōn mē phobēthēte mēde tarachthēte*). Then v.15 adapts Isaiah 8:13, where the LXX reads "**the Lord (κύριος = YHWH) himself, him you shall sanctify** (*hagiasate*)." 1 Peter writes **κύριον δὲ τὸν Χριστὸν ἁγιάσατε** — "but sanctify **the Messiah as Lord**," substituting "the Messiah" for the object of the verb.

The move applies a YHWH-text directly to the Messiah — a **κύριος referent-shift** of the kind the corpus tracks elsewhere (cf. Acts 2:36; the same Isaiah 8 reused at Rom 9:33; 1 Pet 2:8). The TT renders "sanctify the Messiah as Lord" and flags the shift in Tier-2 (Rule 13), neither asserting nor denying a christological identification beyond what the syntax carries: the object of the Isaianic "sanctify" is now named the Messiah. The following clause introduces **ἀπολογία** (*apologia*, "defense, reasoned answer") — a legal-sounding term, the source of English "apologetics" — bounded by "**gentleness and fear**" (v.16). → PROPHECY (Isa 8:12-13 → 1 Pet 3:14-15); §F3.

### A6. The flesh/spirit datives of v.18 — sphere or agency, kept open
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the ambiguity); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the sense)]**

The crux of v.18 is a balanced pair of **anarthrous datives**: **θανατωθεὶς μὲν σαρκὶ ζῳοποιηθεὶς δὲ πνεύματι** (*thanatōtheis men sarki zōopoiētheis de pneumati*), "**put to death in flesh** (*sarki*) but **made alive in {a:wind/spirit}** (*pneumati*)." A Greek dative without a preposition can mark either **sphere** ("in the realm of flesh / in the realm of spirit") or **means / agency** ("by the flesh / by the Spirit"). The two datives are parallel (*men… de*), which pressures them to function the same way — but the construction does not say which way.

The TT keeps the **{a:wind/spirit}** slash on *pneumati* (the wind/spirit field is live in this letter, cf. 1:2, 11; 4:6, 14) and preserves the flesh/spirit parallel, **resolving neither the sphere/agency question nor whether *pneumati* names the divine Spirit or the Messiah's own spirit/mode of existence** (Rule 13). The same dative governs v.19's "in which" (*en hō*), so the reading of v.18 directly conditions the reading of the spirits-in-prison clause (§A7). → editorial-log P-006.

### A7. "Proclaimed to the spirits in prison" — the readings the text leaves open
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the data); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the referent)]**

Verse 19 is the single most debated clause in the letter: **ἐν ᾧ καὶ τοῖς ἐν φυλακῇ πνεύμασιν πορευθεὶς ἐκήρυξεν** = "in which also, having gone, he **proclaimed** (*ekēryxen*) to the **spirits in prison** (*tois en phylakē pneumasin*)." Every load-bearing term is underdetermined, and the TT renders the clause literally and **lists, without choosing among, the major readings**:

- **Who are "the spirits"?** (a) The **disobedient human dead of Noah's generation** (taking v.20's "who once disobeyed… in the days of Noach" as their identity) — souls of the flood-generation. (b) The **disobedient angelic beings / "watchers" of Genesis 6:1-4**, imprisoned for their transgression — the reading anchored in the Second-Temple **Enochic tradition** (1 Enoch 6-21; cf. 2 Peter 2:4 "angels… cast into Tartarus, kept in chains"; Jude 6 "kept in eternal chains for the judgment of the great day"). (c) On a third reading the "going and proclaiming" is the **pre-incarnate Messiah preaching through Noah** to that generation while the ark was built.
- **When did the proclamation happen?** Between death and resurrection (the *en hō pneumati* of v.18 read as the disembodied or spiritual mode), or — on reading (c) — in Noah's own day.
- **What was proclaimed?** *Ekēryxen* ("heralded, proclaimed") is **content-unspecified** — it does not say *good news* (that would be *euangelizō*, used at 4:6) versus *judgment / a victory announcement*.

"Spirits" (*pneumata*) here is rendered **plain "spirits"** (the imprisoned beings), **not** the {a:wind/spirit} slash — no "wind" sense is live for personal beings in a prison. The TT preserves all three readings and the open *ekēryxen*, deciding none (Rule 13, Rule 3). → editorial-log P-006; §B1 (the Enochic tradition); §F4 (the "descent / harrowing of hell" reception).

### A8. Noah, the eight, and baptism "as antitype" — the typology the Greek states
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Verses 20-21 build a flood-to-baptism **type / antitype** pair the TT keeps explicit. Eight souls were "**brought safely through water**" (*diesōthēsan di' hydatos*, 3:20) — the preposition *dia* ("through") carrying both "by means of" and "passing through," so the same flood that judged was, for the eight, the medium of rescue. Then 3:21: **ὃ… ἀντίτυπον νῦν σῴζει βάπτισμα** = "which also, **as an antitype** (*antitypon*), now saves you — **immersion** (*baptisma*)." The flood-water is the *typos*; baptism the *antitypon* ("corresponding pattern / counterpart").

The verse then **qualifies the claim before it can be mistaken**: baptism saves "**not a removal of dirt of the flesh** (*ou sarkos apothesis rhypou*) **but an appeal of a good conscience toward God** (*syneidēseōs agathēs eperōtēma eis theon*)" — and the saving runs "**through the resurrection of Yeshua the Messiah** (Jesus)," not through the water itself. **ἐπερώτημα** (*eperōtēma*) is itself debated — "appeal / request" or "pledge / answer (to formal questions)," possibly a baptismal-rite term; the TT renders "appeal" and notes the option. The TT presents the typology and its self-qualification **without importing a sacramental theology** (Rule 3). **βάπτισμα** is rendered "immersion" per the locked GS glossary. → editorial-log P-007; §D2; §F4.

## B. Ancient Near Eastern and Ancient World Parallels

### B1. The imprisoned "watchers" — the Enochic tradition behind "the spirits in prison"
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED (for the tradition); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for its presence here)]**

The reading of "the spirits in prison" (3:19) as fallen *angelic* beings draws on a well-attested Second-Temple expansion of **Genesis 6:1-4** (the "sons of God" who took human wives). In **1 Enoch** (the *Book of the Watchers*, chs. 6-16, with the punishment scenes in chs. 18-21), two hundred angels ("watchers," *egrēgoroi*) descend under Shemihazah and Asael, corrupt humanity, and are **bound and imprisoned** in pits and darkness "until the great judgment." The same tradition surfaces in **Jubilees** (5:1-11), and explicitly as a NT datum at **2 Peter 2:4** (angels "cast into Tartarus… kept for judgment") and **Jude 6** (angels "kept in eternal chains in deep darkness for the judgment of the great day"). The technical match is the *binding / imprisonment of spirit-beings for pre-flood disobedience*.

This is a genuine parallel that makes reading (b) of §A7 historically live for a first-century audience steeped in the Enochic literature; it does **not** prove that 1 Peter intends the watchers rather than the human dead, and the parallel is presented as background, not as a decision. The TT renders "spirits in prison" literally and lets the reader weigh the Enochic resonance.

**Source:** Nickelsburg, G.W.E., *1 Enoch 1: A Commentary*, Hermeneia, Fortress Press, 2001, pp. 165-272; Charlesworth, J.H. (ed.), *The Old Testament Pseudepigrapha*, vol. 1 (1 Enoch, Jubilees), Doubleday, 1983; Bauckham, R., *Jude, 2 Peter*, Word Biblical Commentary 50, Word, 1983, pp. 248-255.

### B2. Greco-Roman household-management literature (the *oikonomia* tradition)
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

The form of 1 Peter's household code (vv.1-7) has clear analogues in Greco-Roman discourse "on household management" (*peri oikonomias*), a topic treated from Aristotle onward. **Aristotle** (*Politics* 1.1253b-1260b) frames the household around three paired relationships — husband/wife, father/children, master/slave — and grounds the ordering in nature; the (pseudo-)Aristotelian *Oikonomikos* and later **Stoic** writers (e.g. Hierocles, *On Duties*; Musonius Rufus) elaborate the duties of each station. Hellenistic-Jewish writers absorbed the form: **Philo** (*Hypothetica* 7.3, *On the Decalogue* 165-167) and **Josephus** (*Against Apion* 2.199-208) list household duties in the same register, Josephus stating bluntly that "the woman is in all things inferior to the man" — language sharper than 1 Peter's "co-heir."

The NT codes (1 Pet 3; Col 3:18-4:1; Eph 5:21-6:9) take up this widely recognized form and rework it — addressing the *subordinate* members as moral agents in their own right, and (in 1 Peter) grounding the whole in the suffering Messiah (2:21-25) rather than in nature or civic order. The parallel locates the genre; the differences are what the reader is invited to weigh.

**Source:** Balch, D.L., *Let Wives Be Submissive: The Domestic Code in 1 Peter*, SBL Monograph 26, Scholars Press, 1981, pp. 23-62; Aristotle, *Politics* 1.1253b-1260b, Loeb Classical Library; Josephus, *Against Apion* 2.199-208, Loeb Classical Library.

## C. Historical Context

### C1. The recipients — Christians in the provinces of Asia Minor
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The letter is addressed (1:1) to "resident-aliens of the dispersion" in **Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia** — a sweep across the northern and central regions of Roman Asia Minor (modern Turkey). These were ordinary provincial communities under Roman administration, mixing long-settled Greek city populations, indigenous Anatolian peoples, Roman colonists, and Jewish diaspora communities. The household-code instructions of ch. 3 presuppose exactly such a setting: small assemblies of believers embedded in non-believing households and neighborhoods, where conversion frequently *crossed* the household rather than converting it whole — hence the wife with a "disobeying" (non-believing) husband (3:1) and the believers "spoken against" and reviled by neighbors (3:16; cf. 2:12; 4:4).

The social pressure the chapter addresses — winning a hostile household "without a word," giving a measured "defense" to those who ask (3:15) — fits the situation of a suspect minority within the Roman provincial order of the later first century, before any systematic state persecution. The TT keeps the geographic and social frame in view through the "resident-aliens and sojourners" keyword (1:1, 17; 2:11). → §I (the recipients' household world).

**Source:** Elliott, J.H., *1 Peter*, Anchor Bible 37B, Doubleday, 2000, pp. 84-103; Horrell, D.G., *1 Peter*, New Testament Guides, T&T Clark, 2008, pp. 49-66.

### C2. "Without a word" — the social risk of the convert wife
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The instruction that a wife win her husband "without a word" by conduct (3:1-2) is sharpened by a known social expectation: in the Greco-Roman household the wife was expected to **worship the gods of her husband**. **Plutarch**, writing on marriage in this same period (*Advice to Bride and Groom* / *Coniugalia praecepta* 19, *Moralia* 140D), states it as a rule — a wife should worship only her husband's gods and shut the door on "strange" and foreign cults. A wife who adopted a religion her husband had not sanctioned was, by that standard, transgressing her household role and risking suspicion of the very disorder (sexual, social, political) that new cults were accused of fostering.

Against this backdrop, "won without a word" is not merely a counsel of tact but a strategy for a structurally exposed position: the convert wife cannot evangelize by open argument without confirming the charge of household subversion, so the letter directs her to a witness of *conduct* that disarms the suspicion. The TT renders the Greek; this note supplies the social stakes. → §I2 (mixed-faith marriage); §I4 ("won without a word").

**Source:** Plutarch, *Coniugalia praecepta* 19 (*Moralia* 140D), Loeb Classical Library; Balch, D.L., *Let Wives Be Submissive*, Scholars Press, 1981, pp. 81-116; Lieu, J., *Neither Jew nor Greek? Constructing Early Christianity*, T&T Clark, 2002.

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. *Eperōtēma* — "appeal," "request," or "pledge"?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the range); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the sense)]**

**ἐπερώτημα** (*eperōtēma*, 3:21) is a NT *hapax legomenon* (occurring only here) and its sense is genuinely disputed. The base verb *eperōtaō* means "to ask, question, request," giving (a) "**appeal / request** [to God] for a good conscience" or "the request *that arises from* a good conscience." But the noun is also documented in the papyri and in Hellenistic legal/contractual usage as the formal **"question-and-answer"** of a contract — the stipulated response by which a party binds himself — yielding (b) "**pledge / undertaking** of a good conscience," i.e. the answer given to formal questions at baptism. The accompanying genitive **συνειδήσεως ἀγαθῆς** ("of a good conscience") can be subjective ("the appeal a good conscience makes") or objective ("the request *for* a good conscience"), compounding the ambiguity.

The TT renders **"appeal"** and notes the "pledge / answer" option in Tier-2, declining to make the verse a witness to a developed baptismal interrogation-formula (which the "pledge" sense, read maximally, would imply). The lexical openness is the finding. → editorial-log P-007; §A8.

**Source:** Bauer, W., *A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament* (BDAG), 3rd ed., rev. Danker, University of Chicago Press, 2000, s.v. ἐπερώτημα; Moulton, J.H. and Milligan, G., *The Vocabulary of the Greek Testament*, Hodder & Stoughton, 1930, s.v.; Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, Fortress Press, 1996, pp. 266-272.

### D2. *Antitypon* — the type/antitype relation
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

**ἀντίτυπον** (*antitypon*, 3:21) is built from *anti* ("corresponding to, over against") + *typos* ("stamp, impression, pattern, type"), and means "corresponding pattern / counterpart." In the NT it occurs only here and at Hebrews 9:24 (where the earthly sanctuary is the *antitypa*, "copies," of the heavenly). Logically a *typos* is the prefiguring original and the *antitypon* the corresponding fulfillment — so the flood-water (3:20) is the *typos* and baptism (3:21) the *antitypon* that answers to it. The grammar is famously crowded: the neuter relative **ὅ** ("which") most naturally takes "water" (*hydōr*) as its antecedent, so that "**which** [water], as an antitype, now saves you."

The TT renders "as an antitype" and keeps the flood→baptism correspondence the Greek draws, while the immediate qualification (baptism is "not a removal of dirt… but an appeal… through the resurrection," §A8) prevents the typology from being read as water-magic. The TT does not extend the type/antitype relation beyond what the verse states. → §A8.

**Source:** Liddell, H.G. and Scott, R., *Greek-English Lexicon*, 9th ed., rev. Jones, 1940, s.v. ἀντίτυπος; Goppelt, L., *Typos: The Typological Interpretation of the Old Testament in the New*, Eerdmans, 1982, pp. 152-158.

### D3. *Syneidēsis* — "conscience" as the chapter's through-line
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

**συνείδησις** (*syneidēsis*, "conscience, co-knowledge"), literally "a knowing-with [oneself]," runs as a quiet thread through the chapter's second half: the *apologia* is given "**having a good conscience**" (*syneidēsin echontes agathēn*, 3:16), and baptism is "**an appeal of a good conscience** toward God" (3:21). The same word grounded the servants' endurance at 2:19 ("for conscience of God"). The Greek term names the inner faculty of moral self-awareness, not (as later usage can imply) a guilt-feeling; "a good conscience" is integrity before God under hostile scrutiny.

The TT renders *syneidēsis* "conscience" consistently across 2:19; 3:16, 21, so the reader can see the same faculty linking the defense-before-others (v.16) to the appeal-toward-God (v.21). The through-line is a feature of the Greek the TT keeps visible rather than varying the gloss.

**Source:** Bauer, *BDAG*, s.v. συνείδησις; Eckstein, H.-J., *Der Begriff Syneidesis bei Paulus*, Mohr Siebeck, 1983 (on the NT range).

## F. Later Reception in Other Traditions

### F1. The household code (vv.1-7) in the history of interpretation
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

1 Peter 3:1-7, with its parallels in Colossians and Ephesians, became a primary text in the long Christian reception of marriage, gender, and authority — and its reading has shifted markedly across the centuries. Patristic and medieval interpreters generally read the "submit" and "weaker vessel" language as endorsing a natural and permanent hierarchy of husband over wife (e.g. Augustine, *On the Good of Marriage*; Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae*, on the order of the household). Reformation and post-Reformation writers retained the hierarchy while emphasizing the husband's reciprocal duty of "honor" (v.7). From the nineteenth century onward, the passage became a focus of contested interpretation: some read it as culturally bounded missionary counsel to a vulnerable minority (anchoring on the "won without a word" aim, §C2, and the "co-heir" dignity-claim, §A4), others as enjoining an order held to be permanent.

These are documented receptions, not data of the Greek. The TT renders the code's terms (submit; weaker vessel; co-heir; honor) literally and **takes no position** among these readings (Rule 3; P-009); the differences are laid out so the reader can weigh them.

**Source:** Kittredge, C.B., *Community and Authority: The Rhetoric of Obedience in the Pauline Tradition*, Harvard Theological Studies, Trinity Press International, 1998; Bird, J.G., *Abuse, Power and Fearful Obedience: Reconsidering 1 Peter's Commands to Wives*, Library of New Testament Studies, T&T Clark, 2011, pp. 1-40.

### F2. "Weaker vessel" and "co-heir" — the reception of the v.7 tension
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The single sentence of 3:7 that calls the wife both "weaker vessel" and "co-heir of the grace of life" has generated two distinct reception streams. One stream foregrounded **"weaker vessel"** (*asthenesteron skeuos*) as a statement of the wife's lesser physical, intellectual, or spiritual capacity, used in later centuries to justify a subordinate status. A counter-stream foregrounded **"co-heir"** (*synklēronomos*) — an equal-inheritance term — as the controlling claim, reading "weaker" as situational (social exposure, physical force) rather than a verdict on worth, and noting the sanction that mistreatment "**hinders prayers**" (the husband's standing before God depends on his honoring the wife). Both readings are attested across the interpretive tradition.

This is reception history, not lexical adjudication. The lexical situation (§A4) leaves "weaker" undetermined and sets it in the same breath as "co-heir"; the TT renders both and resolves neither.

**Source:** Bird, J.G., *Abuse, Power and Fearful Obedience*, T&T Clark, 2011; Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, Fortress Press, 1996, pp. 217-220.

### F3. "Always ready for a defense" and the apologetic tradition
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The instruction to be "always ready for a **defense** (*apologia*) to everyone asking… the reason for the hope in you" (3:15) became a charter text for the Christian **apologetic** tradition — the second-century *Apologies* of Justin Martyr, Aristides, and Athenagoras took the form of reasoned "defenses" addressed to outsiders and rulers, and the verse is still cited as a foundation for "apologetics" as a discipline. The qualifying phrase "**with gentleness and fear**" (v.16) was likewise invoked in later reflection on the *manner* of such defense. These developments build on the verse; the Greek *apologia* is simply "a reasoned answer," not yet a literary genre.

The TT renders "defense" and "the reason for the hope," and records the apologetic reception here rather than reading the later discipline back into the word.

**Source:** Grant, R.M., *Greek Apologists of the Second Century*, Westminster Press, 1988; Edwards, M., Goodman, M., and Price, S. (eds.), *Apologetics in the Roman Empire*, Oxford University Press, 1999.

### F4. The descent / "harrowing of hell" — reception of vv.19-20 (and 4:6)
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The proclamation "to the spirits in prison" (3:19), read together with "the gospel was preached even to the dead" (4:6), became the principal scriptural basis for the doctrine of the **descent of the Messiah** (*descensus ad inferos*) — his going, between death and resurrection, to the realm of the dead — and, in its dramatized medieval form, the "**harrowing of hell**," in which the Messiah breaks the gates of Hades and leads out the righteous dead. The descent entered the creeds ("he descended into hell," in later forms of the Apostles' Creed), inspired the apocryphal *Gospel of Nicodemus* (*Acts of Pilate*), and became a major subject of Byzantine and Western art (the *Anastasis* icon). Patristic readers were already divided over whether the "spirits" were the watchers of Genesis 6 (so the Enochic line of reading, §B1) or the human dead.

These are documented developments — creedal, literary, artistic — built on a clause the Greek leaves open (§A7). The TT renders vv.19-20 literally, lists the readings, and adopts none of the doctrinal constructions, recording the descent reception as reception.

**Source:** Dalton, W.J., *Christ's Proclamation to the Spirits: A Study of 1 Peter 3:18–4:6*, 2nd ed., Pontifical Biblical Institute, 1989, pp. 27-67; Trumbower, J.A., *Rescue for the Dead: The Posthumous Salvation of Non-Christians in Early Christianity*, Oxford University Press, 2001, pp. 33-55.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. "Calling him lord" — a generalization beyond Genesis
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the data); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the inference)]**

1 Peter says Sarah "**obeyed** Avraham, **calling** him lord" (3:6) as habitual practice, but Genesis records Sarah's "my lord" only once, and *inwardly* (Gen 18:12, in a moment the next verse shows her laughing in disbelief). The letter reads a single inward word as a settled pattern of deference. Whether this reflects a wider Second-Temple Jewish portrait of Sarah (later midrashic tradition does elaborate the matriarchs' virtues) or is the letter's own generalizing use of the verse is not determinable from the text. The TT renders what 1 Peter says ("calling him lord") and the verse-note flags the single Genesis source behind the generalization.

### G2. Why is *pneuma* slashed at v.18 but plain at vv.4 and 19?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The same noun *pneuma* receives three treatments in one chapter, and the differences are deliberate, not inconsistent. At 3:4 ("a gentle and quiet **spirit**") *pneuma* names a human disposition — no "wind" sense is live — so it is rendered plain "spirit." At 3:19 "the **spirits** (*pneumata*) in prison" are personal beings in confinement — again no "wind" sense — so plain "spirits." Only at 3:18 ("made alive in **{a:wind/spirit}**") does the wind/spirit field stay genuinely open (the divine Spirit / the breath-of-life / the spiritual mode of existence are all in play), so the slash is kept. The TT's varied handling tracks where the ambiguity is real versus where the context closes it.

### G3. Does v.22's "powers subjected" answer v.19's "spirits in prison"?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the verbal link); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the connection)]**

The chapter opens and closes with the same verb. The wives are to "**submit**" (*hypotassō*, 3:1) at the start; at the end "angels and authorities and powers" are "**having been subjected** (*hypotagentōn*, the same root) to him" (3:22, echoing Ps 110:1). Many readers see a deliberate frame: the Messiah who "proclaimed to the spirits in prison" (3:19) is now the one over whom every spirit-power is subjected — so that the obscure proclamation and the exaltation belong to a single movement of triumph over the powers. The verbal link is certain; whether the author intends vv.19 and 22 to be read together as proclamation-then-reign is a reasonable inference the text supports but does not state. → PROPHECY (Ps 110:1 → 1 Pet 3:22).

## I. The World at the Time

> **The recipients' world.** Unlike the Gospel and Acts companions (set in Second-Temple Judea), 1 Peter addresses scattered believers in the **Greco-Roman provinces of Asia Minor** (Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, Bithynia — 1:1; §C1) in the later first century CE. The chapter's household instructions (vv.1-7) speak directly into the **marriage and household norms of the wider Greco-Roman world** — which is why this Section I focuses on that domestic-and-social setting rather than on the Temple-city. The flat `### [Topic]` form is used (the default).

---

### I1. Social Structure — The Greco-Roman household and the authority of the *paterfamilias*
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The household (*oikos* / Latin *familia*) was the basic unit of Greco-Roman society, and its order — husband over wife, father over children, master over slaves — was treated as the foundation of the political order itself (the *oikonomia* tradition, §B2). In Roman law the male head, the *paterfamilias*, held formal authority (*patria potestas*) over the household's persons and property, though by the early Empire most marriages were "free" (*sine manu*), leaving the wife legally under her own father's line rather than her husband's, with her own property. The wife's recognized virtues were modesty, fidelity, household management, and *not* public assertion. 1 Peter's wives-and-husbands code (vv.1-7) is framed for and against this structure: it speaks to wives *as moral agents* and obliges husbands to "assign honor" — addressing both stations, where civic ideology typically addressed only the head.

**Source:** Treggiari, S., *Roman Marriage*, Oxford University Press, 1991, pp. 15-36, 237-261; Balch, D.L., *Let Wives Be Submissive*, Scholars Press, 1981, pp. 23-62.

### I2. Social Structure — Mixed-faith marriage and the wife following her husband's gods
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED (for the norm); PROBABLE (for its bearing on 3:1)]**

A strong social expectation held that a wife should worship her **husband's** gods, not introduce her own. **Plutarch** states the rule directly in his marriage advice (*Coniugalia praecepta* 19, *Moralia* 140D): a wife should worship only the gods her husband acknowledges and "shut the front door tight upon all queer rituals and outlandish superstitions." A wife adopting an unsanctioned cult was therefore seen as overstepping her household role — and new "foreign" cults (which Judaism and the Jesus-movement could appear to be) were widely suspected of fostering immorality and disloyalty. This is the precise pressure behind the convert wife of 3:1 whose husband "disobeys the word": her faith itself was, by prevailing norms, a domestic transgression. The letter's counsel — win him *by conduct*, "without a word" — addresses a genuinely exposed social position (§C2).

**Source:** Plutarch, *Coniugalia praecepta* 19 (*Moralia* 140D), Loeb Classical Library; Balch, D.L., *Let Wives Be Submissive*, Scholars Press, 1981, pp. 81-116; Lieu, J., *Neither Jew nor Greek?*, T&T Clark, 2002.

### I3. Daily Life — Women's adornment as a moral and social debate
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The triad of 3:3 — braided hair, gold ornaments, fine clothing — was a recognized topic of ancient moral debate, not a random list. Greco-Roman and Hellenistic-Jewish moralists repeatedly contrasted **outward adornment** with **inward virtue**, often treating elaborate hairstyles, jewelry, and costly dress as marks of luxury (*tryphē*), vanity, or even seduction, and praising the modest woman's restraint instead. The theme appears in Stoic and Pythagorean writings on women (e.g. the letters attributed to Melissa and Phintys), in satire (Juvenal 6 on Roman women's coiffures), and in Jewish-Hellenistic and other early-Christian texts (1 Tim 2:9-10; cf. Philo on female modesty). Sumptuary concern was also legal at points (e.g. the Roman *lex Oppia* debates). 1 Peter's relocation of *kosmos* from the visible triad to "the hidden person of the heart" (§A2) is a recognizable move within this well-worn moralists' contrast — distinctive mainly in grounding the inner adornment as "precious **before God**."

**Source:** Hemelrijk, E.A., *Matrona Docta: Educated Women in the Roman Élite*, Routledge, 1999, pp. 7-58; Balch, D.L., *Let Wives Be Submissive*, Scholars Press, 1981, pp. 101-105; Juvenal, *Satire* 6, Loeb Classical Library.

### I4. Social Structure — "Won without a word": the limits on a wife's speech
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Public and assertive speech was coded as a male prerogative in Greco-Roman society; the ideal wife's authority operated through the household and through influence, not through open argument or public contradiction of her husband — silence and self-restraint were praised virtues (cf. the Pythagorean and Stoic ideals of the modest matron). For a convert wife to argue her husband into a new religion would have transgressed both the religious expectation (§I2) and the speech-norm at once. The letter's strategy — that she "may be won without a word" through observed "pure conduct" (3:1-2) — works *within* these constraints rather than against them, channeling the witness into the one register (exemplary conduct) open to her without scandal. Whether the letter thereby endorses the constraint or merely operates pragmatically within it is exactly the interpretive question §F1 keeps open.

**Source:** Balch, D.L., *Let Wives Be Submissive*, Scholars Press, 1981, pp. 95-116; Hemelrijk, E.A., *Matrona Docta*, Routledge, 1999; Treggiari, S., *Roman Marriage*, Oxford University Press, 1991.
