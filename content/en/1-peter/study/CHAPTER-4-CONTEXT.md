## HOW TO USE THIS COMPANION

This file contains background material organized by topic. Each entry carries **two labels:**

**What kind of claim?** (tells you the source of the information)
- **TEXTUAL** — derived directly from the Greek text
- **STRONG INFERENCE** — logically entailed by the text, not stated explicitly
- **POSSIBLE INFERENCE** — one legitimate reading among others
- **COMPARATIVE PARALLEL** — attested in another ancient source
- **LATER RECEPTION** — post-biblical reading
- **HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL** — based on material or historical evidence
- **SPECULATION** — no strong textual or evidential basis

**How certain?** (tells you the confidence level)
- **VERIFIED** — strong evidence, wide agreement
- **PROBABLE** — well-supported but debated
- **POSSIBLE** — one reasonable reading among others
- **UNCERTAIN** — genuinely unclear
- **SPECULATIVE** — must be rare and clearly flagged
- **DOCUMENTED** — historically attested reception (for LATER RECEPTION entries)

**Reading principle:** Start with TEXTUAL entries — those come from the Greek itself. Then explore outward to parallels, reception, and comparison. The companion presents; you assess.

## A. Source Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. "Arm yourselves with the same resolve" — the military image (4:1)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The exhortation **τὴν αὐτὴν ἔννοιαν ὁπλίσασθε** (*tēn autēn ennoian hoplisasthe*, 4:1), "arm yourselves with the same resolve/intent," is built on **ὁπλίζω** (*hoplizō*), "to equip with weapons" (from *hoplon*, "weapon, tool"; cf. the *hoplitēs*, the armored soldier). The "same" resolve is the Messiah's suffering "in *the* flesh" of the preceding clause (and of 3:18); his suffering becomes the readers' weapon. The TT keeps the martial verb visible rather than softening it to "think the same way," so that the call to suffering is framed, as the Greek frames it, as taking up arms.

The maxim that follows — **ὁ παθὼν σαρκὶ πέπαυται ἁμαρτίας** (*ho pathōn sarki pepautai hamartias*), "the *one* having suffered in *the* flesh has ceased from sin" — is itself genuinely debated. **[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN]** Readings include: the believer whose suffering breaks the hold of sin; the Messiah who, having suffered, is done with the realm where sin operates; or a baptismal "death to sin" (cf. Rom 6:7). The TT renders the gnomic saying as the Greek gives it and does not select among the readings. → see the verse-note at 4:1.

### A2. The break with "the will of the nations" — the vice-catalog (4:3-4)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

At 4:3 the readers' past is summed as **τὸ βούλημα τῶν ἐθνῶν** (*to boulēma tōn ethnōn*), "the will of the nations" — *ta ethnē*, "the nations / Gentiles," used here not ethnically but morally, of the pagan way of life the readers have left (note that the recipients are themselves largely former Gentiles; cf. 1:14, 18). A six-fold vice-list follows: **ἀσελγείαις, ἐπιθυμίαις, οἰνοφλυγίαις, κώμοις, πότοις, καὶ ἀθεμίτοις εἰδωλολατρίαις** ("debaucheries, desires, drunkenness, carousings, drinking-bouts, and lawless idolatries"). The terms cluster around feasting, intoxication, and cult — the social settings (§I) the believers had withdrawn from.

The dry understatement **ἀρκετός** (*arketos*, "sufficient / enough," 4:3) — "the time *that has* passed *is* sufficient" for that — is preserved rather than smoothed. At 4:4 the outsiders' reaction is named with two precise verbs: they are "surprised" (*xenizontai*, lit. "find it strange / foreign") that the believers no longer **συντρεχόντων** ("run with" *them*) into the "flood of dissipation" (*tēs asōtias anachysin*), and so they **βλασφημοῦντες** ("slander / blaspheme") them. The TT keeps the social mechanics — withdrawal, bafflement, slander — exactly as the Greek lays them out (→ §I1).

### A3. **4:6 "the good news preached even to the dead" — the chapter's crux (P-010)**
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the wording); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the referent)]**

The clause **καὶ νεκροῖς εὐηγγελίσθη** (*kai nekrois euēngelisthē*, 4:6), "the good news was preached even to *the* dead," is the chapter's high-restraint crux and is rendered literally, with no reading adopted (Rule 3 / Rule 13; editorial-log P-010). The three live readings:

- **(a) The now-dead Christians.** "The dead" are believers who heard the good news while alive and have since died; the purpose-clause then explains that, though "**judged according to humans in *the* flesh**" (i.e. having died, the common human lot), they "**live according to God in *the* {a:wind/spirit}**." On this reading the verse reassures readers grieving believers who died before the expected end (cf. 4:7).
- **(b) The dead evangelized in the descent.** "The dead" are those addressed in the proclamation of 3:19 — most often the disobedient of Noach's (Noah's) day — heard between the Messiah's death and resurrection. This ties 4:6 directly to the 3:18-20 crux (P-006).
- **(c) A general proclamation to the dead.** A broader descent-and-proclamation to the realm of the dead, without specifying which dead.

The flesh/spirit antithesis (**σαρκὶ… πνεύματι**, anarthrous datives) is the same construction as 3:18 (P-006), and the {a:wind/spirit} slash is kept. The verb **εὐηγγελίσθη** ("good news was announced") is more specific than the *ekēryxen* ("proclaimed") of 3:19 — a textual datum that pulls some readers toward distinguishing the two verses, others toward harmonizing them. The TT does not decide; it preserves the link to 3:19 and resolves none. → editorial-log P-010, P-006; the verse-note at 4:6; cross-ref 3:19 in the chapter-3 companion.

### A4. "Judged… in flesh / live… in spirit" — the antithesis carried over from 3:18 (4:6)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The purpose-clause of 4:6 — **ἵνα κριθῶσι… κατὰ ἀνθρώπους σαρκὶ ζῶσι δὲ κατὰ θεὸν πνεύματι** — sets two prepositional/dative spheres against each other: judged "**according to humans, in *the* flesh**," but living "**according to God, in *the* {a:wind/spirit}**." This is the same flesh/spirit dative parallel the letter draws at 3:18 ("put to death in flesh, made alive in {a:wind/spirit}"), where the datives can read either as **sphere** ("in the realm of flesh / spirit") or as **agency** ("by flesh / by the Spirit"). The TT keeps the slash and the bare datives rather than collapsing them into a single resolved sense.

The double **κατά** ("according to") phrases — *kata anthrōpous* ("according to humans") and *kata theon* ("according to God") — frame the contrast as two measures or standards, the human and the divine, rather than two times or two persons. The TT preserves the symmetry. → §A3; editorial-log P-006.

### A5. "The end of all things has drawn near" — the perfect tense (4:7)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

At 4:7 **πάντων δὲ τὸ τέλος ἤγγικεν** (*pantōn de to telos ēngiken*), "the end of all *things* has drawn near," uses **ἐγγίζω** (*engizō*, "draw near, approach") in the **perfect** tense — "has come near and stands near," a settled nearness rather than a single past arrival. The same verb in the same proclamation-of-nearness sense stands at the opening of Yeshua's message ("the kingdom of God **has drawn near**," Mark 1:15) and in the Gospel-tradition eschatology generally. **τέλος** (*telos*) is "end / goal / completion," and "of all *things*" (*pantōn*) gives it cosmic scope.

The TT renders the perfect as "**has drawn near**" rather than the flatter "is near," keeping the aspectual force. Crucially, the imminence is stated as the **ground of ethics**, not of speculation: "**be sober therefore, and clear-headed for prayers**" (4:7b). The expectation of the end produces sobriety and prayer, and the chapter's whole ethic (love, hospitality, gift-service, endurance) follows from it.

### A6. "Christian" — *Christianos*, an outsiders' label reclaimed (4:16)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the term); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE (for the "outsiders' label" sense)]**

**Χριστιανός** (*Christianos*, 4:16) is one of only **three** occurrences in the New Testament (the others: Acts 11:26, where the disciples were "first called Christians in Antioch," and Acts 26:28, Agrippa's "in a little you persuade me to make me a Christian"). The form is a Latinism: the *-ianos* suffix (Latin *-ianus*) names a partisan or follower of a person — as *Hērōdianoi* ("Herodians") names partisans of Herod — so *Christianoi* means "the Christ-people / partisans of Christus." Such a name is most naturally one coined by **outsiders** to label the group from outside, not a self-designation (the group's own terms are "brothers," "the elect," "those of the Way"). 

In 4:16 the term appears in a hostile setting — "if [one suffers] as a *Christianos*" — alongside the verbs of reviling and slander (4:14, 4:4). The letter's move is to **reclaim** the outsiders' label as a name to "glorify God in," not "be ashamed" of. The TT keeps the established English "Christian" (a fixed lexicalized form) rather than transliterating, and the social force of the term is set out here. → §I3; the verse-note at 4:16.

### A7. "A faithful Creator" — *ktistēs*, a GS hapax (4:19)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

At 4:19 God is named **πιστῷ κτίστῃ** (*pistō ktistē*), "a faithful **Creator**" — and **κτίστης** (*ktistēs*, "creator, founder") occurs **only here** in the Greek Scriptures (a *hapax legomenon* in the GS corpus). The title grounds endurance not in a generic providence but specifically in God's **creative** faithfulness: the One who made the world can be trusted to keep those who suffer "according to *the* will of God." The verb **παρατίθημι** (*paratithēmi*, "entrust, deposit, commit for safekeeping") echoes the Messiah's dying word, "into your hands I **entrust** my spirit" (Luke 23:46, citing Ps 31:5), making the readers' self-commitment a participation in his.

The TT renders "**faithful Creator**" and "**entrust their souls**" literally, keeping the rare creator-title and the deposit-image visible rather than generalizing to "trust God." The pairing with "**in doing good**" (*en agathopoiia*, the keyword of 2:14-15, 20; 3:6, 17) closes the chapter's argument: endurance is active, not passive.

## B. Ancient Near Eastern and Ancient World Parallels

### B1. "Love covers a multitude of sins" — Proverbs 10:12 (4:8)
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

The maxim **ἀγάπη καλύπτει πλῆθος ἁμαρτιῶν** (*agapē kalyptei plēthos hamartiōn*, 4:8), "love covers a multitude of sins," renders **Proverbs 10:12** — but follows the **Hebrew** sense ("love covers all transgressions") rather than the Septuagint, which reads quite differently ("friendship covers all those who are not contentious"). The same proverb is quoted in the same form at James 5:20 ("will cover a multitude of sins"), which suggests a fixed early-Christian wisdom-citation circulating independently of the LXX. "Cover" (*kalyptō*, Hebrew *kissah*) carries the sense of throwing a covering *over* offenses — not concealing them dishonestly but refusing to expose, prolong, or retaliate (the antithesis in Prov 10:12a is hatred, which "stirs up strife").

The parallel shows the verse drawing on the Hebrew wisdom tradition through a shared Christian citation, not on the Greek Bible the letter elsewhere prefers. The TT keeps the form as 1 Peter gives it. → see the PROPHECY companion (Prov 10:12 → 1 Pet 4:8).

**Source:** Beale, G.K. and Carson, D.A. (eds.), *Commentary on the New Testament Use of the Old Testament*, Baker Academic, 2007, pp. 1037-1039 (on 1 Peter); Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, Fortress Press, 1996, pp. 295-296.

### B2. "The spirit of glory and of God rests on you" — Isaiah 11:2 (4:14)
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The blessing on the reviled — **τὸ τῆς δόξης καὶ τὸ τοῦ θεοῦ πνεῦμα… ἀναπαύεται** (*to tēs doxēs kai to tou theou pneuma… anapauetai*, 4:14), "the {a:wind/spirit} of glory and of God **rests** upon you" — echoes **Isaiah 11:2** ("the spirit of YHWH shall **rest** upon him," LXX *anapausetai ep' auton pneuma tou theou*), the messianic-shoot oracle, with the verb *anapauomai* ("rest upon") supplying the verbal link. What rested on the Davidic shoot now rests on the persecuted community; the "of glory" expansion (unique to 1 Peter) ties the resting {a:wind/spirit} to the glory-after-suffering pattern of the letter (1:11; 4:13). 

The whole verse takes the form of a **beatitude** — "*you are* **blessed**" (*makarioi*) — matching Jesus's beatitude on the reviled-and-persecuted (Matt 5:11-12), which likewise pairs reviling with rejoicing. The parallel places the verse within the prophetic-and-Gospel stream of the spirit resting on the chosen; it does not flatten the {a:wind/spirit}, which the TT keeps with the slash. → see the PROPHECY companion (Isa 11:2 → 1 Pet 4:14).

**Source:** Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, 1996, pp. 311-313; Beale and Carson (eds.), *Commentary on the NT Use of the OT*, 2007, pp. 1043-1045.

### B3. "Judgment begins at the house of God" — Ezekiel 9 and Malachi 3 (4:17)
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

At 4:17 **ἄρξασθαι τὸ κρίμα ἀπὸ τοῦ οἴκου τοῦ θεοῦ** (*arxasthai to krima apo tou oikou tou theou*), "the judgment to begin from the **house of God**," draws on two HB traditions of judgment that **starts at the sanctuary**. In **Ezekiel 9:6** the executioners are commanded to slay the guilty but "**begin from my sanctuary**" (LXX *apo tōn hagiōn mou arxasthe*) — judgment commencing at the Temple, with the marked spared. **Malachi 3:1-5** has "**the Lord** whom you seek" come suddenly "to his temple" as a refiner's fire, purifying first the sons of Levi. The principle — that God's purifying judgment falls **first** on his own people/house — is the engine of the lesser-to-greater argument that follows ("**if first from us**, what *is* the end of those disobeying…?").

"House of God" (*oikos tou theou*) also ties back inside the letter to the "spiritual **house**" (*oikos pneumatikos*) of 2:5. The parallel supplies the scriptural logic; it does not make 1 Peter a citation of either prophet (no formula introduces it). → see the PROPHECY companion (Ezek 9:6 / Mal 3 → 1 Pet 4:17).

**Source:** Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, 1996, pp. 315-317; Elliott, J.H., *1 Peter*, Anchor Bible 37B, Doubleday, 2000, pp. 805-807.

### B4. "If the righteous is scarcely saved" — Proverbs 11:31 (LXX) (4:18)
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

The quotation **εἰ ὁ δίκαιος μόλις σῴζεται** (*ei ho dikaios molis sōzetai*, 4:18), "if the righteous *is* scarcely saved, where will the ungodly and *the* sinner appear?" follows **Proverbs 11:31 LXX** essentially verbatim. Here the Greek and Hebrew diverge sharply: the **Hebrew** reads "the righteous is requited **on earth**, how much more the wicked and the sinner" (Hebrew *be'arets*, "in/on the earth"), while the **LXX** translator read or rendered "**scarcely saved**" (*molis sōzetai*) — apparently reading the consonants differently. 1 Peter cites the **Greek** form, whose "scarcely saved" supplies the *a fortiori* (lesser-to-greater) argument the chapter needs. This is the sage's voice, not God's first-person speech, so it is **not** divine-marked (Rule 30; main-text Rule-30 note).

The parallel is a clear case of the letter's dependence on the Septuagint over the Hebrew (one of the standard observations in the authorship debate; see Introduction §B/§C). The TT renders the verse as 1 Peter cites it, in the LXX form. → see the PROPHECY companion (Prov 11:31 → 1 Pet 4:18).

**Source:** Elliott, J.H., *1 Peter*, Anchor Bible 37B, 2000, pp. 808-810; Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, 1996, pp. 317-318.

### B5. Suffering as participation and as "testing by fire" in Jewish tradition (4:12-13)
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The "**burning *trial***" (*pyrōsis*, 4:12) and the call to "**share** (*koinōneō*) in the sufferings of the Messiah" (4:13) draw on two deep streams. First, the image of **testing by fire**: the assaying of metals as a figure for the testing of the righteous is widespread in the HB and Second Temple literature (Prov 17:3; Zech 13:9; Mal 3:3; Wisdom 3:5-6, "as gold in the furnace he tested them"; Sirach 2:1-5, "when you come to serve the Lord, prepare yourself for testing… for in fire gold is tested"). 1 Peter has already used this image at 1:6-7 (faith "tested by fire," *dia pyros*). Second, the motif of the righteous **suffering with** the anointed/community, and of suffering as the precondition of glory, belongs to the martyrdom traditions (cf. 2 Maccabees 6-7; the suffering-then-vindication pattern).

The parallels show the verse standing within an established Jewish theology of tested-and-refined righteousness, reframed christologically as sharing the Messiah's sufferings. They establish a shared symbolism, not literary borrowing. The TT keeps "**burning**" (*pyrōsis*) rather than the abstract "ordeal," so the refining-fire resonance stays audible.

**Source:** Selwyn, E.G., *The First Epistle of St. Peter*, 2nd ed., Macmillan, 1947, pp. 221-224; Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, 1996, pp. 305-309.

## C. Historical Context

### C1. "Suffering as a Christian" and the legal use of *the name* (4:14-16)
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — POSSIBLE]**

Three times in vv.14-16 the suffering is attached to **a name**: reviled "**in *the* name** of *the* Messiah" (4:14), and suffering "**as a Christian**" who should "glorify God **in this name**" (4:16). The phrasing — being punished *for the name itself*, apart from any specific crime (the list of real crimes in 4:15 is explicitly set aside) — resembles the procedure later described in the **Pliny-Trajan correspondence** (c. 112 CE), where Pliny asks whether to punish "the name itself" (*nomen ipsum*) of Christians or only "the crimes attaching to the name" (*Epistulae* 10.96). Whether 1 Peter reflects that kind of formal, magistrate-level procedure or a looser social hostility in which "the name" was simply the slur attached to the group is exactly the disputed question (→ §I2, Introduction §D); the verbal overlap is real but does not by itself establish formal prosecution or fix a date.

The TT renders "in *the* name" and "in this name" literally, keeping "the name" as the recurring keyword the letter builds on (cf. the name-motif in Acts 3-5). → §A6; §I2-§I3; Introduction §D.

**Source:** Pliny the Younger, *Epistulae* 10.96-97, Loeb Classical Library; Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, 1996, pp. 28-36; Williams, T.B., *Persecution in 1 Peter: Differentiating and Contextualizing Early Christian Suffering*, Brill, 2012, pp. 179-236.

### C2. House-churches, traveling believers, and hospitality "without grumbling" (4:9-11)
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The instruction to be "**hospitable** (*philoxenoi*, lit. 'lovers of strangers') to one another **without grumbling**" (4:9), followed by the stewardship of "gifts" in speaking and serving (4:10-11), reflects the concrete conditions of an early Christian movement that met in **private houses** and depended on a network of hospitality for traveling believers, messengers, and teachers (cf. Acts 16:15; 18:3; Rom 16:23; 3 John 5-8 on receiving traveling workers). With no public buildings and a movement spread across five provinces (1:1), the home was the unit of assembly, and hosting strangers — costly, intrusive, and open to abuse — was a load-bearing form of communal love, which is why the qualifier "**without grumbling**" is needed. The "**oracles of God**" (*logia theou*, 4:11) for speakers and the "**strength God supplies**" for servers map the two basic functions of such a gathering.

The TT keeps "**lovers of strangers**" audible in the note and "**without grumbling**" in the text, preserving the practical realism of the instruction. → the verse-notes at 4:9-11.

**Source:** Elliott, J.H., *1 Peter*, Anchor Bible 37B, 2000, pp. 746-757; Malherbe, A.J., *Social Aspects of Early Christianity*, 2nd ed., Fortress Press, 1983, pp. 60-91.

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. *Allotriepiskopos* — the rare "meddler" in a list of crimes (4:15)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the rarity); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the sense)]**

In the list of things one must *not* suffer for — "**murderer or thief or evildoer or as a** ***meddler***" (4:15) — the last word, **ἀλλοτριεπίσκοπος** (*allotriepiskopos*), is extraordinarily rare and may be **coined here** (no certain earlier attestation). It is a compound of *allotrios* ("belonging to another / others' affairs") + *episkopos* ("overseer, supervisor"), so literally "an overseer of others' matters" — hence "meddler, busybody, one who interferes in what is not his concern." Its presence is striking: the first three items are serious crimes, while a "meddler" is a social nuisance, not a capital offense. 

Proposed senses include: a busybody who interferes in others' households (perhaps the kind of zealous interference that earned believers a reputation for disrupting families and trades, → §I1); an embezzler or one who misappropriates others' property; or a "spy / informer." The Greek does not decide. The TT renders "**meddler** *in others' affairs*" and notes the rarity, neither resolving the sense nor smoothing the oddness of the term in this list.

**Source:** Liddell, H.G. and Scott, R., *Greek-English Lexicon*, 9th ed., rev. Jones, 1940, s.v. ἀλλοτριεπίσκοπος; Bauer, W., *A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament* (BDAG), 3rd ed., rev. Danker, University of Chicago Press, 2000, s.v. ἀλλοτριεπίσκοπος; Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, 1996, pp. 309-311.

### D2. *Pyrōsis* — "burning / conflagration" as testing (4:12)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

**πύρωσις** (*pyrōsis*, 4:12), from *pyr* ("fire") via *pyroō* ("to burn, set on fire, refine by fire"), means "a burning, a being-on-fire, conflagration" — and, in the metallurgical register, "the firing / smelting" by which metal is purified. The same root drives the gold "**refined by fire**" (*dia pyros dokimazomenou*) of 1:7, which the chapter deliberately recalls: the trial is the firing-process, not a random catastrophe. The accompanying phrase **πρὸς πειρασμόν** (*pros peirasmon*, "for a testing") makes the purpose explicit — the burning is *for* assay. The Septuagint uses *pyrōsis* of a literal conflagration (Amos 4:9) and the cognate verb of refining metal and testing persons (Ps 65:10 LXX; Prov 27:21).

The TT renders "**the burning *trial***" rather than the flatter "fiery ordeal," keeping both the literal heat and the refining-by-fire metaphor live, since the verse's whole point ("do not be surprised… as *though* a strange *thing*") depends on hearing the trial as the expected refining-process, not an anomaly. → §B5; the verse-note at 4:12.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. πύρωσις, πυρόω; Bauer, *BDAG*, s.v. πύρωσις; Hatch, E. and Redpath, H.A., *A Concordance to the Septuagint*, 2nd ed., Baker, 1998, s.v. πύρωσις.

### D3. *Koinōneō* — "share / have fellowship in" the Messiah's sufferings (4:13)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

**κοινωνέω** (*koinōneō*, 4:13), "to share in, have a common portion in, partake of," is from *koinos* ("common, shared") and belongs to the *koinōnia* word-field ("fellowship, partnership, communion"). At 4:13 the readers **κοινωνεῖτε τοῖς τοῦ Χριστοῦ παθήμασιν** ("share in the sufferings of the Messiah") — not merely *like* his sufferings but *in* them, a genuine participation, with the dative "the sufferings of the Messiah" as the thing shared. The construction makes present suffering the participatory counterpart to future shared glory: "so that **also** at the revelation of his glory you may rejoice." The pattern restates the prophets' "sufferings of the Messiah and the glories after them" (1:11) and turns it participatory.

The TT renders "**share in** the sufferings" rather than "have a part in" or "fellowship," keeping the partaking sense without importing later sacramental or mystical-union vocabulary. → §B5; the verse-note at 4:13.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. κοινωνέω, κοινωνία; Bauer, *BDAG*, s.v. κοινωνέω; Selwyn, E.G., *The First Epistle of St. Peter*, 2nd ed., 1947, pp. 192-193.

## F. Later Reception in Other Traditions

### F1. 4:6 and the "descent into hell" (*descensus ad inferos*)
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

Read together with 3:19, the words "the good news was preached even to *the* dead" (4:6) became a principal scriptural anchor for the doctrine of the Messiah's **descent to the dead** ("he descended into hell," *descendit ad inferos*) — a clause that entered later forms of the Apostles' Creed and was elaborated in patristic and medieval theology (e.g. the "Harrowing of Hell," dramatized in the *Gospel of Nicodemus*). Interpreters from antiquity (Clement of Alexandria, Origen) onward used 4:6/3:19 to discuss whether the dead — and which dead — could receive the proclamation after death. These are documented receptions built on the verses; the Greek of 4:6 itself does not specify the descent, the recipients, or the content (§A3).

The TT renders 4:6 literally, preserves the link to 3:19, and adopts none of the later doctrinal constructions. → §A3; editorial-log P-010, P-006.

**Source:** Kelly, J.N.D., *Early Christian Creeds*, 3rd ed., Longman, 1972, pp. 378-383; Dalton, W.J., *Christ's Proclamation to the Spirits: A Study of 1 Peter 3:18–4:6*, 2nd ed., Pontifical Biblical Institute, 1989, pp. 42-67.

### F2. "Christian" as a name — from slur to self-designation
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The reclaiming move of 4:16 — wearing the outsiders' label "Christian" as an honor — is paralleled and extended in the second-century writers, for whom *Christianos* becomes a proudly worn self-name precisely under interrogation. **Ignatius of Antioch** (c. 110 CE) speaks of being "found a Christian" and of "Christianity" (*Christianismos*, apparently his coinage); the martyrdom accounts make the confession "**I am a Christian**" (*Christianus sum*) the decisive courtroom answer (the *Martyrdom of Polycarp*; the *Acts of the Scillitan Martyrs*; the Pliny correspondence reports interrogating the accused on whether they were Christians). The early Christian apologists (e.g. the *Apology* attributed to Tertullian) argued about the very legality of punishing "the name."

These developments trace how the term moved from hostile label to confessional badge — documented reception of the dynamic 4:16 already shows in seed form, not a claim about the word's meaning in the verse. → §A6; §C1; §I3.

Χριστιανός, Χριστιανισμός; Wilken, R.L., *The Christians as the Romans Saw Them*, 2nd ed., Yale University Press, 2003, pp. 1-30; Musurillo, H. (ed.), *The Acts of the Christian Martyrs*, Oxford University Press, 1972.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. Where does the doxology fall — and does it end the letter's first half? (4:11)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the data); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the inference)]**

The full doxology at 4:11 — "**to whom belong the glory and the power into the ages of the ages. Amen**" — is the kind of closing formula that elsewhere ends a letter (cf. the doxology at 5:11). Its appearance here, in the middle of the letter, has led many to propose that 1:3–4:11 forms one major movement (with 4:12–5:11 a second), and a minority to argue that 1 Peter was assembled from two pieces (e.g. a baptismal homily plus a persecution-letter). The "Amen" and the change of address at 4:12 ("Beloved…") are the data; whether they mark a seam, a deliberate hinge, or merely a heightened pause is the open question. The TT renders the doxology as it stands and does not adjudicate the composition theories (cf. Introduction §B).

### G2. Two "surprises" frame the chapter (4:4, 4:12)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The verb **ξενίζω** (*xenizō*, "to find strange / be surprised," related to *xenos*, "stranger / foreign") brackets the chapter's two social scenes. At 4:4 the **outsiders** "are surprised" (*xenizontai*) that the believers no longer join the old life; at 4:12 the believers are told **not** to "be surprised" (*mē xenizesthe*) at the burning trial "as *though* a strange *thing* (*xenou*) were happening." The same root names both the pagans' bafflement at the believers' withdrawal and the believers' temptation to find their suffering strange — a deliberate verbal echo that the TT keeps by rendering both with "surprised / strange." The word also resonates with the letter's identity-language of the readers as *xenoi*, "strangers / resident-aliens" (cf. 1:1, 17; 2:11): the people who are themselves strangers must not find suffering strange.

## I. The World at the Time

> **The recipients' world.** 1 Peter addresses believers scattered across five provinces of Roman Asia Minor (1:1) in the later first century — for the political, economic, and provincial setting (the Roman administration of Asia Minor; the Greek-speaking cities and the Jewish diaspora communities), see the book Introduction §C (Dating) and §I (Historical Setting), which this companion does not duplicate. The entries below address the specific social world that chapter 4 foregrounds: the **pagan communal life** the believers had withdrawn from (4:3-4), the **nature of the "fiery trial"** (4:12), and the **name "Christian"** as an outsiders' label (4:16). Because the date of the letter is disputed (Introduction §B-§C), these entries describe the *kind* of world the readers inhabited without fixing the year.

---

### I1. Daily Life / Religion — Pagan communal life and the cost of withdrawal (4:3-4)
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The vice-list of 4:3 (drinking-bouts, carousings, "lawless idolatries") names the ordinary fabric of civic and social life in a Greco-Roman city, where religion was not a separable compartment but woven through nearly every public occasion. **Trade and craft guilds** (*collegia*) held common meals under the patronage of a god, with sacrifices, libations, and the consumption of sacrificed meat; **civic festivals** marked the calendar with processions, public sacrifices, games, and feasts honoring the city's gods and, increasingly, the emperor (the **imperial cult**); private **banquets and symposia** opened with libations to the gods and could move into the drinking and revelry the vice-list catalogs. To decline participation — to stop attending the guild dinner, skip the festival sacrifice, refuse the libation — was not a private spiritual choice but a **visible social secession**: it looked like disloyalty to the city's gods, ingratitude to patrons, and a rejection of one's neighbors, trade-partners, and family. This is precisely the friction 4:4 describes: the outsiders are "surprised" the believers no longer "run with" them, and so they "slander" them (→ §A2). The believers' withdrawal from cult-saturated social life is the concrete behavior behind the hostility the whole letter addresses.

**Source:** MacMullen, R., *Paganism in the Roman Empire*, Yale University Press, 1981, pp. 18-48; Harland, P.A., *Associations, Synagogues, and Congregations*, Fortress Press, 2003, pp. 55-87, 177-212; Williams, T.B., *Persecution in 1 Peter*, Brill, 2012, pp. 237-296.

### I2. Political / Religion — The "fiery trial": social hostility or state persecution? (4:12)
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — POSSIBLE]**

The nature of the suffering behind the "burning *trial*" (4:12) is the central historical debate of the letter, and it bears on dating without resolving it (Introduction §C-§D). On one reading, the suffering is **localized social ostracism and mob hostility** — the slander (4:4), reviling (4:14), and reproach (cf. 2:12; 3:16) that fell on a group that had withdrawn from civic and religious life (§I1); on this view the language fits sporadic, unofficial pressure anywhere in the provinces and need not imply formal trials. On the other reading, the suffering reflects **formal state persecution** — magistrate-level prosecution "for the name" (§A6, §C1) — associated by older scholarship with Nero (60s) or, on some accounts, with the situation the **Pliny-Trajan correspondence** describes in Bithynia (one of the very provinces of 1:1) c. **112 CE**, where the governor tried people simply for *being* Christians (*Epistulae* 10.96). The Pliny letters are the clearest evidence for "punishing the name" and are often invoked in dating discussions; but most scholars now judge 1 Peter's own language — slander, reproach, social shaming, scattered and informal — **insufficient to establish organized, empire-wide prosecution**, and the verbal echo with Pliny does not by itself date the letter or settle which kind of suffering is in view. The TT records the debate and takes no position on the date or the mechanism (Rule 29).

**Source:** Pliny the Younger, *Epistulae* 10.96-97, Loeb Classical Library; Williams, T.B., *Persecution in 1 Peter*, Brill, 2012, pp. 179-236, 297-336; Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, 1996, pp. 28-36.

### I3. Religion / Social Structure — The origin and use of the name "Christian" (4:16)
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

"Christian" (*Christianos*, 4:16) was, on the best evidence, a name applied to the group **from outside** rather than chosen by it. The Latinate *-ianos* suffix marks partisans of a leader (as "Herodians"), and the three earliest surviving uses all sit in **outsiders'** mouths or hostile/forensic settings: Acts 11:26 reports the name first given at Antioch (passive, "were called"), Acts 26:28 has the Roman-allied Agrippa use it, and the Roman historian **Tacitus** (*Annals* 15.44, on Nero's blaming of the fire of Rome) reports that "the crowd called them **Chrestiani**." In Roman administrative practice the name itself could become the charge: Pliny interrogates the accused on whether they are "Christians" and punishes accordingly (*Epistulae* 10.96; → §I2, §C1). Against this background, 4:16's instruction — if one suffers "as a Christian," not to "be ashamed" but to "glorify God **in this name**" — is a deliberate **reclaiming** of an outsiders' slur as a badge of honor, a move the second-century martyr-confession "*Christianus sum*" ("I am a Christian") would carry further (→ §F2). The TT keeps the established English "Christian" and locates the social history here.

**Source:** Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44, Loeb Classical Library; Wilken, R.L., *The Christians as the Romans Saw Them*, 2nd ed., Yale University Press, 2003, pp. 1-30; Elliott, J.H., *1 Peter*, Anchor Bible 37B, 2000, pp. 789-794, 803-805.
