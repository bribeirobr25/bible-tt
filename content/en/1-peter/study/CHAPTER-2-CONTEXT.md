## HOW TO USE THIS COMPANION

This file contains background material organized by topic. Each entry carries **two labels:**

**What kind of claim?** (tells you the source of the information)
- **TEXTUAL** — derived directly from the Greek text
- **STRONG INFERENCE** — logically entailed by the text, not stated explicitly
- **POSSIBLE INFERENCE** — one legitimate reading among others
- **COMPARATIVE PARALLEL** — attested in another ancient source
- **LATER RECEPTION** — post-biblical reading
- **HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL** — based on material or historical evidence
- **SPECULATION** — no strong textual or evidential basis

**How certain?** (tells you the confidence level)
- **VERIFIED** — strong evidence, wide agreement
- **PROBABLE** — well-supported but debated
- **POSSIBLE** — one reasonable reading among others
- **UNCERTAIN** — genuinely unclear
- **SPECULATIVE** — must be rare and clearly flagged
- **DOCUMENTED** — historically attested reception (for LATER RECEPTION entries)

**Reading principle:** Start with TEXTUAL entries — those come from the Greek itself. Then explore outward to parallels, reception, and comparison. The companion presents; you assess.

## A. Source Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. *Logikon* — "of the word" or "rational/spiritual"? (2:2)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the ambiguity); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the sense)]**

At 2:2 the readers are to long for **τὸ λογικὸν ἄδολον γάλα** (*to logikon adolon gala*), "the pure *logikon* milk." The adjective **λογικός** (*logikos*) is genuinely double. It can mean "of the word" — built on **λόγος** (*logos*), tying directly back to "the living and abiding word of God" by which they were "born anew" in 1:23-25 — or "rational / spiritual," as in **λογικὴ λατρεία** ("reasonable/spiritual worship," Rom 12:1). The TT keeps both with the slash {a:milk *of the* word/spiritual milk} rather than choosing.

The choice is not trivial. The "word" reading makes the milk the message that gave them new birth, an extension of 1:23-25; the "rational/spiritual" reading makes it nourishment proper to the renewed mind, contrasted with the merely physical. **ἄδολον** ("unadulterated, pure," literally "without guile") puns back on the **δόλος** (*dolos*, "deceit") of the vice-list in 2:1 — the milk is "deceit-free" where the put-away vices were full of deceit. The TT preserves the open adjective and the pun rather than resolving to a single English gloss.

**Source:** Liddell, H.G. and Scott, R., *Greek-English Lexicon*, 9th ed., rev. Jones, 1940, s.v. λογικός; Michaels, J.R., *1 Peter*, Word Biblical Commentary 49, Word Books, 1988, pp. 87-90.

### A2. The stone-catena — three texts stacked (2:6-8)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Verses 6-8 stack three "stone" scriptures in sequence, and the TT keeps each one recognizable rather than blending them into a single quotation. The sequence is: Isaiah 28:16 ("a chosen, precious cornerstone… will never be put to shame," 2:6), Psalm 118:22 ("the stone the builders rejected… became the head of the corner," 2:7), and Isaiah 8:14 ("a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense," 2:8). The linking word across all three is **λίθος** (*lithos*, "stone"), the catchword that holds the chain together — a known Jewish exegetical technique (*gezerah shawah*, linking texts by a shared word).

The result is a single argument built from three voices: the same stone is "precious" to those who believe (Isa 28) and "stumbling" to those who do not (Isa 8), with the rejected-then-vindicated stone of Psalm 118 in the middle. Only the Isaiah 28:16 portion is God's first-person speech ("I lay in Zion"), so the TT marks that clause **@@…@@** and leaves the other two unmarked (the psalmist's and the prophet's third-person voice) — see the verse-by-verse notes at 2:6-8 and §A6 of the editorial scheme.

### A3. Israel's titles transferred to the readers (2:9-10)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the citation); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE (for the source-mix)]**

Verse 9 applies four titles of Israel to the letter's scattered readers: **γένος ἐκλεκτόν, βασίλειον ἱεράτευμα, ἔθνος ἅγιον, λαὸς εἰς περιποίησιν** ("a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for possession"). The wording fuses Exodus 19:6 LXX ("a royal priesthood and a holy nation") with Isaiah 43:20-21 ("my chosen race… a people I acquired/formed for myself"). Verse 10 then adds Hosea's reversal — "once not a people, now a people of God; once not pitied, now pitied" (Hos 1:9-10; 2:23).

The TT renders the transfer exactly as the Greek has it and adds **no** interpretive gloss in either direction — neither a supersessionist "the church has replaced Israel" nor a corrective denial of the transfer (Rule 3, restraint cutting both ways). The text states that these titles are now spoken over the readers; it does not state a theology of what that does to Israel's own standing. That question — and the long history of reading it as supersession — is reception, handled in §F1, not a datum of the verse. The verse-by-verse note at 2:9 flags the same restraint.

### A4. "As they were appointed" — *etethēsan* (2:8)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexeme); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the scope)]**

The end of 2:8 reads **εἰς ὃ καὶ ἐτέθησαν** (*eis ho kai etethēsan*), "to which also they were appointed/set." **τίθημι** (*tithēmi*) is "to place, set, appoint," and the aorist passive here leaves God as the unstated agent. The clause is genuinely ambiguous in what it attaches the appointing *to*: to the *stumbling* (they were appointed to stumble), or to the *disobedience* (they stumble because they disobey, and that disobedience is what they were set toward), or — on a third reading — the whole condition. The grammar does not decide.

This is one of the New Testament's sharpest pieces of election/destiny language, and the TT renders it without resolving its scope (Rule 13): "to which also they were appointed." It does not soften the passive into "they brought it on themselves," nor harden it into "God predestined them to stumble." The verse-by-verse note marks the referent of "appointed" as **Uncertain — rendered, not resolved**.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. τίθημι; Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, Fortress Press, 1996, pp. 161-163.

### A5. *Hypogrammos* — the writing-copy to trace (2:21)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

At 2:21 the Messiah's unjust suffering is called a **ὑπογραμμός** (*hypogrammos*) — a New Testament *hapax legomenon* (occurring only here). The word is concrete and pedagogical: it names the *writing-copy* a schoolteacher set at the top of a page or wax tablet, the model letters or lines a child traced over to learn to write. The readers are to "follow in his footsteps (**ἴχνεσιν**, *ichnesin*)" — the metaphor shifts from tracing letters to walking in tracks, both imitation-images.

The TT keeps the vivid "writing-copy / example" sense rather than flattening to "model," because the word grounds the whole household-code panel: the servants' endurance under unjust suffering (2:18-20) is not a social rule but the *tracing* of a pattern set by the Messiah's own unjust suffering (2:22-25). The pedagogy-image is what binds the ethic to the Christology.

**Source:** Bauer, W., *A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament* (BDAG), 3rd ed., rev. Danker, University of Chicago Press, 2000, s.v. ὑπογραμμός; Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. ὑπογραμμός.

### A6. The Isaiah 53 weave — allusion, not formula-citation (2:22-25)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexical contacts); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE (for the sustained dependence)]**

Verses 22-25 are the densest use of Isaiah's fourth Servant Song (Isa 52:13–53:12) in the New Testament, but — unlike the stone-catena (2:6-8) — they are **woven**, not introduced by a citation formula. The contacts are verbal and sequential: 2:22 ("committed no sin, nor was deceit found in his mouth") tracks Isaiah 53:9 LXX ("he did no lawlessness, nor was deceit in his mouth"); 2:24 ("himself bore up our sins… by whose wound you were healed") tracks Isaiah 53:4, 12 ("he bears our sins") and 53:5 ("by his bruise we were healed"); 2:25 ("you were straying like sheep") tracks Isaiah 53:6 ("all we like sheep have gone astray").

Because the dependence is allusive, the TT renders the words on their own terms and does not insert "as Isaiah said" into the running text; the Servant background is carried in the notes and the PROPHECY companion. This is the same Servant figure that Acts 3 calls God's **παῖς** (*pais*, "servant," Acts 3:13, 26) — the two passages draw on one body of Isaianic material. **ξύλον** (*xylon*, "tree/wood") for the cross (2:24) is a distinct strand, drawing Deuteronomy 21:23 ("hanged on a tree") into the picture (cf. Acts 5:30; 10:39; Gal 3:13). → See the PROPHECY companion for the Servant-Song mechanics.

**Source:** Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter*, Hermeneia, 1996, pp. 192-205; Schutter, W.L., *Hermeneutic and Composition in 1 Peter*, Mohr Siebeck, 1989, pp. 138-144.

## B. Ancient Near Eastern and Ancient World Parallels

### B1. The "stone" texts in Second Temple use
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The three stone-texts of 2:6-8 (Isa 28:16; Ps 118:22; Isa 8:14) were already being read together and messianically before the New Testament, which makes 1 Peter's catena a participant in an existing Jewish exegetical stream rather than an ad hoc invention. At Qumran, Isaiah 28:16 is read of the community as God's sure foundation (1QS 8:7-8, "a tested wall, a precious cornerstone"), and the "stone" imagery for the elect community recurs in the *Hodayot* (1QH). The same three texts cluster in the Gospels applied to the Messiah — Psalm 118:22 at Mark 12:10 (par. Matt 21:42; Luke 20:17), Isaiah 8:14 and 28:16 in the stumbling-stone material at Romans 9:32-33, where Paul fuses the very same two Isaiah texts as 1 Peter does.

The convergence of 1 Peter, Romans, and the Qumran usage points to a shared pool of "stone" testimonia in early Judaism and the early Jesus-movement. The parallel shows a common interpretive grammar; it does not by itself establish which writer drew on which.

**Source:** Vermes, G., *The Complete Dead Sea Scrolls in English*, rev. ed., Penguin, 2011 (1QS 8:7-8); Elliott, J.H., *1 Peter*, Anchor Bible 37B, Doubleday, 2000, pp. 410-430; Lindars, B., *New Testament Apologetic*, SCM Press, 1961, pp. 169-186.

### B2. "Living stone" and the community as temple
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The image of the elect community as a built structure — a "spiritual house" of "living stones" with "a holy priesthood" (2:5) — has a close analogue at Qumran, where the *Community Rule* describes the council of the community as "a holy house for Israel and a foundation of the holy of holies for Aaron… a tested wall, a precious cornerstone" (1QS 8:5-9). There too a human community takes on temple-and-priesthood language and reads its own identity through Isaiah 28:16. The "spiritual sacrifices" the readers offer (2:5) parallel the Qumran notion of prayer and right conduct as the offerings of a community that has, in effect, relocated the temple into itself.

The parallel illuminates a recognizable Second Temple move — a marginal community reimagining itself as the true temple — without implying that 1 Peter borrowed from Qumran; both draw on the shared scriptural stock and a shared situation of distance from the Jerusalem cult.

**Source:** Gärtner, B., *The Temple and the Community in Qumran and the New Testament*, Cambridge University Press, 1965, pp. 72-88; Elliott, J.H., *1 Peter*, Anchor Bible 37B, 2000, pp. 408-418.

### B3. Household codes in the Greco-Roman moral tradition
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

The instruction to subordinate members of a household (2:18-25 servants; continued in 3:1-7 wives, with husbands addressed) belongs to a recognizable ancient genre, the *Haustafel* or "household code," with deep roots in Greco-Roman discussion of household management (*oikonomia*). Aristotle's *Politics* (1.1253b–1260b) treats the three paired relationships of the household — master/slave, husband/wife, father/child — as the building blocks of the well-ordered city, and the topic recurs in Hellenistic moralists (e.g. the Stoic Hierocles; pseudo-Aristotle's *Oeconomica*) and in Hellenistic-Jewish writers (Philo, *Hypothetica* 7.3, 14; Josephus, *Against Apion* 2.190-219). The New Testament codes (Col 3:18–4:1; Eph 5:21–6:9; 1 Pet 2:18–3:7) share the genre's shape while differing in motivation.

The distinctive features of 1 Peter's version are visible against the genre: it addresses the subordinate parties as moral agents in their own right (the servants directly, 2:18), it grounds the conduct in the Messiah's example rather than in natural hierarchy (2:21-25), and — unusually — it gives the servant panel by far the most space, with no matching "masters" panel. The genre comparison clarifies what is conventional and what is not; it does not settle how the passage should be applied (see §F2).

**Source:** Balch, D.L., *Let Wives Be Submissive: The Domestic Code in 1 Peter*, Society of Biblical Literature / Scholars Press, 1981, pp. 23-62; Elliott, J.H., *1 Peter*, Anchor Bible 37B, 2000, pp. 503-510.

## C. Historical Context

### C1. *Oiketai* — household slaves and the realities of the institution
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The servants addressed at 2:18 are **οἰκέται** (*oiketai*), specifically *household* slaves, not the general **δοῦλοι** (*douloi*) or rural/agricultural slaves — the people who lived inside the home and served at close quarters. Slavery was pervasive and legally fundamental in the early Roman empire: estimates put the enslaved at a large minority of the population of Italy and the cities, and household slaves performed domestic, administrative, educational, and skilled work. A household slave had no legal personhood in the full sense, was subject to the *paterfamilias*'s authority including corporal punishment, and — as 2:18-20 presupposes — could be beaten justly or unjustly at a master's discretion. Manumission was possible and not uncommon, but it was the master's to grant.

The text describes this world without endorsing it. It addresses the *oiketai* as moral agents who may suffer "unjustly" (2:19) under a "crooked" master (2:18) — language that names the institution's cruelty rather than excusing it — while taking no position in the main text on the institution's legitimacy (Rule 3; editorial-log P-009). This historical note supplies the social facts the verse assumes; it does not convert them into an evaluation, which the chapter itself withholds.

**Source:** Bradley, K.R., *Slavery and Society at Rome*, Cambridge University Press, 1994, pp. 1-30, 81-106; Harrill, J.A., *Slaves in the New Testament*, Fortress Press, 2006, pp. 85-117.

### C2. Emperor and governors — the structure of Roman provincial rule
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The civic instruction of 2:13-17 maps onto the actual chain of Roman authority that the readers, scattered across Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia (1:1), lived under. "A king as supreme" (**βασιλεύς**, *basileus*, 2:13) is the standard Greek term for the **emperor** (Latin *imperator*/*princeps*); "governors as those sent through him" (**ἡγεμόσιν**, *hēgemosin*, 2:14) names the provincial governors — proconsuls in senatorial provinces like Asia and Bithynia-Pontus, imperial legates or procurators elsewhere — described exactly by their delegated function: to punish wrongdoers and commend the good. The phrase "sent through him" captures the legal reality that a governor's authority derived from the emperor.

The framing is notably limited: authority is to be honored "for the Lord's sake" (2:13), the emperor receives **honor** (*timē*, the same verb used of "everyone," 2:17) while **fear** is reserved for God alone — a calibration that stops short of the absolute reverence the imperial cult sought (§C3). For readers in Asia Minor, where Roman administration was the visible framework of daily life and the locus of any legal danger they faced, this was a concrete and pointed instruction.

**Source:** Millar, F., *The Emperor in the Roman World*, 2nd ed., Duckworth, 1992, pp. 363-456; Winter, B.W., *Seek the Welfare of the City*, Eerdmans, 1994, pp. 12-40.

### C3. The imperial cult in Asia Minor
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The provinces named in 1:1 were precisely the heartland of the imperial cult — the organized worship of Rome and the emperor. Asia and Bithynia had erected provincial temples to Rome and Augustus from 29 BCE onward (Pergamum, Nicomedia), and by the late first century the cult permeated civic life: temples, priesthoods (often the most prestigious local offices), festivals, games, sacrifices, and oaths sworn by the emperor's *genius*. Participation was a normal expression of civic loyalty and social belonging, woven into the calendar and the public honors of every city. The famous early-second-century correspondence of Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia-Pontus (c. 110 CE), shows the cult used as a test: suspected Christians were required to invoke the gods, offer incense and wine to the emperor's image, and curse Christ (*Letters* 10.96-97) — exactly the social-religious pressure 1 Peter's readers were beginning to feel.

This is the charged background of "honor the emperor" (2:17). The instruction grants the emperor genuine honor while withholding the worship the cult demanded — a distinction that would itself mark the readers out and help explain why they were "spoken against" (2:12). The note supplies the cultic context; the TT renders only the honor/fear calibration the Greek states (§C2), without reading Pliny's later test back into the letter.

**Source:** Price, S.R.F., *Rituals and Power: The Roman Imperial Cult in Asia Minor*, Cambridge University Press, 1984, pp. 53-100, 234-248; Pliny the Younger, *Letters* 10.96-97, Loeb Classical Library.

### C4. *Paroikoi kai parepidēmoi* — the legal-social status of resident aliens
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED (for the terms); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the literal-status reading)]**

The readers are addressed as **παροίκους καὶ παρεπιδήμους** ("sojourners and exiles / resident-aliens and visiting-strangers," 2:11; cf. 1:1, 17). Both words name real legal-social categories in the Greco-Roman city. A **πάροικος** (*paroikos*) was a resident alien — a free person dwelling long-term in a city without its citizenship, lacking full civic rights, often economically and legally vulnerable. A **παρεπίδημος** (*parepidēmos*) was a visiting stranger, a temporary sojourner. Whether 1 Peter intends these as the readers' literal civic status (so J.H. Elliott: actual displaced resident-aliens) or as a metaphor for their estrangement from society as believers (the more common reading) is debated.

The TT keeps the dual-sense rendering and does not decide between the literal-status and metaphorical readings (the verse-by-verse note flags both). Either way, the social reality the terms evoke — life as a marginal, rights-poor, suspected outsider — illuminates the letter's whole ethic of honorable conduct under hostility (2:12, 15). → See §I for the social situation of a maligned minority.

**Source:** Elliott, J.H., *A Home for the Homeless: A Social-Scientific Criticism of 1 Peter*, 2nd ed., Fortress Press, 1990, pp. 21-58; Feldmeier, R., *Die Christen als Fremde*, Mohr Siebeck, 1992, pp. 8-22.

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. *Logikos* — the breadth of the word (2:2)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the range); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the intended sense)]**

**λογικός** (*logikos*) is a derivative of **λόγος** (*logos*) and carries the linked senses "pertaining to the word/speech" and "pertaining to reason, rational." In Stoic and Hellenistic-Jewish usage it leans toward "rational" — Philo speaks of the *logikē psychē* (the rational soul), and Romans 12:1 famously calls right worship **λογικὴ λατρεία** (usually "spiritual/reasonable worship"). But within 1 Peter the immediate context pulls the other way: 1:23-25 has just made "the living and abiding **word** (*logos*) of God" the seed of new birth, citing Isaiah 40, so "the *logikon* milk" most naturally extends that "word" — the message that begets is now the message that nourishes.

No single English word holds both. "Of the word" foregrounds the *logos* link to 1:23-25; "spiritual / rational" foregrounds the contrast with merely physical milk. The TT renders the slash {a:milk *of the* word/spiritual milk} and marks the sense **Uncertain**; the breadth, and its tie to the preceding chapter, is the point. The pairing with **ἄδολον** ("unguileful, pure," 2:2) and the metaphor of "newborn infants" (*artigennēta brephē*) keep the nourishment-image vivid whichever sense dominates.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. λογικός; Bauer, *BDAG*, s.v. λογικός; Michaels, J.R., *1 Peter*, WBC 49, 1988, pp. 87-90.

### D2. *Akrogōniaios* — "cornerstone" or "capstone"? (2:6)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the lexeme); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — UNCERTAIN (for the architectural referent)]**

**ἀκρογωνιαῖος** (*akrogōniaios*, 2:6, from Isa 28:16 LXX) is "the stone at the extreme corner / angle." Two architectural identifications are debated: a **foundation cornerstone** — the large, carefully cut stone at the base corner that sets the alignment of two walls — or a **capstone / keystone** at the top of a structure or arch. The foundation sense fits the "spiritual house being built" of 2:5 and the LXX of Isaiah 28:16 (a stone laid "in the foundations of Zion"); the capstone sense is argued chiefly from the parallel imagery of Psalm 118:22 ("head of the corner," *kephalē gōnias*, 2:7).

The TT renders "cornerstone," following the foundation sense that the building-metaphor and the Isaiah source favor, while the note records the capstone possibility. The word is rare enough — outside the LXX and the NT it is nearly unattested in this exact form before the Christian era — that the architectural precision cannot be pressed; what is secure is the "corner/angle" element and the load-bearing, alignment-setting role the stone plays in the argument.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. ἀκρογωνιαῖος; McKelvey, R.J., "Christ the Cornerstone," *New Testament Studies* 8 (1962), pp. 352-359; Bauer, *BDAG*, s.v. ἀκρογωνιαῖος.

### D3. *Hypotassō* — "submit / be subordinate" across the chapter
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

**ὑποτάσσω** (*hypotassō*) is the verb that binds 1 Peter's social ethic together. A compound of **ὑπό** ("under") + **τάσσω** ("arrange, order, station"), it is a military and administrative term for being ranked or stationed under an ordering authority; the middle/passive ("submit, subordinate oneself") often carries a voluntary, self-ordering nuance rather than coerced subjection. It appears for civic order ("submit to every human institution," 2:13), for household servants ("be submitting… to your masters," 2:18), and continues for wives (3:1) and into the general "all of you" (5:5). The TT renders it consistently "submit / be subordinate" so the recurrence stays visible, and flags the word-field in Tier-2 (editorial-log P-009).

Two features the TT preserves matter for reading the passage. First, the servants and wives are *addressed directly* as the subjects who do the submitting — they are moral agents, not objects of someone else's command. Second, the whole sequence is framed by 2:16 ("as free… as servants of God") and grounded "for the Lord's sake" (2:13) and in the Messiah's example (2:21) — the submission is voluntary self-ordering within a prior freedom and a prior allegiance to God, not an absolute or unqualified subjection. The TT renders the verb literally and lets that framing stand without importing a modern resolution.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. ὑποτάσσω; Bauer, *BDAG*, s.v. ὑποτάσσω; Elliott, J.H., *1 Peter*, Anchor Bible 37B, 2000, pp. 486-490.

### D4. *Mōlōps* and *iaomai* — "by his wound you were healed" (2:24)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Two health-words close the Servant weave. **μώλωψ** (*mōlōps*, 2:24) is a "welt, weal, bruise" — the raised mark left on the skin by a blow or a lash, the very mark a beaten slave would bear; it is the LXX word at Isaiah 53:5. **ἰάομαι** (*iaomai*) is "to heal, cure." The aorist passive **ἰάθητε** ("you were healed") makes the readers the recipients of a healing accomplished by the Servant's wounding — a deliberate paradox: the welt that ordinarily marks suffering inflicted *on* a victim is here the means by which others are healed.

The TT keeps the concrete "wound / welt" rather than a generalized "suffering," because the word's force in 2:18-25 is precise: it sits inside the panel addressed to *household servants* who themselves face being "beaten" (*kolaphizomenoi*, 2:20), and it presents the Messiah's body bearing the kind of mark a flogged slave would bear. The vocabulary binds the servants' situation to the Messiah's, which is the whole point of the *hypogrammos* (§A5). → see the verse-by-verse note at 2:24.

**Source:** Liddell and Scott, *Greek-English Lexicon*, s.v. μώλωψ, ἰάομαι; Hatch, E. and Redpath, H.A., *A Concordance to the Septuagint*, 2nd ed., Baker, 1998, s.v. μώλωψ (Isa 53:5).

## F. Later Reception in Other Traditions

### F1. The identity-titles transfer and the history of supersessionist reading
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The application of Israel's titles to the readers (2:9-10, "a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation… a people of God") became one of the principal texts in the later Christian doctrine that the church had succeeded to, or replaced, Israel as the people of God (supersessionism / "replacement theology"). From the second century (e.g. Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 11, 116-119, who calls the church "the true Israelite race") through the patristic and medieval traditions, 2:9 was read as transferring Israel's election wholesale to the church. The same Exodus-19 and Hosea material in 1 Peter and Romans 9 stood at the center of those debates. In the twentieth century, after the Shoah, much Christian and Jewish scholarship reassessed this reading, distinguishing the New Testament's *participation* language (gentiles brought *into* Israel's story) from later *replacement* claims.

Per Rule 3 and §A3, the TT renders only what 2:9-10 says — that these titles are now spoken over the readers — and adds **no** supersessionist gloss, and equally no corrective denial. The replacement reading and its modern reassessment are documented reception of the verse, recorded here, not lexical content of the Greek. The text states a transfer; it does not state a theology of Israel's displacement, and the companion does not supply one.

**Source:** Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho*, in *Ante-Nicene Fathers* vol. 1; Soulen, R.K., *The God of Israel and Christian Theology*, Fortress Press, 1996, pp. 1-56; Elliott, J.H., *1 Peter*, Anchor Bible 37B, 2000, pp. 443-449.

### F2. The household-code servant panel and its reception — including slavery debates
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The servant panel of 2:18-25 has one of the most fraught reception histories of any New Testament passage. Across late antiquity and the medieval period it was read, alongside the other household codes (Eph 6:5-9; Col 3:22–4:1; the Onesimus material in Philemon), as scriptural warrant for the existing order of slavery and obedience. In the era of transatlantic slavery (16th–19th centuries) it became a standard proof-text for proslavery argument, cited to enjoin enslaved people to submit "even to the crooked" master and to suffer without resistance; "slave Bibles" prepared for enslaved people in the British colonies retained such texts while excising liberation material (e.g. the Exodus). Abolitionist readers contested this, arguing from the same scriptures (the *imago Dei*, the Exodus, Gal 3:28, Philemon read as undermining the institution) that slavery was incompatible with the gospel. Modern interpreters widely emphasize that the passage describes conduct *within* an institution it neither created nor commends, addresses the enslaved as moral agents, and grounds endurance in the Messiah's solidarity with the unjustly suffering — not in any divine sanction for slavery itself.

This entire history is **reception**, not the meaning of the Greek. The TT, following editorial-log P-009, renders the passage faithfully (the servants' submission, the "crooked" master, the unjust suffering, the Messiah as *hypogrammos*), records the interpretive history here and in Tier-2, and **takes no position in the main text** on the institution — neither endorsing it (which the passage does not do) nor reading a modern abolition ethic back into a first-century text (Rule 3, restraint in both directions). The note at 2:18 and §C1 supply the social facts; this entry supplies the history of use; the verse states only what it states.

**Source:** Glancy, J.A., *Slavery in Early Christianity*, Oxford University Press, 2002, pp. 102-156; Harrill, J.A., *Slaves in the New Testament*, Fortress Press, 2006, pp. 165-192; Callahan, A.D., Horsley, R.A., and Smith, A. (eds.), *Slavery in Text and Interpretation* (Semeia 83/84), Society of Biblical Literature, 1998.

### F3. "Royal priesthood" and the priesthood-of-all-believers tradition
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The phrase "a royal priesthood… a holy priesthood" (2:5, 9) became a key text in later Christian reflection on ministry and the laity. While patristic and medieval reading generally held it alongside an ordained priesthood, the sixteenth-century Reformers — Martin Luther especially (*To the Christian Nobility*, 1520) — made 2:9 a foundation of the "priesthood of all believers," arguing that the text grants priestly standing to the whole body of the faithful and not to a clerical caste alone. The phrase remains a touchstone in debates over ministry, lay vocation, and church order across confessions.

This is documented reception, not a datum of the Greek; the text says the readers collectively *are* a priesthood offering spiritual sacrifices (2:5), without addressing the later question of ordained ministry. The TT renders the corporate priesthood-language as it stands and leaves the ecclesiological debate to its history.

**Source:** Luther, M., *To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation* (1520), in *Luther's Works*, vol. 44, Fortress Press, 1966; Elliott, J.H., *The Elect and the Holy*, Brill, 1966, pp. 219-228.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. The *chrēstos* / *Christos* pun (2:3)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the data); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE (for the intent)]**

At 2:3, "you tasted that the Lord is **good**" cites Psalm 34:8 (LXX 33:9), where the word for "good/kind" is **χρηστός** (*chrēstos*). In the Koine of the period **χρηστός** ("good, kind") and **Χριστός** (*Christos*, "Messiah/Christ") were pronounced nearly identically (both roughly *khristós*, the *ē*/*i* distinction having largely collapsed), and outsiders are known to have confused the two — Suetonius reports disturbances at the instigation of one "Chrestus" (*Claudius* 25.4), and early Christians were sometimes called *Chrestiani*. Whether 1 Peter intends the readers to hear "the Lord is *Christos*" beneath "the Lord is *chrēstos*" cannot be proven, but the immediate pivot in 2:4 to "coming to *him*" (the Messiah, the living stone) makes the pun plausible and probably deliberate. The TT renders "good" and leaves the wordplay to this note.

### G2. Why a servant panel but no masters panel?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED (for the data); POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE (for the explanation)]**

1 Peter's household code is lopsided: it gives a full panel to *household servants* (2:18-25) and to *wives* (3:1-6) and a brief word to *husbands* (3:7), but — unlike Colossians 3:22–4:1 and Ephesians 6:5-9 — it includes **no** address to masters at all. The most common inference is that the letter's communities were composed largely of socially subordinate members (slaves, wives of unbelieving husbands) and few or no slave-owning believers, so the code addresses the situations actually present; another inference is rhetorical emphasis on the suffering-as-witness theme, which the subordinate parties most embody. The text states the asymmetry; the reason is an inference, reasonable but not stated.

### G3. *Episkopos* — "overseer" of souls, and a verbal thread (2:25)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The Messiah is called the "shepherd and **overseer** (**ἐπίσκοπος**, *episkopos*) of your souls" (2:25). The same root runs quietly through the chapter: at 2:12 outsiders, "observing" the readers' good works, glorify God "in the day of **visitation** (**ἐπισκοπή**, *episkopē*)" — God's "visiting," in mercy or judgment. The TT renders *episkopos* "overseer" (not the later ecclesiastical "bishop," which would import a developed church office into a title here applied to the Messiah himself) and "visitation" at 2:12, leaving the shared *episkop-* root visible as a thread without overstating a deliberate link. It is a small window onto how the chapter's vocabulary circles back on itself.

## I. The World at the Time

> **Cross-reference:** 1 Peter addresses scattered believers in five provinces of Asia Minor (Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, Bithynia — 1:1) in the second half of the first century CE. For the broad Roman-imperial setting of the period, see the John 1 companion (Section I, `en/john/study/CHAPTER-1-CONTEXT.md`). Unlike the Gospels/Acts companions, the world that matters for 1 Peter 2 is **the recipients' world** — the Greco-Roman household, honor/shame culture, slavery, and the imperial order — not the world of a Judean narrative. The entries below address those specific settings. Two scenarios are distinguished by the social vantage point the chapter's instructions presuppose.

---

### SCENARIO A — The dominant culture's vantage (the household and honor/shame order the readers lived inside)

#### IA-1. Social Structure — The Greco-Roman household (*oikos*) and the *paterfamilias*
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The household code of 2:18-25 (continued in 3:1-7) presupposes the *oikos* / *familia* as the basic unit of Greco-Roman society — a structure far larger than the modern nuclear family, comprising the male head (*paterfamilias*), his wife, children, slaves, freedmen, and dependents, all under his legal and economic authority. The household was treated by ancient political thought (Aristotle onward) as the microcosm and foundation of the city: its good order was a civic concern, and the subordination of wife, child, and slave to the head was held to be natural and necessary to social stability. Religion, too, was household-based — the *paterfamilias* led the household cult, and a household commonly followed the head's religion. For 1 Peter's readers, the address to *servants* and *wives* as believers within households whose heads might not share their faith (3:1) was therefore socially explosive: it treated subordinate members as moral and religious agents in their own right, against the grain of the *oikos* order.

**Source:** Balch, D.L., *Let Wives Be Submissive*, Scholars Press, 1981; Saller, R.P., *Patriarchy, Property and Death in the Roman Family*, Cambridge University Press, 1994, pp. 71-101.

#### IA-2. Social Structure — Honor and shame as the governing social values
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The Mediterranean world of the first century operated on an honor/shame value system: honor (public reputation, standing, the esteem of one's peers) was the central social good, and shame (the loss of standing, public disgrace) the central social danger. Conduct was calibrated to what brought honor and avoided shame in the eyes of the community, and one's worth was largely conferred by others' judgment rather than self-defined. Chapter 2 is saturated with this idiom: the readers are to keep their conduct "honorable" so that outsiders, despite "speaking against" them, may end by glorifying God (2:12); they "honor" everyone, the brotherhood, and the emperor (2:17); the stone is "precious"/"honored" (*entimos*, *timē*) to believers but a cause of "shame" avoided for those who trust it ("will never be put to shame," 2:6). The letter works *within* the honor/shame grammar even as it redefines whose esteem ultimately counts — God's "day of visitation" (2:12), not the neighbors' approval.

**Source:** Malina, B.J., *The New Testament World: Insights from Cultural Anthropology*, 3rd ed., Westminster John Knox, 2001, pp. 27-57; deSilva, D.A., *Honor, Patronage, Kinship and Purity*, InterVarsity Press, 2000, pp. 23-93.

#### IA-3. Social Structure — Slavery in the early Roman empire
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Slavery was an unquestioned, structurally fundamental institution of the early empire, and the *oiketai* of 2:18 belonged to its domestic form. Enslaved people made up a large share of the urban population; they were acquired by birth, capture, sale, and debt, and worked across every sector from field and mine to skilled crafts, business management, medicine, and teaching. A household slave was legally property, subject to the head's authority and to corporal correction, and could be sold, willed, or freed at the owner's discretion; sexual use of slaves by owners was legally unremarkable. There was no organized abolition movement and little principled questioning of the institution as such in the surrounding culture, though manumission was frequent enough that freed slaves (*liberti*) formed a substantial social class. This is the world 2:18-20 describes — servants who may suffer "unjustly" under "crooked" masters — and the world the chapter addresses without endorsing (§C1, §F2).

**Source:** Bradley, K.R., *Slavery and Society at Rome*, Cambridge University Press, 1994; Harrill, J.A., *Slaves in the New Testament*, Fortress Press, 2006, pp. 85-117.

#### IA-4. Political — The emperor, the provinces, and the imperial cult
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The "king as supreme" and "governors" of 2:13-14 were the lived political reality of Asia Minor. By the later first century the provinces of 1:1 were long-settled parts of the empire, governed from Rome through proconsuls and legates, taxed and policed, and bound to the emperor by an elaborate web of honors. The imperial cult — temples, priesthoods, festivals, and sacrifices to Rome and the emperor — was especially developed in Asia and Bithynia, where it functioned as the public expression of loyalty and civic belonging (§C3); to abstain looked like disloyalty and antisocial impiety. The provincial elite competed for the cult's prestigious offices, and the calendar was punctuated by imperial festivals. Into this order 1 Peter directs genuine "honor" for the emperor and obedience to governors "for the Lord's sake" — while reserving "fear" for God alone (2:17), a calibration that quietly refuses the cult's totalizing claim even as it commends civic loyalty.

**Source:** Price, S.R.F., *Rituals and Power: The Roman Imperial Cult in Asia Minor*, Cambridge University Press, 1984; Winter, B.W., *Seek the Welfare of the City*, Eerdmans, 1994.

### SCENARIO B — The minority's vantage (the social situation of a maligned, suspected group)

#### IB-1. Social Structure — A maligned minority: slander, suspicion, and social pressure
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

From the readers' own vantage, chapter 2 presupposes a small, socially marginal group under sustained verbal hostility rather than (yet) systematic state persecution. The letter repeatedly assumes that outsiders "speak against" the believers "as evildoers" (2:12), spread "ignorance" and slander (2:15), and malign them (3:16) — the situation of a suspect minority whose withdrawal from ordinary social and cultic life (the temples, the festivals, the household cult, the imperial honors) read to neighbors as misanthropy and impiety. Early non-Christian notices confirm the suspicion: Tacitus calls the Christians a group "hated for their abominations" and convicted of "hatred of the human race" (*Annals* 15.44), and Pliny investigates them as a "depraved, excessive superstition" (*Letters* 10.96). The chapter's strategy — silence slander by visible good conduct (2:12, 15), not by argument or retaliation — fits a group managing hostility from a position of social weakness.

**Source:** Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44, Loeb Classical Library; Pliny the Younger, *Letters* 10.96-97, Loeb Classical Library; Elliott, J.H., *A Home for the Homeless*, 2nd ed., Fortress Press, 1990, pp. 78-100.

#### IB-2. Social Structure — Resident aliens and exiles: the *paroikoi* identity
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The self-description "sojourners and exiles" (*paroikoi kai parepidēmoi*, 2:11; cf. 1:1, 17) frames the readers' whole social situation, whether read as literal civic status or as metaphor for estrangement (§C4). Resident aliens in a Greco-Roman city were a real and recognizable class: free but without citizenship, lacking full legal protection and civic rights, often economically vulnerable, and dependent on the goodwill of patrons and neighbors. To address a whole community in those terms — and to add the harsher "exiles" — was to name a life lived on society's edge, never fully belonging, exposed to suspicion. The chapter turns this disadvantage into vocation: precisely *as* resident-aliens the readers are to live honorably (2:11-12), submitting to the institutions of a society to which they do not finally belong (2:13) "for the Lord's sake," and longing instead for the inheritance and the "day of visitation" their true belonging promises.

**Source:** Elliott, J.H., *A Home for the Homeless*, 2nd ed., Fortress Press, 1990, pp. 21-58; Feldmeier, R., *Die Christen als Fremde*, Mohr Siebeck, 1992; Bowe, B.E., in *The Catholic Biblical Quarterly* (on 1 Peter's social setting).
