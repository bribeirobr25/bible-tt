## 1. Hope under suffering — the living hope that holds through trial

**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 1, 2, 3, 4, 5

The letter's central pastoral arc runs from a hope *given* to a hope *tested* to a hope *vindicated*, and the vocabulary of hope (*elpis*) and suffering (*paschō / pathēma*) is laced through every chapter. It is **founded** in chapter 1: God "gave us new birth into a **living hope** through *the* resurrection" (1:3), and the readers "rejoice, *though* now for a little *while*… grieved by **various trials**," so that tested faith — "more precious than gold… tested through fire" — may be found to praise (1:6-7). It is **patterned** on the Messiah, whose "sufferings… and the glories after these" the prophets foresaw (1:11) and whose unjust suffering is the readers' model (2:21-23). It is **reframed** as participation in chapter 4: "do not be surprised at the burning *trial*… but insofar as you share in the **sufferings of the Messiah**, rejoice" (4:12-13), and being "reviled in *the* name of *the* Messiah" is itself blessedness (4:14). It is **resolved** in chapter 5: "the God of all grace… *after you have* **suffered a little**, will himself restore, support, strengthen, *and* establish *you*" (5:10). The TT keeps the suffering-language plain (the "burning trial," 4:12; the "various / many-colored trials," 1:6) and renders *elpis* consistently as "hope" (the locked term), so the reader can trace the single thread from the living hope of 1:3 to its promised restoration in 5:10.

**Source:** Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter* (Hermeneia), Fortress, 1996, on suffering and eschatological hope as the letter's controlling concern.

## 2. Exile and diaspora identity — the readers as resident-aliens

**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 1, 2

A cluster of "stranger" words frames the readers' whole situation, and the TT keeps each literal so the identity is not dissolved into metaphor. The letter opens by addressing them as "*the* elect **sojourners** (*parepidēmoi*) of *the* **dispersion** (*diaspora*)" scattered across five provinces (1:1) — casting them in Israel's exile identity from the first line. It returns in chapter 1 as a self-description of the present age: "conduct yourselves in fear *during* the time of your **sojourning** (*paroikia*)" (1:17). It becomes the **ground of the ethic** in chapter 2: "Beloved, I urge *you* as **sojourners and exiles** (*paroikous kai parepidēmous*) to abstain from the fleshly desires" (2:11) — those who do not belong to the present order live differently within it. The TT preserves the dual sense of the keyword (resident-alien *and* visiting-stranger) and declines to flatten *diaspora* into a bare "scattering," so the term carries both its literal social reality (foreigners in the provinces) and its Israel-shaped resonance. The motif sets the letter's posture: a people at home nowhere in the present age, and therefore free to be different in it.

**Source:** Elliott, J.H., *A Home for the Homeless: A Sociological Exegesis of 1 Peter*, Fortress, 1981, on the *paroikoi* and the readers' resident-alien status.

## 3. The new people of God — Israel's titles transferred

**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 1, 2

The scattered believers are given Israel's own identity-language, climaxing in chapter 2 but prepared from chapter 1. They are "**elect**" from the salutation (1:1-2), an inheritance-people whose "**inheritance** (*klēronomia*)… kept in *the* heavens" (1:4) echoes Israel's land-inheritance. In chapter 2 the transfer is made architecturally — they "come to him, a **living stone**… *and* you yourselves also, as living stones, are being built *as* a **spiritual house** into a **holy priesthood**" (2:4-5) — and then verbally, in the densest concentration of covenant titles in the NT: "you *are* a **chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for *God's* possession**" (2:9), reproducing Exodus 19:6 and Isaiah 43:20-21; "*you* who once *were* **not a people but now *are* a people of God**" (2:10), reworking Hosea's "Lo-Ammi → Ammi" reversal. The TT renders the transfer **exactly as the Greek has it, adding no supersessionist gloss** (Rule 3): the text applies Israel's titles to the readers without itself adjudicating Israel's standing, and the translation leaves that restraint intact. The motif binds the "stone" imagery (block 4) to the exile identity (block 2): the homeless sojourners are precisely the ones being built into God's house.

**Source:** Jobes, K.H., *1 Peter* (Baker Exegetical Commentary), Baker, 2005, on the ecclesial application of Israel's covenant designations.

## 4. Following the suffering Messiah — the example to trace

**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 1, 2, 3, 4, 5

The Messiah's suffering-then-glory is presented not only as the ground of salvation but as the **shape the readers' own lives take**, a christological pattern recurring across the letter. It is **announced** in chapter 1: the prophets' *pneuma* testified beforehand "to the **sufferings** *destined* for the Messiah and the **glories after these**" (1:11) — the suffering→glory template. It is made **imitable** in chapter 2: the Messiah's unjust suffering leaves "an **example** (*hypogrammos*) [a child's writing-copy to trace], so that you might follow in his **footsteps**" (2:21), drawn out through the Isaiah-53 weave (2:22-25). It becomes **participation** in chapters 3–4: the Messiah "suffered once for sins, *the* righteous for *the* unrighteous" (3:18), and the readers "**share in the sufferings of the Messiah**" (4:13). It shapes **leadership** in chapter 5: elders lead "not as **lording it over**… but becoming **examples** (*typoi*)" (5:3), Peter himself a "**fellow-elder and witness of the sufferings of the Messiah**" (5:1), under "the **chief Shepherd**" (5:4). The TT keeps the rare vivid terms literal — *hypogrammos* as a tracing-pattern (2:21), *typoi* as models (5:3) — so the *imitatio* logic is visible: the readers do not merely benefit from the Messiah's suffering, they reproduce its pattern.

**Source:** Michaels, J.R., *1 Peter* (Word Biblical Commentary), Word, 1988, on the *Vorbild* (exemplar) Christology of 1 Peter.

## 5. Holiness and honorable conduct — *anastrophē* as witness

**[STRONG INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**
**Chapters:** 1, 2, 3

A single keyword — *anastrophē* ("conduct / way of life") — threads an ethic of visible holiness through the letter, functioning as the readers' missionary strategy. It is **grounded** in chapter 1 in God's own holiness: "be holy in all *your* **conduct** (*anastrophē*)… because it is written, @@'Be holy, because I *am* holy'@@" (1:15-16, citing Leviticus), and the old "**way of life** (*anastrophē*) handed down from *the* fathers" is what they were ransomed *from* (1:18). It is turned **outward** in chapter 2: "keeping your **conduct** among the nations **honorable**, so that… observing *your* good works they may glorify God" (2:12) — conduct as the answer to slander. It is made **persuasive** in chapter 3: unbelieving husbands "may be won **without a word**, through the **conduct** of the wives… having observed your pure **conduct** in fear" (3:1-2), and a "**good conduct** in *the* Messiah" shames revilers (3:16). The TT renders *anastrophē* consistently ("conduct / way of life," the keyword flagged in the glossaries) rather than varying it, so the reader can see that holiness in this letter is not private piety but a publicly *legible* manner of living — the witness that "silences the ignorance of foolish people… by doing good" (2:15).

**Source:** Green, J.B., *1 Peter* (Two Horizons), Eerdmans, 2007, on *anastrophē* and the missional function of honorable conduct.

## 6. Submission and social order — render and flag, take no position

**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 2, 3, 5

The verb *hypotassō* ("submit / be subordinate") organizes the letter's most interpretively loaded material — the civic order and the household code — and the TT's policy across all of it is uniform: **render the Greek faithfully, flag the interpretive and reception history in the notes, and take no position in the main text** (Rule 3; editorial-log P-009). It governs **civic order** in chapter 2: "**Submit** to every human institution **for the Lord's sake** — whether to a king as supreme" (2:13), grounded not in the state's intrinsic authority but "for the Lord's sake," with the emperor *honored*, not *feared* (2:17). It governs the **household code** (2:18–3:7): "*Household* servants, *be* **submitting**… even… to the crooked" (2:18); "wives, *be* **submitting** to *your* own husbands" (3:1); the matriarchs "**submitting** to *their* own husbands" (3:5). It governs **church order** in chapter 5: "younger *ones*, **submit** to *the* elders; and all *of you*, clothe yourselves with **humility**" (5:5). The TT keeps the difficult terms literal — "weaker vessel" *and*, in the same breath, "co-heir of *the* grace of life" (3:7); the servant-submission grounded in the Messiah's example (2:21-25), not in an endorsement of the institution — and relocates the reception history to Tier-2 and the companions. The motif's own internal checks (mutuality, "co-heir," "so that your prayers may not be hindered," the limited "for the Lord's sake") are left visible rather than smoothed.

**Source:** Achtemeier, P.J., *1 Peter* (Hermeneia), Fortress, 1996, on the *Haustafel* (household code) and the *hypotassō* word-field.

## 7. The Hebrew Bible as the church's Scripture — quoted, echoed, typological

**[STRONG INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**
**Chapters:** 1, 2, 3, 4, 5

For its size, 1 Peter is among the most Scripture-saturated books in the NT: the Hebrew Bible is not decoration but the **argumentative substance**, deployed in three modes the Prophecy companions track — formula-quoted, woven/echoed, and typological. **Formula citation** carries the major turns: Isaiah 40:6-8 closing chapter 1 ("all flesh *is* like grass… the word of *the* Lord endures," 1:24-25); the stone-catena of Isaiah 28:16 + Psalm 118:22 + Isaiah 8:14 (2:6-8); Proverbs 3:34 at 5:5. **Allusive weaving** carries the christological heart: Isaiah 53 threaded through 2:22-25 (the densest NT use of the fourth Servant Song, never formula-marked); Psalm 34 sounded at 2:3 and quoted at 3:10-12; Exodus echoes (the "gird your loins" of 1:13; the Passover lamb of 1:19; the "mighty hand" of 5:6). **Typology** carries the cruxes: Noah's flood as the antitype of baptism (3:20-21). The TT renders citations **at face value, preserving where the Greek follows the LXX against the Hebrew** (e.g. Isaiah 40 at 1:24-25) rather than harmonizing, and marks only God's *first-person* word inside a citation as divine speech (1:16; 2:6), leaving the prophet's or psalmist's voice unmarked (Rule 30). The motif shows a letter that argues *from* Israel's Scripture as its own — the same Scripture the gospels and Acts apply to the Messiah (the stone-texts; the Servant).

**Source:** Schutter, W.L., *Hermeneutic and Composition in 1 Peter* (WUNT 2/30), Mohr Siebeck, 1989, on the citation, allusion, and homiletic use of the Old Testament.

## 8. Grace — the letter's frame, from greeting to farewell

**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 1, 2, 4, 5

*Charis* ("grace / favor") opens and closes the letter and recurs at its hinges, so that the whole is, by the author's own summary, an exposition of grace. It **frames** the greeting and the blessing: "may **grace** and peace be multiplied to you" (1:2), and the "**grace** *coming* to you" that the prophets searched out (1:10), the "**grace** being brought… at *the* revelation of Yeshua" on which to set hope completely (1:13). It surprises in the household code, where enduring *unjust* suffering "because of conscience *toward* God" is twice named "**this *is* grace** before God" (2:19-20) — *charis* as what finds favor with God. It funds the community: "as good stewards of *the* **varied grace** of God" (4:10). It frames the ending: "God… gives **grace** to *the* humble" (5:5), "the God of all **grace**… will himself restore" (5:10), and the explicit summary of the whole: "I wrote to you briefly… testifying *that* this is *the* **true grace of God**; **in which stand**" (5:12). The TT renders *charis* consistently as "grace / favor" (declining to vary it into "thanks," "kindness," or "credit" without flag — note the explicit "this *is* grace" of 2:19-20), so the reader can see the *inclusio*: the letter that begins with grace multiplied ends by naming itself a witness to "the true grace of God," and tells the reader to stand in it.

**Source:** Jobes, K.H., *1 Peter* (Baker Exegetical Commentary), Baker, 2005, on *charis* as the letter's framing theme and the summary of 5:12.
