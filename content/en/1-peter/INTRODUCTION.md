## A. Overview

### A1. What the First Letter of Peter is
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The First Letter of Peter is one of the General (or Catholic) Epistles of the New Testament (5 chapters, ~105 verses). It is a *letter*, not a narrative — the first letter to enter the Transparent Translation corpus — and it carries the standard marks of an ancient epistle: an opening salutation naming sender and recipients (1:1–2), a body of blessing and exhortation (1:3–5:11), and a closing with personal news and greetings (5:12–14). The author writes as "Kefa (Peter), an apostle of Yeshua *the* Messiah (Jesus)" (1:1) to believers scattered across Asia Minor.

The letter's mode is argument and exhortation rather than story. It does not report events; it addresses a situation — believers under social pressure and hostility — and presses a sustained appeal: stand firm in hope, become holy, and let your conduct answer the world that maligns you.

**Source:** Achtemeier, *1 Peter* (Hermeneia, 1996), 1–12; Elliott, *1 Peter* (Anchor Bible, 2000), 3–40.

### A2. Occasion and situation
**[STRONG INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**

The letter's recurring concern is suffering and social alienation. The readers face "various trials" (1:6), are "spoken against as evildoers" (cf. 2:12; 3:16), endure unjust treatment (2:18–20), and are warned of a "fiery ordeal" (4:12). The pressure described is consistently *social* — slander, ostracism, and reproach for the name — rather than the language of a formal, empire-wide legal prosecution (see §D). The letter casts this experience through the lens of exile: the readers are "sojourners" and "resident-aliens" (1:1, 17; 2:11), a people not at home in their own society.

**Source:** Elliott, *1 Peter* (AB), 97–103; Achtemeier, *1 Peter* (Hermeneia), 28–36.

### A3. Message and structure
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The letter develops a small set of interlocking themes:

**Living hope (1:3–12):** A single soaring Greek sentence of blessing — new birth into a living hope through the resurrection, an imperishable inheritance kept in the heavens, a tested faith more precious than gold, a salvation the prophets searched out.

**Holiness (1:13–2:10):** The call to be holy as the One who called them is holy (1:15–16, citing Leviticus); the ransom by the Messiah's precious blood; the readers built as "living stones" into a spiritual house, a "chosen people."

**Following the suffering Messiah (2:18–25; 3:13–4:6):** The Messiah's own unjust suffering as the pattern (2:21–25, drawing on Isaiah 53); doing good in the face of hostility.

**Submission and household order (2:11–3:7):** Ordered conduct toward governing authorities, masters, husbands, and wives — the household code (see §D).

**Standing firm (4:7–5:11):** Mutual love, sober watchfulness, the elders' charge, humility, resistance to the adversary, and the promise that the God of all grace will restore those who suffer "a little while."

**This edition covers 1 Peter 1–5** — the whole letter, the first fully complete book in the corpus.

**Source:** Achtemeier, *1 Peter* (Hermeneia), 73–82; Elliott, *1 Peter* (AB), 80–86; Michaels, *1 Peter* (Word Biblical Commentary, 1988), xxxiv–xliv.

## B. Authorship and Composition

### B1. What the text itself says
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The letter names its author in the first word: "Kefa (Peter), an apostle of Yeshua *the* Messiah (Jesus)" (1:1). It speaks in the first person as "a fellow elder and a witness of the Messiah's sufferings" (5:1). At the close it states the means of writing: "Through Silvanus (Silas), the faithful brother (as I regard *him*), I wrote to you briefly" (5:12), and it sends greetings from "she who is in Babylon, chosen together *with you*, and Markos (Mark) my son" (5:13). The internal data, then, are: Kefa as named author, Silvanus as the one "through" whom the letter was written, Markos and the "Babylon" community as co-senders of greetings.

### B2. The Petrine view and the Silvanus question
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE]**

On the traditional view, the apostle Kefa (Peter) is the author. The chief difficulty raised against direct authorship is the letter's polished Greek and its sustained use of the Septuagint — a literary register that some find unexpected for a Galilean fisherman (cf. the description in Acts 4:13). The clause "through Silvanus… I wrote" (5:12) is central to the discussion. On one reading it names Silvanus as the *amanuensis* (secretary) or composer who gave Kefa's message its Greek form, which would account for the style while preserving Petrine origin. On another, "through (διά) Silvanus" identifies him as the letter-*carrier* rather than the writer — a common idiom for the bearer of a letter — in which case it says nothing about composition. The phrase is genuinely ambiguous and is read both ways.

**Source:** Elliott, *1 Peter* (AB), 118–130, 872–874; Achtemeier, *1 Peter* (Hermeneia), 1–43, 349–351.

### B3. The case for later (pseudonymous) authorship
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE]**

Many scholars judge the letter to be written in Kefa's name by a later author or "Petrine circle" after his death. The arguments commonly offered: the cultivated Greek and rhetorical skill; the heavy dependence on the Septuagint rather than the Hebrew; apparent acquaintance with Pauline themes and language; the use of "Babylon" (5:13) as a cipher for Rome, a usage well attested only after 70 CE (see §D); and a social situation that some read as fitting a later generation. Defenders of Petrine authorship answer each point — an amanuensis explains the Greek, shared early-Christian tradition explains the overlaps, and the "Babylon" cipher is not decisively late. Scholarship is divided, and the TT takes no position; the question does not govern any rendering.

**Source:** Achtemeier, *1 Peter* (Hermeneia), 1–43; Elliott, *1 Peter* (AB), 118–130; Michaels, *1 Peter* (WBC), lv–lxvii.

### B4. The recipient community — "elect exiles of the dispersion"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The letter is addressed "to *the* elect sojourners of *the* dispersion of Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia" (1:1). Several features of this address are textual and undisputed:

- **The five provinces** are regions of **Roman Asia Minor** (modern Turkey). The sequence — Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, Bithynia — has often been read as tracing the likely *circuit of the letter-carrier*, beginning and ending in the north. (The same regions overlap the diaspora list of the Pentecost crowd in Acts 2:9.)
- **"Elect" (ἐκλεκτοῖς)** identifies the readers as God's chosen, picking up Israel's election language and applying it to them (developed at 2:9–10, "a chosen race… God's people").
- **"Dispersion" (διασπορά)** is the technical term for the scattering of Israel among the nations; the letter casts its readers in that exile identity.

**Source:** Elliott, *1 Peter* (AB), 84–94, 311–319; Achtemeier, *1 Peter* (Hermeneia), 81–86.

### B5. The recipients' social situation — the *paroikoi* identity
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Beyond the geography, the letter gives its readers a social identity. They are "sojourners and resident-aliens" (παρεπίδημοι / πάροικοι, 1:1, 17; 2:11) — terms that in the Greco-Roman world named people without full citizen status: foreigners, displaced persons, those living somewhere they did not belong by birth or right. Some scholars read this *literally*, as describing the readers' actual legal status as a displaced or marginal population; others read it *metaphorically*, as a theological self-description of believers estranged from their society because of their faith — or as both at once. On either reading, the letter addresses a **maligned minority under social pressure**: slandered, reproached, and treated as outsiders by the surrounding culture (2:12; 3:16; 4:4, 14). The *paroikos* identity — at home and not at home — is the letter's controlling self-image and the ground of its appeal. The TT records this as background; the exile language is the text's own (kept literal in the main text; see §F).

**Source:** Elliott, *A Home for the Homeless: A Sociological Exegesis of 1 Peter* (1981), esp. 21–58, 101–164; Elliott, *1 Peter* (AB), 94–103, 476–483; Achtemeier, *1 Peter* (Hermeneia), 55–57.

## C. Dating

### C1. The range of positions
**[HISTORICAL — POSSIBLE]**

The date of the letter is tied to the authorship question (§B) and is correspondingly disputed:

- **Petrine authorship** requires a date before the apostle's death, traditionally placed in the persecution under Nero (c. 64–65 CE). On this view the letter is written from Rome ("Babylon," 5:13) in the early-to-mid 60s.
- **Pseudonymous authorship** allows a later date, commonly placed in the 70s–90s CE, after Kefa's death, by an author or circle writing in his name. Some push toward the end of this range or just beyond.

The chief dating clues are internal and indecisive: the "Babylon" cipher (5:13), better attested as a name for Rome after the fall of Jerusalem in 70 CE; the character of the suffering described (social hostility rather than formal state prosecution — see §D); and the letter's relation to Pauline tradition. None of these fixes a date on its own. The TT takes no position; the date does not affect the translation.

**Source:** Achtemeier, *1 Peter* (Hermeneia), 43–50; Elliott, *1 Peter* (AB), 134–138; Michaels, *1 Peter* (WBC), lv–lxvii.

## D. Historical Setting

### D1. Asia Minor under Rome
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The five provinces named in 1:1 — Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia — were part of the Roman administration of Asia Minor in the first century: a patchwork of cities, rural districts, and overlapping provincial boundaries, with substantial Greek-speaking populations and established Jewish diaspora communities. The letter's readers live within this Roman provincial order; the letter's instruction to "be subject… to the emperor as supreme, or to governors" (2:13–14) reflects that setting. The TT records this as background and does not let it shape any rendering.

**Source:** Elliott, *1 Peter* (AB), 84–94; Achtemeier, *1 Peter* (Hermeneia), 50–55.

### D2. The nature of the "suffering" — debated
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE]**

The letter's "suffering" has been read in two broad ways, and the difference bears on dating (§C):

- **Social ostracism / local hostility:** On the now-dominant view, the suffering is informal and local — slander, reproach, exclusion, abuse for the name (2:12; 3:16; 4:4, 14). Believers are maligned by neighbors and former associates as a deviant minority, not prosecuted by the state.
- **Formal persecution:** An older view connected the "fiery ordeal" (4:12) and "suffering as a Christian" (4:16) to official Roman persecution — under Nero (60s) or, on some readings, the trials Pliny the Younger describes in Bithynia under Trajan (c. 112 CE). Most now consider the letter's language insufficient to establish formal, organized persecution.

The text describes the suffering vividly but does not specify its legal mechanism. The TT preserves what the text says and does not resolve the debate (Rule 3).

**Source:** Achtemeier, *1 Peter* (Hermeneia), 23–36; Elliott, *1 Peter* (AB), 97–103; Pliny, *Epistulae* 10.96 (the Bithynia correspondence with Trajan).

### D3. Epistolary conventions — the letter form
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

1 Peter follows the conventions of the Greco-Roman letter, adapted to a Jewish-Christian register:

- **Salutation (1:1–2):** sender → recipients → greeting, the standard opening. The Greco-Roman *chairein* ("greetings") is reshaped into the Christian "grace and peace be multiplied to you" — *charis* (grace) joined to *eirēnē* (peace, Hebrew *shalom*).
- **Body (1:3–5:11):** opening with a blessing (εὐλογητός, "Blessed *be*…," 1:3), the Jewish *berakah* form, then sustained exhortation.
- **Closing (5:12–14):** the note on writing "through Silvanus," personal greetings from "Babylon" and "Markos my son," and a final benediction of peace.

The letter is a *circular* — addressed to readers across five provinces, designed to be carried and read in many communities, not to a single congregation.

**Source:** Achtemeier, *1 Peter* (Hermeneia), 8–12, 348–352; Elliott, *1 Peter* (AB), 40–80.

### D4. The household code (Haustafel)
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

1 Peter 2:18–3:7 belongs to a recognized ancient form, the **household code** (German *Haustafel*) — a structured set of instructions to the members of a household: here, household slaves toward masters (2:18–25), wives toward husbands and husbands toward wives (3:1–7). Such codes have parallels in Greco-Roman moral writing (e.g. Aristotle's discussion of household management) and in other early Christian letters (Colossians, Ephesians). The form addresses the ordering of the ancient household within its social world. This is the letter's **highest-restraint passage**: the TT renders the Greek faithfully, gives the interpretive history in Tier 2, and takes no side in the main text (Rule 3; see the editorial log, P-009). The TT records the form as background and does not let it shape any rendering.

**Source:** Balch, *Let Wives Be Submissive: The Domestic Code in 1 Peter* (1981); Elliott, *1 Peter* (AB), 503–509, 549–599; Achtemeier, *1 Peter* (Hermeneia), 52–55, 187–217.

### D5. "Babylon" (5:13) as a cipher for Rome
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**

The greeting from "she who is in Babylon" (5:13) is widely read as a coded reference to **Rome**. By the late first century, "Babylon" had become a standard Jewish and Christian cipher for Rome — the great imperial power that, like ancient Babylon, had destroyed Jerusalem and its Temple (a usage prominent in Revelation, 4 Ezra, and 2 Baruch). On this reading the letter is written from Rome, and the figure of Babylon reinforces the readers' exile identity: they, like the letter's senders, live in "Babylon." A minority take "Babylon" literally (the city in Mesopotamia) or as the Roman fort in Egypt, but the cipher reading is dominant. The TT renders the place-name as the text gives it — Bavel (Babylon) — and notes the likely referent in Tier 2.

**Source:** Elliott, *1 Peter* (AB), 131–134, 882–887; Achtemeier, *1 Peter* (Hermeneia), 353–354.

## E. Manuscript Transmission

### E1. How the text reached us
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The Greek text of 1 Peter was transmitted through the New Testament manuscript tradition:

- Composition (1st century CE — date debated; see §C)
- Early papyri:
  - P72 (3rd–4th c.) — the earliest substantial witness, containing 1 Peter, 2 Peter, and Jude (part of the Bodmer collection)
- Major codices (4th–5th century CE):
  - Codex Sinaiticus (א, 4th c.)
  - Codex Vaticanus (B, 4th c.)
  - Codex Alexandrinus (A, 5th c.)
- Byzantine tradition (5th–15th c.)
- Printed editions: Erasmus (1516); Textus Receptus tradition; critical editions NA28 (Nestle-Aland 28th edition — the standard scholarly Greek text) / UBS5.

**Source:** Metzger, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* (2nd ed., 1994), 615–626; Elliott, *1 Peter* (AB), 138–141.

### E2. A comparatively stable text
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Unlike Acts — whose Alexandrian/Western divergence is the largest in the New Testament — **1 Peter's text is comparatively stable.** There is no large-scale parallel-edition problem; the manuscript tradition is relatively unified, and the variants are mostly small (word order, conjunctions, the presence or absence of short phrases). A few carry exegetical weight — for example, the wording at 3:18 (whether the Messiah "suffered" or "died" for sins) and the reading at 4:14 — but these are local, not a wholesale divergence. **The TT follows NA28.** Significant variants are noted in Tier 2 rather than silently adopted (Rule 22).

**Source:** Metzger, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* (2nd ed., 1994), 615–626; Achtemeier, *1 Peter* (Hermeneia), 1–7.

### F1. The corpus's first letter — genre-adapted method
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

1 Peter is the first *letter* in the Transparent Translation corpus. The verse-level GS (Greek-source) ruleset applies unchanged, but the genre differs from narrative: this is sustained argument and exhortation. The reader will notice long, suspended Greek sentences — most strikingly the single periodic blessing of 1:3–12 — which the TT keeps in their subordinated, suspended structure rather than breaking into short modern sentences (Prime Directive: do not simplify what the source keeps complex). The letter's frame is preserved as the text gives it: salutation (1:1–2), body, and closing greetings (5:12–14).

### F3. Text-highlight markers in 1 Peter
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The TT marks the text with a small set of conventions the reader will encounter throughout 1 Peter 1–5:

- `*added*` — words added for English grammar (Rule 11), shown in italics. (Frequent here: the copula in "I *am* holy," 1:16; "Blessed *be*," 1:3.)
- `{t:…}` — a transliterated term carried with its gloss, where the text itself supplies one.
- `{a:…}` — a live double sense kept open rather than resolved, e.g. {a:wind/spirit} for πνεῦμα, which stays open throughout (1:2, 11, 12; 3:18, 19; 4:6, 14).
- `@@…@@` — divine speech (Rule 30). 1 Peter is the apostle's exhortation **in his own voice** — there is no narrated divine speech. Only God's first-person word *inside* an OT citation is marked: **1:16** ("@@be holy, because I *am* holy@@," Lev 11:44/19:2) and **2:6** ("behold, I lay in Zion a stone…," Isa 28:16). Citations that speak *about* the Lord in the third person — the psalmist's and prophet's human voice (1:24–25, Isa 40; 2:3, Ps 34; 3:10–12, Ps 34) — are **not** marked.

### F4. The exile keyword — *paroikoi / parepidēmoi / diaspora*
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The letter's identity language is its controlling image, and the TT keeps it literal rather than smoothing it into "Christians" or "pilgrims":

- **παρεπίδημοι** (*parepidēmoi*) = "sojourners / exiles" (1:1; 2:11)
- **πάροικοι / παροικία** (*paroikoi / paroikia*) = "resident-aliens" / "time of sojourning" (1:17; 2:11)
- **διασπορά** (*diaspora*) = "dispersion" (1:1)

These are rendered with their literal exile/foreigner force, with the dual sense (literal status / theological self-description; see §B5) noted in Tier 2. The readers' whole self-understanding — at home and not at home — turns on these words.

### F5. Kyrios — Divine Name Policy in 1 Peter (Option C)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

When 1 Peter quotes or echoes Hebrew Bible passages containing the Tetragrammaton (the four-letter Hebrew divine name יהוה, transliterated YHWH), the Greek text uses κύριος (*kyrios*). Per the TT's Divine Name Policy (Option C, RULES-GS.md):

- The main text renders *kyrios* as "the Lord" in OT-quotation and OT-echo contexts.
- A Tier 2 note identifies the source and states that the underlying Hebrew has the Tetragrammaton.
- When *kyrios* refers to Yeshua (Jesus) or to a human master (not an OT YHWH reference), no YHWH note is needed.
- Ambiguous cases are flagged per Rule 13.

The OT citations carry **YHWH** behind the Greek *kyrios*:

- **1:25** (Isa 40:8) — "the word of *the Lord* (YHWH) endures forever."
- **2:3** (Ps 34:8) — "*the Lord* (YHWH) is good / kind."
- **3:12** (Ps 34:15–16) — "the eyes of *the Lord* (YHWH)… the face of *the Lord*."

A distinct, deliberate case is **3:15**: "sanctify *the Messiah* as Lord," which reapplies Isaiah 8:13 ("the Lord of hosts") to the Messiah — a κύριος *referent-shift* (from YHWH in Isaiah to the Messiah here). The TT preserves what the Greek does, flags the move (Rule 13), and explains it in Tier 2.
