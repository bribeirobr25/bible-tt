**What:** The Gospel of the Savior for all peoples — an "orderly account" with the fullest infancy narrative and the canticles (Magnificat, Benedictus, Gloria, Nunc Dimittis).
**When:** Events around 4 BCE–30 CE; widely dated to around 80–90 CE; the companion volume to Acts.
**Who:** Traditionally Luke the physician, Paul's travel companion; authorship is debated.
**To whom:** Addressed to Theophilus; written for a Greek-speaking, likely Gentile-inclusive audience.

## A. Overview

### A1. What the Gospel of Luke is
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The Gospel of Luke is the third book of the New Testament canon and the longest of the four Gospels (24 chapters, approximately 1,150 verses). With the Acts of the Apostles it forms a two-volume work — Luke–Acts — by a single author; the two share a prologue style, a dedication to Theophilus, and continuous narrative concerns. Together they make up roughly a quarter of the New Testament, more than the corpus attributed to any single other author.

Luke is distinguished by a formal literary prologue (1:1–4) in polished Greek, by the most extensive infancy narrative of any Gospel (chapters 1–2), and by four canticles — the Magnificat (1:46–55), the Benedictus (1:68–79), the Gloria (2:14), and the Nunc Dimittis (2:29–32). It carries recurring emphases on the poor and the outsider, on women, on prayer, on the Holy Spirit, and on table fellowship. Material unique to Luke includes the parables of the Good Samaritan (10:25–37) and the Prodigal Son (15:11–32).

**Source:** Fitzmyer, *The Gospel According to Luke* (Anchor Bible, 1981–1985), I.3–34; Bovon, *Luke* (Hermeneia, 2002–2013), I.1–9.

### A2. Place among the Synoptic Gospels
**[HISTORICAL — PROBABLE]**

Luke shares substantial material with Matthew and Mark — the three together are the Synoptic Gospels (from Greek *syn* + *opsis*, "seen together"), because they follow a similar structure and often share wording. On the dominant scholarly model, Luke draws on Mark and on a body of sayings tradition (often labeled "Q") also used by Matthew, alongside material found only in Luke (sometimes labeled "L"). The Gospel's own prologue acknowledges that "many" had already undertaken to compile accounts (1:1), which the TT renders as the text states without endorsing any particular source theory. The TT renders Luke on its own terms and does not harmonize its wording to the parallels.

**Source:** Fitzmyer, *Luke* (AB), I.63–106; Bovon, *Luke* (Hermeneia), I.1–9.

### A3. Narrative framework
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The narrative of Luke moves through several major phases:

**Prologue and infancy (1:1–2:52):** The formal dedication to Theophilus (1:1–4); the intertwined annunciations and births of Yochanan (John) and Yeshua (Jesus); the canticles; the presentation in the Temple and the boy Yeshua among the teachers.

**Preparation (3:1–4:13):** Yochanan (John) the Immerser's ministry dated by Roman and Judean rulers (3:1–2), Yeshua (Jesus)'s immersion (3:21–22), the genealogy traced back to Adam and to God (3:23–38), and the testing in the wilderness (4:1–13).

**Galilean ministry (4:14–9:50):** Teaching, healing, and the calling of disciples, opening with the programmatic reading at Natzeret (Nazareth) (4:16–30).

**Journey to Yerushalayim (Jerusalem) (9:51–19:27):** A long travel section, distinctive to Luke, containing much of its unique parable material.

**Yerushalayim, passion, and resurrection (19:28–24:53):** Entry into the city, Temple teaching, the last supper, arrest, trial, crucifixion, the empty tomb, and resurrection appearances ending with the ascension.

**Source:** Fitzmyer, *Luke* (AB), I.134–142; Bovon, *Luke* (Hermeneia), I.2–9.

## B. Authorship and Composition

### B1. What the text itself says
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The Gospel of Luke does not name its author. The text is anonymous — the title "According to Luke" (ΚΑΤΑ ΛΟΥΚΑΝ) is a later addition found in manuscript headings, not part of the original composition. What the text does state is a method: the author addresses "most excellent Theophilus" (1:3), reports that "many" had already compiled accounts (1:1), claims to have "followed all things closely" (1:3), and states the aim of writing an "orderly account" (*kathexēs*) "so that you may know the certainty (*asphaleia*) of the things you have been taught" (1:4). The author writes as a careful compiler, not as an eyewitness to the events narrated.

### B2. Traditional attribution
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

Early tradition identifies the author as **Luke**, a physician and companion of Paul. The identification rests on second-century witnesses — the Muratorian Fragment, Irenaeus (*Adv. Haer.* 3.1.1; 3.14.1), and the anti-Marcionite prologue — combined with the New Testament references to a "Luke the beloved physician" (Col 4:14; cf. Phlm 24; 2 Tim 4:11) and with the "we-passages" in Acts (e.g. Acts 16:10–17), read as marking stretches where the author traveled with Paul. The Gospel itself makes no such claim; the attribution is traditional, not internal.

**Source:** Fitzmyer, *Luke* (AB), I.35–53; Bovon, *Luke* (Hermeneia), I.8–9.

### B3. Modern assessment
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**

Scholarly opinion is divided. The traditional attribution to Luke the physician retains defenders, partly because there was little motive to attribute an anonymous Gospel to a relatively minor, non-apostolic figure. Many scholars, however, are cautious: the "we-passages" admit other explanations (a source, a literary convention), the theology of Luke–Acts diverges at points from Paul's letters, and the polished Greek and use of Mark point to a second- or third-generation author working from sources. The most that can be said with confidence from the text is that the author was a skilled Greek writer, familiar with the Septuagint, addressing a Greek-speaking audience and drawing on earlier accounts. The TT records the attribution as background and does not let it govern any rendering.

**Source:** Fitzmyer, *Luke* (AB), I.35–53; Bovon, *Luke* (Hermeneia), I.8–9; Marshall, *The Gospel of Luke* (NIGTC, 1978), 33–35.

## C. Dating

### C1. Composition dating
**[HISTORICAL — PROBABLE]**

Luke is commonly dated to approximately 80–90 CE, on several converging grounds:

- **Dependence on Mark:** On the dominant model Luke uses Mark, generally dated c. 65–70 CE, which requires a later date for Luke.
- **Post-70 indicators:** The form of the prediction concerning Jerusalem (e.g. 19:43–44; 21:20, "Jerusalem surrounded by armies") is often read as reflecting knowledge of the city's fall in 70 CE, though this is debated.
- **The "many" of the prologue:** 1:1 presupposes earlier written accounts already in circulation, implying a degree of remove from the founding events.

A minority argue for an earlier date (the 60s), partly on the ground that Acts ends with Paul still alive in Rome and does not narrate his death or the fall of Jerusalem; others place Luke–Acts well into the early second century. The TT takes no position; the date does not affect the translation.

**Source:** Fitzmyer, *Luke* (AB), I.53–57; Bovon, *Luke* (Hermeneia), I.8–9.

## D. Historical Setting

### D1. A Greek-speaking, Gentile-inclusive audience
**[STRONG INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**

Several features point to an audience that included, or was oriented toward, non-Jewish readers:

- **Greek literary register:** The prologue (1:1–4) is a single periodic sentence in the manner of Hellenistic historiography, addressed to a named patron, "most excellent Theophilus."
- **Universal horizon:** The genealogy runs back not merely to Avraham (Abraham) (as in Matthew) but to Adam and to God (3:38), and Simeon's canticle names Yeshua (Jesus) "a light for revelation to the nations" (2:32) — framing the story for all peoples.
- **Roman dating frame:** Events are anchored by reference to Roman and provincial rulers (2:1–2; 3:1–2), situating the narrative within the wider empire rather than within Israel alone.

At the same time, the infancy narrative is steeped in the Septuagint and in Temple piety, and the canticles echo Hebrew Scripture throughout — so the audience is Gentile-inclusive, not Gentile-only. The TT records this as background and does not let it shape any rendering.

### D2. Who was Theophilus?
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE]**

The dedication names "Theophilus" (Θεόφιλος, "friend/beloved of God") and calls him "most excellent" (*kratistos*, 1:3) — an honorific elsewhere used for officials (cf. Acts 23:26; 24:3; 26:25). Proposals include a real patron or recent convert of some social standing, a Roman official, or — taking the name's meaning — a symbolic address to any "lover of God." The text does not resolve the question. The TT renders the name and title as they stand and records the options here as background only.

### D3. Distinctive Lukan emphases
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Across the Gospel, recurring concerns mark Luke's presentation: the poor and the socially marginal (e.g. the Magnificat's reversal, 1:52–53; the programmatic reading at Nazareth, 4:18); women, who appear with unusual frequency and prominence (e.g. Elisheva (Elizabeth), Miryam (Mary), Hannah/Anna, 2:36–38); prayer and the Holy Spirit; joy and praise; and table fellowship. These are features of the text itself; the TT renders them as they stand and does not amplify or explain the pattern in the main text.

## E. Manuscript Transmission

### E1. How the text reached us
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The Greek text of Luke was transmitted through the same manuscript tradition as the other Gospels:

- Composition (c. 80–90 CE)
- Early copies (2nd–3rd century CE):
  - P4 (late 2nd / 3rd c.) — portions of Luke 1–6
  - P75 (Bodmer XIV–XV, c. 175–225 CE) — large portions of Luke and John; an exceptionally important early witness
  - P45 (3rd c.) — portions of all four Gospels + Acts
- Major codices (4th–5th century CE):
  - Codex Sinaiticus (א, 4th c.)
  - Codex Vaticanus (B, 4th c.)
  - Codex Alexandrinus (A, 5th c.)
  - Codex Bezae (D, 5th c.) — important witness to the "Western" text-type
- Byzantine tradition (5th–15th c.)
- Printed editions: Erasmus (1516); Textus Receptus tradition; critical editions NA28 (Nestle-Aland 28th edition — the standard scholarly Greek text) / UBS5.

### E2. Notable variants within chapters 1–3
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The opening chapters contain several variants that meet the RULES-GS significance threshold and are handled in the chapter notes:

- **1:46** — "**Mary** said" (the Magnificat) is read by a few Old Latin witnesses as "**Elizabeth** said." The overwhelming manuscript evidence reads "Mary"; the minority reading is noted, not adopted.
- **2:14** — "peace among people **of good will / with whom he is pleased**" (*eudokias*, genitive: א*, A, B*, D, W — the earlier reading) vs. "peace, good will **toward** people" (*eudokia*, nominative: Byzantine). The genitive is the critical text; the difference turns on a single final letter.
- **3:22** — at the immersion the divine voice reads "You are my beloved son; with you I am well pleased" (NA28). Codex Bezae and several Fathers read instead "You are my son; today I have begotten you" (quoting Psalm 2:7) — the *lectio difficilior*, noted in the chapter apparatus.

### E3. Comparative transmission interval
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The interval between the composition of Luke (c. 80–90 CE) and the earliest substantial surviving witnesses (P4 for Luke 1–6, late 2nd / 3rd c.; P75 for large portions, c. 175–225 CE) is, by the standards of ancient historiography, relatively short. For comparison: Arrian's *Anabasis of Alexander*, the most comprehensive surviving biography of Alexander the Great (d. 323 BCE), was composed c. 130–135 CE — a gap of roughly 450 years between subject and biography. Tacitus's *Annales*, covering events from 14 CE onward, was composed c. 116 CE — a gap of roughly 80–100 years between events and narrative. The NT's transmission interval sits at the shorter end of the range typical for ancient narrative literature about historical figures. **This does not bear on the historical accuracy of the Gospel's contents; it bears only on the textual-transmission gap between composition and first surviving witness.** The same data is sometimes deployed apologetically — that is not the TT's frame: the comparison is descriptive, not evidential.

**Source:** Metzger, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* (2nd ed., 1994), 109–135; Arrian, *Anabasis of Alexander*, Preface; cf. Bosworth, *A Historical Commentary on Arrian's History of Alexander*, OUP, vols. I (1980) + II (1995); Tacitus, *Annales*; cf. Goodyear, *The Annals of Tacitus*, Cambridge, vols. I (1972) + II (1981).

## F. Reading Luke in the TT

### F1. The prologue and "orderly account"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Luke 1:1–4 is a single, carefully constructed Greek sentence — the only formal historiographic prologue among the Gospels. Two of its terms shape how the book presents itself: *kathexēs* ("in order," "consecutively," 1:3) and *asphaleia* ("certainty," "security," "firm ground," 1:4). The TT renders these as the text states — "orderly account" and "certainty" — without deciding whether *kathexēs* means strict chronological order or coherent literary arrangement; the ambiguity is preserved and noted (Rule 2).

### F3. The canticles
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Luke 1–2 contains four songs that have become known by their Latin opening words: the **Magnificat** (Miryam (Mary), 1:46–55), the **Benedictus** (Zekharyah (Zechariah), 1:68–79), the **Gloria** (the angelic host, 2:14), and the **Nunc Dimittis** (Shimon (Simeon), 2:29–32). All four are saturated with Septuagint language (the Magnificat especially echoes Hannah's song, 1 Samuel 2:1–10). These are **human** speech — songs of Mary, Zechariah, the angelic host, and Simeon — and so are **not** marked as divine speech under Rule 30. By the established TT convention (editorial log L-002), they are rendered as flowing prose rather than lineated poetry; visual lineation is deferred with the broader Hebrew-poetry question.

### F4. Direct divine speech (Rule 30)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The TT marks the direct speech of God and of Yeshua (Jesus) with a distinct colour. Within Luke 1–3 this covers the divine voice at the immersion (3:22 — "You are my beloved son; with you I am well pleased") and the sayings of Yeshua (e.g. 2:49, "Did you not know that I must be about my Father's…?"). The speech of **Gavriel (Gabriel)** — an angelic messenger (1:13–20; 1:28–37) — is **not** marked, consistent with the Matthew/Mark precedent for angelic speakers (editorial log L-001). The canticles, the speech of Elisheva (Elizabeth), Zekharyah (Zechariah), Shimon (Simeon), and the crowd are likewise not divine-marked.

### F5. Kyrios — Divine Name Policy in Luke
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

When Luke quotes or echoes Hebrew Bible passages containing the Tetragrammaton (the four-letter Hebrew divine name יהוה, transliterated YHWH), the Greek text uses κύριος (*kyrios*). Per the TT's Divine Name Policy (Option C, RULES-GS.md):

- The main text renders *kyrios* as "the Lord" in OT quotation and OT-echo contexts.
- A note identifies the source and states that the underlying Hebrew has the Tetragrammaton.
- When *kyrios* refers to Yeshua (Jesus) or to a human master (not an OT YHWH reference), no YHWH note is needed.
- Ambiguous cases — common in the infancy narrative, where "the Lord" can hover between YHWH and the child — are flagged per Rule 13.

This is especially relevant in the canticles and infancy narrative, which are dense with Septuagint *kyrios* language (e.g. 1:32, 38, 46; 2:9, 11, 23, 26; 3:4 quoting Isaiah 40:3).
