## Note on Luke's mode of allusion

Luke 1 contains **no fulfillment-formula citations** of the Matthean type (ἵνα πληρωθῇ, "so that it might be fulfilled"). Its prophetic density is instead **allusive and canticle-borne**: the two songs (Magnificat, Benedictus) and Gavriel's (Gabriel's) two annunciations are woven from HB diction — Hannah's song, the Davidic covenant, the Abrahamic oath, Malachi's Elijah, Daniel's Gabriel. The TT therefore labels most entries **CLAIMED** (the connection is asserted by the text's own echo and named hope) or **PARTIAL** (the hope is broader than the single figure), and reserves explicit-citation language only where the source is quoted. Where the referent of "the Lord" fuses YHWH and Yeshua, the entry preserves the fusion rather than resolving it (Rule 13).

## 1 Samuel 2:1–10 — Hannah's Song behind the Magnificat (Luke 1:46–55)

**Verse:** Luke 1:46–55
**Text says:** "My soul magnifies the Lord… he looked upon the lowliness of his *female* servant… He pulled down rulers from thrones and lifted up *the* lowly; *the* hungry he filled with good *things*, and *the* rich he sent away empty."
**Context:** After Elisheva's (Elizabeth's) blessing, Miryam (Mary) sings the Magnificat (vv.46–55) — human speech, not divine-marked (Rule 30), rendered as prose. The song is the densest single web of HB allusion in the chapter; its base text is the song of Channah (Hannah) at 1 Sam 2:1–10, the mother who received a son against the odds and dedicated him to YHWH.
**Subject:** The divine reversal celebrated by a lowly woman given a child. Hannah's song (1 Sam 2:1–10) celebrates YHWH who breaks the bows of the mighty and exalts the poor from the dust; Mary's reworks the same reversal. The opening "my soul magnifies / my spirit exulted" also echoes Ps 34:4 / Ps 35:9; "he looked upon the lowliness of his female servant" (v.48) is nearly verbatim from Channah's vow (1 Sam 1:11 LXX, "if you look upon the lowliness of your female servant").
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion / typological reuse**, not a fulfillment-formula citation — there is no "as it is written" and no quotation marks in Luke; the Magnificat re-voices Hannah's song and the reversal psalms (Ps 113:7–8; Ps 107:9) in Mary's mouth. The connection is dense and deliberate (the 1 Sam 1:11 line is close to verbatim) but the text *enacts* the pattern rather than *claiming to fulfill a prediction* — Hannah's song was praise, not prophecy. The CLAIMED label reflects that Luke claims the Hannah-pattern explicitly by saturating Mary's words with it; the series of aorists in vv.51–53 may be gnomic (timeless reversal) or prophetic-past (reversal as accomplished in the child), and the TT does not resolve which. **[comparative parallel — PROBABLE]** → See `content/en/luke/CHAPTER-1.md` Cross-Chapter Tracking (1 Sam 1–2 → Luke 1) for the element-by-element table.
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: 1 Samuel 2:1–10 is read as Hannah's thanksgiving for Samuel's birth and a celebration of YHWH's reversal of the proud and the lowly; it is not read as a messianic prediction. Mary's reuse is recognized as a Second Temple Jewish compositional practice (re-voicing scripture in prayer) rather than a claim that Hannah foretold Mary. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The Magnificat has been read since antiquity as the new Hannah's song — the faithful daughter of Israel announcing the eschatological reversal accomplished in her son. The Hannah-base is treated as evidence of God's consistent pattern of lifting the lowly across both Testaments. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: The Quran honors Maryam highly (Surah 19; 3:42–47) and affirms her favored status, but does not engage 1 Samuel or the Magnificat's Hannah-typology. [DOCUMENTED]

## Genesis 18:14 — "nothing impossible with God" (Luke 1:37)

**Verse:** Luke 1:37
**Text says:** "because nothing will be impossible with God."
**Context:** Closing Gavriel's (Gabriel's) annunciation to Miryam, after the sign of Elisheva's (Elizabeth's) own conception in old age. The line is the reassurance that answers Mary's "how will this be?" (v.34).
**Subject:** The God for whom no word/thing is impossible. The Greek (οὐκ ἀδυνατήσει παρὰ τοῦ θεοῦ πᾶν ῥῆμα) closely echoes the LXX of Gen 18:14, the word spoken to Sarah when she laughed at the promise of Yitschaq (Isaac): "Is anything impossible (*adynatēsei*) with God?" *Rhēma* carries both "word" and "thing/matter" (Semitic *davar*).
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED
**Fulfillment notes:** This is a **near-verbatim LXX echo**, not a formula citation: Luke does not say "as it is written," but the wording (*adynatēsei para tou theou*) reproduces Gen 18:14 LXX closely enough to be a deliberate quotation-by-allusion. The function is typological binding — the new aged-barren and virgin conceptions are placed in the line of Sarah's against-the-odds birth of Isaac. The CLAIMED label reflects that Luke deliberately draws the Sarah line; it is an allusion to a past divine word (to Sarah), not the fulfillment of a standing prediction. **[comparative parallel — PROBABLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Gen 18 → Luke 1:37).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Genesis 18:14 is read in its narrative setting — YHWH's word to Sarah and Avraham (Abraham) that the aged couple will have a son. The verse is a statement of divine power, not a prophecy. Luke's reuse is read as scriptural allusion, not as a claim that Gen 18 predicted Mary. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The echo is read as binding the conception of Yeshua to the founding miracle of Israel's election (Isaac), framing both as works of the same God "for whom nothing is impossible." [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: The Quran narrates the annunciation of a son to the aged Ibrahim and Sarah (Surah 11:69–73; 51:24–30) and, separately, the virginal conception of Isa by Maryam (19:19–21), affirming God's power over both — without deriving one from the other or engaging Luke's allusion. [DOCUMENTED]

## Malachi 3:1, 3:23–24 (Eng. 4:5–6) + Isaiah 40:3 — John as the returning Elijah (Luke 1:17, 1:76)

**Verse:** Luke 1:17, 1:76
**Text says:** "And he will go before him in *the* spirit and power of Eliyahu (Elijah), to turn back *the* hearts of fathers to children and *the* disobedient to *the* mindset of *the* righteous, to prepare for the Lord a people made ready" (1:17); "And you, child, will be called *a* prophet of *the* Most High, for you will go before *the* Lord to prepare his ways" (1:76).
**Context:** Gavriel's announcement of Yochanan (John) (1:17) and the closing turn of Zecharyah's (Zechariah's) Benedictus addressed to the infant John (1:76). Both cast John as the forerunner.
**Subject:** The forerunner who prepares the way. 1:17 quotes the substance of Malachi 3:24 (Eng. 4:6 LXX) — "to turn the hearts of fathers to children" — the task of the returning Eliyahu (Elijah) sent before "the great and terrible day of YHWH" (Mal 3:23). 1:76 fuses Malachi 3:1 ("I send my messenger to prepare the way before me") with Isaiah 40:3 ("prepare the way of YHWH"). In the source texts "the Lord / before me" is YHWH; the Gospel applies the preparation to Yeshua.
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED
**Fulfillment notes:** 1:17 is the strongest claim in the chapter: it reproduces Malachi's "turn the hearts of fathers to children" closely and names Eliyahu, so the Elijah-redivivus identification is the text's own explicit assertion. The referent of "before him / before the Lord" is genuinely fused — the antecedent in 1:16–17 is "the Lord their God" (YHWH), while the Gospel directs the preparation toward Yeshua; the TT preserves the fusion rather than resolving it. The CLAIMED label reflects that Luke claims the Elijah-forerunner role explicitly; the YHWH/Yeshua referent-fusion is flagged **Possible** (Rule 13), since the text does not collapse the two. **[textual — PROBABLE; referent-fusion — POSSIBLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Mal 3:1, 3:23–24 / Isa 40:3 → Luke 1:17, 76); the Isa 40:3 quotation proper is treated in CHAPTER-3-PROPHECY.md.
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Malachi 3:23–24 is read as the promise that Eliyahu will return before the Day of YHWH to reconcile the generations; this remains a live expectation (e.g. Eliyahu's cup and chair at Pesach and circumcision). The identification of that returning Elijah with John the Baptist is not accepted; mainstream Jewish interpretation still awaits Elijah. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The Gospels read John as the prophesied Elijah-forerunner (cf. Matt 11:14; 17:12–13), preparing the way not for YHWH's coming in judgment but for Yeshua. Luke 1:17, 76 is treated as the inaugural statement of that role. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Yahya (John) is honored in the Quran as a prophet confirming "a word from God" (Surah 3:39; 19:7, 12–15), but the Elijah-forerunner framework of Malachi is not part of the Quranic presentation. [DOCUMENTED]

## 2 Samuel 7:12–16 + Isaiah 9:6 / Daniel 7:14 — the Davidic throne and unending kingdom (Luke 1:32–33)

**Verse:** Luke 1:32–33
**Text says:** "This *one* will be great and will be called Son of *the* Most High, and the Lord God will give to him the throne of David his father, and he will reign over the house of Ya'aqov (Jacob) into the ages, and of his kingdom there will be no end."
**Context:** The heart of Gavriel's annunciation to Miryam — the description of who the child will be. The language is royal-Davidic.
**Subject:** The Davidic heir whose throne is everlasting. "The throne of David his father… reign… no end" draws on the Davidic covenant of 2 Sam 7:12–16 ("I will establish his throne forever") and Ps 89; "Son of the Most High" sounds the divine-sonship note of 2 Sam 7:14 / Ps 2:7; "into the ages… no end" echoes the everlasting dominion of Dan 7:14 and the unending peace of Isa 9:6 (Eng. 9:7). The Davidic descent attaches through Yosef (Joseph), "of the house of David" (1:27).
**Fulfillment status:** PARTIAL
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion to the Davidic-covenant promise**, not a formula citation — Luke clusters covenant language (throne / father David / house / no end) without quoting a single verse. The Davidic-king-with-everlasting-throne expectation is broader than any one claimant: 2 Sam 7 in its setting concerns David's dynastic line generally, and several figures could in principle answer "a Davidic ruler." The PARTIAL label reflects that Luke's *application* to Yeshua is CLAIMED (reinforced by the Davidic descent at 1:27 and the genealogy at 3:23–38), while the underlying promise is corporate and dynastic. The "no end" / "into the ages" eschatological intensification (Dan 7 / Isa 9) is the text's claim that this heir's reign is the final one. **[textual — PROBABLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (2 Sam 7 / Ps 18, 132 → Luke 1:32–33, 69).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: 2 Samuel 7 is read as the foundational dynastic promise to the house of David, and Isa 9 / Dan 7 as hopes of a restored Davidic kingdom and an everlasting dominion; these nourish messianic expectation of a Davidic king. The identification of that king with Yeshua is not accepted, and the everlasting-kingdom hope remains unrealized in mainstream Jewish reading. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The annunciation is read as the announcement that the Davidic covenant's everlasting throne is fulfilled in Yeshua, son of David and Son of the Most High. The "no end" clause is read of the eternal kingdom. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: The Quran affirms Isa as a prophet and a "sign," and Dawud (David) as a prophet-king given the Zabur (Psalms), but does not frame Isa through the Davidic-throne covenant of 2 Samuel 7. [DOCUMENTED]

## Daniel 8:16, 9:21 — Gabriel the interpreting messenger (Luke 1:19, 1:26)

**Verse:** Luke 1:19, 1:26
**Text says:** "I am Gavriel (Gabriel), the *one* standing before God, and I was sent to speak to you and to bring you this good news" (1:19); "the messenger Gabriel was sent from God to a town of the Galilee named Natseret (Nazareth)" (1:26).
**Context:** Gabriel's self-identification to Zecharyah (1:19) and his commissioning to Miryam (1:26). He is *angelos* — his speech is not divine-marked (Rule 30).
**Subject:** The named interpreting angel. Gavriel ("God is my strength") is the revealing/interpreting messenger of Daniel 8:16 ("Gabriel, make this man understand the vision") and 9:21–23, who comes to Daniel "at the time of the evening offering" with insight about the appointed end. His appearance to Zechariah at the hour of incense (1:8–11) recalls that setting; his presence places the annunciations in the apocalyptic-revelatory tradition.
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion by named character**, not a prophecy citation — Luke does not cite Daniel, but the choice of Gabriel (one of only two named angels in the HB) deliberately evokes Daniel's revelations of the appointed time. The incense-hour setting strengthens the Daniel 9 resonance. The CLAIMED label reflects that the allusion is intentional and recognizable; no predictive "fulfillment" is in view — rather, Luke signals that these births belong to the unfolding of God's appointed plan announced through the same messenger. **[comparative parallel — POSSIBLE]**
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Gabriel in Daniel 8–9 is the interpreting angel of the visions of the end; in later Jewish tradition he is one of the chief angels. Luke's use is recognized as drawing on this angelology; it carries no acceptance of the Lukan annunciations' specific claims. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: Gabriel's appearance is read as continuity between Daniel's revelation of the appointed time and the announcement of its arrival in the births of John and Yeshua. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Jibril (Gabriel) is the foremost angel of revelation in Islam, the bearer of the Quran to Muhammad and the announcer to Maryam of Isa's birth (Surah 19:17–19; 66:12, "the spirit"). The Islamic tradition strongly affirms Gabriel as annunciator, though it engages the Quranic, not the Lukan, account. [DOCUMENTED]

## Genesis 17:7, 22:16–18 — the oath to Abraham (Luke 1:55, 1:72–73)

**Verse:** Luke 1:55, 1:72–73
**Text says:** "just as he spoke to our fathers, to Avraham (Abraham) and to his seed, into the age" (1:55); "to remember his holy covenant, *the* oath that he swore to Avraham (Abraham) our father" (1:72–73).
**Context:** The closing line of the Magnificat (1:55) and the covenant core of the Benedictus (1:72–73). Both canticles ground the salvation they celebrate in the Abrahamic promise, not only the Davidic.
**Subject:** The sworn covenant with Abraham and his seed. "To Abraham and to his seed" (1:55) draws on the everlasting covenant of Gen 17:7 ("to you and to your seed after you"); "the oath that he swore to Abraham" (1:73) points to the sworn promise of Gen 22:16–18 ("by myself I have sworn… I will surely bless you… in your seed all the nations will be blessed"), spoken after the binding of Yitschaq (Isaac).
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED
**Fulfillment notes:** This is **allusion to the Abrahamic oath**, not a formula citation — the canticles invoke the covenant ("seed," "oath," "swore," "remember his covenant") in HB diction without quoting a verse. The connection is explicit in intent (the songs name Abraham and his seed and the sworn oath) but the mode is doxological recollection, not prediction-and-fulfillment. The CLAIMED label reflects that Luke deliberately roots the births in the Abrahamic promise; the "all nations blessed in your seed" horizon of Gen 22:18 quietly anticipates Luke's reach to the nations (cf. 2:32; 3:6, 38). **[textual — PROBABLE]** → See Cross-Chapter Tracking (Gen 22:16–18 / Gen 17:7 → Luke 1:55, 72–73).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Genesis 17:7 and 22:16–18 are foundational covenant texts — the everlasting covenant with Abraham and his seed and the oath after the Akedah. They ground Israel's election and the blessing of the nations through Abraham's line. Their reuse in the canticles is scriptural allusion; it does not entail the canticles' specific christological claims. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The Benedictus and Magnificat are read as declaring that the Abrahamic oath is being kept in the births of John and Yeshua — salvation as covenant faithfulness. The "seed" and "all nations blessed" are read toward the inclusion of the nations. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: Ibrahim (Abraham) is central in Islam as the model monotheist and recipient of covenant and promise (Surah 2:124–129); the lineage of prophets descends through him. The specific oath-of-Genesis-22 framing of the Lukan canticles is not part of the Quranic narrative. [DOCUMENTED]
