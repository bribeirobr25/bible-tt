**What kind of claim?** (tells you the source of the information)
- **TEXTUAL** — derived directly from the Greek text
- **STRONG INFERENCE** — logically entailed by the text, not stated explicitly
- **POSSIBLE INFERENCE** — one legitimate reading among others
- **COMPARATIVE PARALLEL** — attested in another ancient source
- **LATER RECEPTION** — post-biblical reading
- **HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL** — based on material or historical evidence
- **SPECULATION** — no strong textual or evidential basis

**How certain?** (tells you the confidence level)
- **VERIFIED** — strong evidence, wide agreement
- **PROBABLE** — well-supported but debated
- **POSSIBLE** — one reasonable reading among others
- **UNCERTAIN** — genuinely unclear
- **SPECULATIVE** — must be rare and clearly flagged
- **DOCUMENTED** — historically attested reception (for LATER RECEPTION entries)

**Reading principle:** Start with TEXTUAL entries — those come from the Greek itself. Then explore outward to parallels, reception, and comparison. The companion presents; you assess.

## A. Source Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. The demons' confession — why it is not marked as divine speech
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

At 3:11 the unclean spirits cry **σὺ εἶ ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ θεοῦ** (*sy ei ho huios tou theou*), "You are the Son of God." Although the words are true, the TT does **not** mark them as divine speech (Rule 30): the speaker is a non-divine being (an unclean spirit), exactly as at 1:24. Rule 30 marks *who* speaks, not whether the content is correct. Yeshua's immediate silencing (3:12) belongs to the messianic-secret pattern — the demons' supernatural recognition is suppressed.

### A2. "He made twelve" and Mark's self-glossing — *Boanerges*
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

"He made twelve" (3:14) uses **ἐποίησεν** (*epoiēsen*, "made/appointed"); the number evokes the twelve tribes of Israel (a reconstitution motif, implied not stated). At 3:17 Mark transliterates a Semitic nickname and **glosses it himself**: "Boanerges, which is 'sons of thunder'" — a recurring Markan habit (cf. *talitha koum* 5:41; *ephphatha* 7:34; *Eloi Eloi* 15:34). The TT keeps both the transliteration and Mark's own translation. The clause "[whom he also named emissaries/apostles]" (3:14) is bracketed in NA28 (possibly imported from Luke 6:13) and is rendered as text-uncertain.

### A3. Blasphemy against the holy wind/spirit — "eternal sin"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

At 3:29 "whoever blasphemes against the holy {wind/spirit}" keeps the *pneuma* slash (the divine spirit, as in 1:8). **οὐκ ἔχει ἄφεσιν εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα** = "has no forgiveness for the age/forever"; **ἁμαρτήματος αἰωνίου** = "an eternal sin." Crucially, the narrator **defines** the blasphemy in 3:30: "because they were saying, 'He has an unclean spirit'" — i.e. attributing the holy spirit's work to an unclean spirit. The TT renders the severity plainly and lets the narrator's definition stand.

### A4. Family redefined around the will of God (3:31–35)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The closing scene reconstitutes kinship: "whoever does the will of God, this one is my brother and sister and mother" (3:35). The list omits "father" — possibly reserving that designation for God. This is not a denial of family but a redefinition around obedience. The TT renders the reconstitution without drawing the inference, and preserves the spatial contrast (the blood family "outside," 3:31, vs. those seated "around him," 3:34) that the intercalation (3:20–35) builds.

## B. Ancient Near Eastern and Second Temple Parallels

### B1. Saving life overrides the sabbath — *pikuach nefesh*
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

Yeshua's question — "Is it lawful on the sabbath to do good or to do harm, to save a life or to kill?" (3:4) — engages a principle later formalized as *pikuach nefesh*: the saving of life sets aside the sabbath. The principle is debated and developed in Second Temple and rabbinic sources; 1 Maccabees 2:39–41 records the Maccabees deciding to fight defensively on the sabbath to avoid annihilation, and later rabbinic rulings (*m. Yoma* 8:6; *b. Yoma* 85a–b) affirm that danger to life suspends sabbath restrictions. The withered hand was not life-threatening, so Yeshua's framing pushes the principle from "saving life" toward "doing good" — a broader claim that the watchers do not answer.

**Source:** Doering, L., *Schabbat*, Mohr Siebeck, 1999; Sanders, E.P., *Jewish Law from Jesus to the Mishnah*, SCM Press, 1990.

### B2. Beelzevul, Satan, and exorcism-by-collusion accusations
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The charge that Yeshua casts out demons "by the ruler of the demons" (3:22) fits a recognizable polemic: an exorcist's power could be attributed to a higher demonic authority rather than to God. "Satan" (*ha-satan*, "the adversary") develops across the HB and Second Temple literature from a heavenly accuser (Job 1–2; Zech 3) to a more personalized opponent (Jubilees, the Qumran "Belial"). "Beelzevul" (Βεελζεβούλ) is a name for the demonic ruler, likely related to a Semitic title and to Baal-zebub of Ekron (2 Kings 1:2). Yeshua's reply turns the accusation on its logic: a self-divided Satan would collapse.

**Source:** Wright, A.T., *The Origin of Evil Spirits*, Mohr Siebeck, 2005; Twelftree, G.H., *Jesus the Exorcist*, Mohr Siebeck, 1993.

## C. Historical Context

### C1. Pharisees and Herodians — an unusual alliance
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

"The Perushim (Pharisees)... with the Herodians" plotting against Yeshua (3:6) pairs groups normally at odds. The **Herodians** were supporters of the Herodian dynasty (here, Antipas in Galilee) — a political faction, not a religious sect; the Pharisees were a lay movement focused on Torah observance. Their joint counsel signals that opposition to Yeshua crossed the usual political-religious lines. Such ad hoc alliances against a shared perceived threat are historically plausible in the factional landscape of Antipas's Galilee.

**Source:** Meier, J.P., *A Marginal Jew*, vol. 3, Doubleday, 2001 (on Herodians); Sanders, E.P., *Judaism: Practice and Belief*, 1992.

### C2. The geographic catalogue of the crowd (3:7–8)
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The crowd comes from the Galil (Galilee), Yehudah (Judea), Yerushalayim (Jerusalem), Idumea (Edom, to the south), beyond the Yarden (Jordan) (Peraea/the Transjordan), and the regions of Tsor (Tyre) and Tsidon (Sidon) (Phoenician coast, to the north). The list spans the whole land and its Gentile-adjacent edges, defining the reach of Yeshua's fame and anticipating a mission beyond strictly Jewish territory. The named regions are real first-century administrative/cultural zones, plausibly drawn by reports of healings.

**Source:** Marcus, J., *Mark 1–8* (Anchor Yale Bible), Yale, 2000; Reed, J.L., *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, 2000.

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. *Boanerges* — a difficult transliteration
**[TEXTUAL — POSSIBLE]**

**Βοανηργές** (*Boanērges*) is Mark's Greek rendering of a Semitic phrase he translates as "sons of thunder." The first element is plausibly *bnê* ("sons of"); the second is debated — perhaps *regesh*/*rogez* ("commotion, rage") or an Aramaic word for "thunder" (*ra'am*); the exact underlying form is uncertain, and the Greek spelling is irregular. The TT keeps the transliteration and Mark's own gloss without resolving the etymological puzzle.

### D2. "The Kena'ani (Cananaean)" = Zealot, not "from Cana"
**[TEXTUAL — PROBABLE]**

**Σίμων ὁ Καναναῖος** (*Simōn ho Kananaios*, 3:18) is **not** "Simon from Cana." The epithet transliterates Aramaic *qan'ana* ("zealous/zealot"); Luke 6:15 confirms the sense by translating it into Greek as *zēlōtēs* ("the Zealot"). The TT renders "Shimon the Kena'ani" and notes the "zealous/Zealot" meaning — flagging that "Cananaean" can mislead a reader into a place-name.

### D3. *Beelzebul* — the name's background
**[TEXTUAL — POSSIBLE]**

**Βεελζεβούλ** (*Beelzeboul*) likely derives from a Semitic title beginning *ba'al* ("lord"). Proposals for the second element include *zebul* ("exalted dwelling / height" — "lord of the [heavenly] dwelling") and a polemical distortion toward *zebub* ("flies" — Baal-zebub of Ekron, 2 Kings 1:2) or *zebel* ("dung"). The variant spelling Beelzebub in some later witnesses reflects assimilation to 2 Kings. The TT transliterates "Beelzevul" and notes the contested background.

## F. Later Reception in Other Traditions

### F1. The "unforgivable sin" in Christian tradition
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

Mark 3:28–30 generated centuries of pastoral and theological reflection on the "sin against the Holy Spirit." Readings range from a specific first-century act (attributing the Spirit's work to demonic power, per the narrator's own definition in v.30) to a settled, final refusal of God's grace. Anxious self-application — fear that one has committed the unpardonable sin — recurs in Christian pastoral literature. The TT renders the saying with the narrator's definition intact (v.30) and does not extend it into later systematic frameworks.

**Source:** Boring, M.E., *Mark: A Commentary* (NTL), Westminster John Knox, 2006.

### F2. The "brothers of Yeshua" (3:31–35; cf. 6:3)
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

Mark names Yeshua's "mother and brothers" (3:31; cf. the named brothers and sisters of 6:3). Later traditions interpreted "brothers" in three main ways: as full siblings (children of Miryam and Yosef — the most natural reading of the Greek *adelphoi*); as step-siblings (children of Yosef from a prior marriage — the Eastern/"Epiphanian" view); or as cousins (the "Hieronymian" view, supporting the perpetual virginity of Miryam). The TT renders *adelphoi* as "brothers" and notes that the reception history is shaped by later Marian doctrine, not by Mark's wording.

**Source:** Bauckham, R., "The Brothers and Sisters of Jesus," *CBQ* 56 (1994), pp. 686–700.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. What exactly is the unforgivable sin?
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE]**

The narrator ties it specifically to calling the holy spirit's work an unclean spirit (3:30) — a deliberate, knowing inversion of good and evil at their source. Whether the saying describes a single decisive act or a settled disposition, and whether it is even possible after Yeshua's lifetime, are questions Mark does not answer. The TT preserves the text's own framing (v.30) and the openness.

### G2. Why does Yeshua feel anger and grief (3:5)?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Mark alone reports that Yeshua looked around "with anger, grieved at the hardness of their heart" (3:5) — Matthew and Luke omit the emotion. The object of the anger is the watchers' silence and "hardness of heart" (*pōrōsis*) in the face of a chance to do good. Mark's willingness to attribute strong emotion to Yeshua (cf. 1:41 compassion/anger variant; 10:14 indignation) is a feature later Gospels tend to soften. The TT keeps it.

### G3. Mark's "sandwich" (intercalation) at 3:20–35
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Mark frequently splits one story to wrap another inside it. Here the family's approach (3:20–21) is interrupted by the Beelzevul dispute (3:22–30) and resumes at 3:31–35, so the two episodes interpret each other: those who think Yeshua is "beside himself" (3:21) and those who say he "has an unclean spirit" (3:30) are juxtaposed, and the redefinition of family (3:35) answers the blood family's attempt to "seize him" (3:21). The TT renders the sequence as Mark structures it.

### SCENARIO A — Pre-70 CE (Temple still standing)

#### IA-1. Social Structure — "Twelve" and the symbolism of tribal restoration
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Appointing **twelve** (3:14) would resonate in a first-century Jewish setting as evocative of the twelve tribes of Israel — ten of which were "lost" since the Assyrian conquest. Hopes for the regathering and restoration of all twelve tribes were a live strand of Second Temple expectation (cf. Sirach 48:10; the Qumran *War Scroll* and *Temple Scroll* organize Israel by twelve; *Psalms of Solomon* 17). Constituting a group of twelve around himself would be read as a symbolic claim about the reconstitution of Israel — a claim Mark states by the number, not by commentary.

**Source:** Sanders, E.P., *Jesus and Judaism*, Fortress, 1985, pp. 95–106; Horsley, R.A., *Jesus and the Spiral of Violence*, Harper & Row, 1987.

#### IA-2. Political — Herodians, Pharisees, and Antipas's Galilee
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The Pharisee–Herodian counsel (3:6) reflects the factional politics of Herod Antipas's tetrarchy. Antipas (r. 4 BCE–39 CE) ruled Galilee and Peraea as a Roman client; his supporters ("Herodians") had political and economic stakes in stability. A popular Galilean teacher drawing large crowds (3:7–9) and challenging recognized authority could be read as a destabilizing figure — the same Antipas had executed Yochanan (John) the Immerser (Mark 6; Josephus, *Ant.* 18.116–119, citing fear of unrest). The plot of 3:6 is thus historically intelligible as elite anxiety about a popular movement, not merely a theological dispute.

**Source:** Jensen, M.H., *Herod Antipas in Galilee*, 2nd ed., Mohr Siebeck, 2010; Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.116–119, Loeb Classical Library.

---

### SCENARIO B — Post-70 CE (Temple destroyed)

#### IB-1. Religion — Family, allegiance, and community after 70 CE
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The redefinition of family around "the will of God" (3:35) would carry particular weight for communities reading Mark after 70 CE. As followers of Yeshua increasingly faced tension with both Roman authority and the reorganizing Jewish community, allegiance to the movement could strain blood-family ties (a theme explicit at 13:12, "brother will hand over brother to death"). A community whose own membership cut across and sometimes against natural families would hear 3:31–35 as charter: the doer of God's will, not the blood relative, is kin. The Beelzevul-style charge — that the movement's power is illegitimate or deranged — also maps onto post-70 polemics against the young movement.

**Source:** Marcus, J., *Mark 1–8* (Anchor Yale Bible), 2000, intro; Kee, H.C., *Community of the New Age: Studies in Mark's Gospel*, Westminster, 1977.
