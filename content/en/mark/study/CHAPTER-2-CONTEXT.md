## A. Source Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. "Son of Man" — first occurrence (2:10, 2:28)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

**ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου** (*ho huios tou anthrōpou*) is literally "the son of the human/man" — a Greek calque of the Aramaic **בַּר אֱנָשׁ** (*bar enash*). The idiom ranges from generic "a human being," through a self-referential circumlocution, to the exalted figure of **Daniel 7:13** ("one like a son of man" coming with the clouds, given dominion). Mark's first two uses already attach divine prerogatives — authority to forgive (2:10) and lordship over the sabbath (2:28) — tilting toward the exalted sense without abandoning the human one. The TT keeps the established "Son of Man" and notes the range; it does not collapse the ambiguity to either "human being" or "the Messiah."

### A2. *Aphiēmi* — "forgive" and "release"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

"Your sins are forgiven" (2:5,9) renders **ἀφίενται** (*aphientai*), from **ἀφίημι** (*aphiēmi*) = "send away / let go / release / forgive" — the verbal counterpart of the noun *aphesis* ("release," 1:4). The present passive ("are forgiven") may be a *divine passive* (God forgives), which is exactly what the scribes contest in 2:7: "who can forgive sins but God?" The TT keeps the readable "forgiven" with the concrete "release" sense noted, preserving the link to 1:4.

### A3. "The sabbath came into being for the human" (2:27)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

**τὸ σάββατον διὰ τὸν ἄνθρωπον ἐγένετο** (*to sabbaton dia ton anthrōpon egeneto*) = "the sabbath came into being for the human." **ἄνθρωπος** (*anthrōpos*) is generic (humanity), not male-specific; the TT renders "the human." **ἐγένετο** ("came into being") grounds the sabbath in its origin as a creation gift (Gen 2:2–3) ordered to human benefit. At 2:28 *kyrios* ("lord even of the sabbath") is **not** the divine name (no OT quotation) — it means "lord/master."

### A4. *Pneuma* at 2:8 — Yeshua's own spirit (no slash)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

"Knowing in his spirit" (2:8) uses **τῷ πνεύματι αὐτοῦ** (*tō pneumati autou*) — here *pneuma* is Yeshua's own inner spirit/perception, not the divine or cosmic wind/spirit. "Wind" is not a live sense, so the TT does **not** apply the wind/spirit slash here (contrast 1:8,10,12). The same context-gated principle keeps "unclean spirit" unslashed elsewhere.

## B. Ancient Near Eastern and Second Temple Parallels

### B1. Forgiveness of sins as a divine prerogative
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The scribes' objection — "who can forgive sins except one, God?" (2:7) — voices a widely held Second Temple conviction (cf. Isa 43:25, "I, I am he who blots out your transgressions"). A possible parallel is the *Prayer of Nabonidus* (4Q242) from Qumran, where a Jewish exorcist "forgave" the king's sin — though the text is fragmentary and its sense debated. Human pronouncement of God's forgiveness (e.g. by a priest in the cult) was conceivable; an unmediated declaration on one's own authority, as in 2:5, is what provokes the charge of blasphemy.

**Source:** Collins, J.J., *Daniel* (Hermeneia), Fortress, 1993 (on 4Q242); Sanders, E.P., *Judaism: Practice and Belief*, SCM Press, 1992.

### B2. Table fellowship, tax collectors, and "sinners"
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

Eating together carried strong social meaning in Second Temple Judaism; shared meals signaled acceptance and shared status. "Tax collectors" (*telōnai*) — toll-farmers who profited above the assessed rate — were widely despised and grouped with "sinners" (those outside observant society). Pharisaic concern for purity at the table made dining with such people pointed. Yeshua's table fellowship (2:15–17) is a deliberate boundary-crossing; the physician proverb reframes it as care for the sick rather than endorsement of wrongdoing.

**Source:** Neyrey, J.H., "Ceremonies in Luke-Acts," in *The Social World of Luke-Acts*, Hendrickson, 1991; Donahue, J.R., "Tax Collectors and Sinners," *CBQ* 33 (1971), pp. 39–61.

## C. Historical Context

### C1. The dug-open roof — Galilean house construction
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The four who "unroofed the roof... and having dug it out" (2:4) fit a typical Galilean village house: walls of basalt fieldstone, a flat roof of wooden beams overlaid with branches, reeds, and packed earth, reached by an external stair. Such a roof could be dug through and patched. Excavated houses at Kfar Nachum (Capernaum) and elsewhere in the Galilee match this construction. The detail is concrete and physically plausible, characteristic of Mark's vivid narration.

**Source:** Hirschfeld, Y., *The Palestinian Dwelling in the Roman-Byzantine Period*, Franciscan Printing Press, 1995; Reed, J.L., *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, 2000.

### C2. Levi at the tax booth — toll collection at Capernaum
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Levi sitting "at the tax booth" (*telōnion*, 2:14) reflects the customs system. Kfar Nachum (Capernaum) lay near a frontier between the territories of Herod Antipas (Galilee) and Philip (Gaulanitis) and on trade routes around the lake — a plausible toll/customs station. Toll collectors leased collection rights and profited from the margin, a structure that incentivized over-collection and earned the profession its reputation. Calling Levi from this booth (2:14) parallels the call of the fishermen from their nets (1:16–20): a person summoned directly out of an ordinary, compromised livelihood.

**Source:** Hanson, K.C. and Oakman, D.E., *Palestine in the Time of Jesus*, 2nd ed., Fortress, 2008; Reed, J.L., *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, 2000.

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. The "Son of Man" idiom — Aramaic substratum and Daniel 7
**[TEXTUAL — PROBABLE]**

Aramaic *bar enash* (and Hebrew *ben adam*) can function as a generic ("a human"), as an indefinite ("someone"), or — in some usage — as a self-reference. **Daniel 7:13** gives the phrase a specific charge: "one like a son of man" receives everlasting dominion. The Greek articular form *ho huios tou anthrōpou* is unusual (doubly articular) and reads as a fixed title. Whether Mark's Yeshua intends the generic, the self-referential, or the Daniel-7 sense — or holds them together — is exactly what the TT preserves by keeping "Son of Man" and refusing to disambiguate.

### D2. "Abiathar the high priest" (2:26) — a textual and historical crux
**[TEXTUAL — UNCERTAIN]**

Mark's "in *the time of* Abiathar the high priest" (**ἐπὶ Ἀβιαθὰρ ἀρχιερέως**) names a different priest than **1 Samuel 21:1–6**, where the priest who gives David the bread is **Ahimelech** (Abiathar's father). Proposed resolutions: (a) *epi* means "in the passage about Abiathar" (a section heading sense) — grammatically strained; (b) Abiathar, the more famous priest (David's later companion), is named loosely; (c) a genuine slip. Notably, some manuscripts (D, W, and the Old Latin) **omit** the phrase, easing the difficulty — itself evidence that early scribes felt the problem. The TT renders the NA28 text and flags the crux.

## F. Later Reception in Other Traditions

### F1. "Son of Man" in later Christology
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

In later Christian tradition "Son of Man" was often read as a title designating Yeshua's humanity (paired with "Son of God" for divinity) — a reading shaped by later two-natures christology. This inverts the term's apocalyptic weight: in Daniel 7 and much Second Temple usage the "son of man" figure is exalted and heavenly, not a marker of mere humanity. The TT renders the phrase as Mark writes it and notes the Daniel-7 background; the "humanity" reading is documented later reception.

**Source:** Hurtado, L.W., *Lord Jesus Christ: Devotion to Jesus in Earliest Christianity*, Eerdmans, 2003.

### F2. Sabbath controversies and the parting of the ways
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The sabbath disputes (2:23–28; 3:1–6) became focal in later Jewish–Christian differentiation. As emerging Christian communities relaxed or reinterpreted sabbath observance, passages like 2:27–28 were read as warrant. In their first-century setting, however, the disputes are inner-Jewish debates over *how* to keep the sabbath (what counts as work), not whether to keep it — a distinction the TT preserves by rendering the controversy without later supersessionist framing.

**Source:** Bauckham, R., "Sabbath and Sunday in the Post-Apostolic Church," in *From Sabbath to Lord's Day*, ed. Carson, Zondervan, 1982.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. The Abiathar problem — how readers have handled it
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE]**

Beyond the text-critical note (D2), the discrepancy has generated a long interpretive history: harmonizers argue Abiathar was present or co-served; others take it as a minor inexactitude that does not affect the argument (need overrides ritual restriction); still others see Mark citing from memory. The TT does not adjudicate; it preserves the reading and the question, consistent with not smoothing difficulties.

### G2. New wine, fresh wineskins — what is the "new"?
**[TEXTUAL — POSSIBLE]**

The paired sayings (2:21–22) state a principle — the new bursts old containers — without naming the referent. Candidates: the in-breaking kingdom, Yeshua's message, the new community, the joy of the bridegroom's presence. Mark gives the parable, not the application; the TT renders the image and leaves the inference to the reader.

### G3. Whose house? (2:15)
**[TEXTUAL — POSSIBLE]**

"He reclines in his house" (2:15) is ambiguous: the host may be Levi (just called) or Yeshua. Greek pronoun reference does not settle it. The TT preserves the ambiguity rather than supplying a name.

### SCENARIO A — Pre-70 CE (Temple still standing)

#### IA-1. Economy — The customs-and-toll system behind Levi's booth
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Levi's tax booth (2:14) sits inside a layered extraction system. Direct Roman taxes (land and poll tax) applied in provinces under direct rule; in Antipas's Galilee, Herodian taxation plus tolls and customs (*telē*) on goods in transit funded the tetrarch and Rome. Toll-collection rights were leased; the lessee profited from whatever he gathered above the contracted sum — a structure that built over-collection into the system and made *telōnai* bywords for dishonesty. Capernaum's position near territorial borders and lake trade routes made a customs post economically logical. The social scandal of 2:14–17 is therefore concrete: Yeshua calls, and dines with, a participant in the machinery of imperial-Herodian extraction.

**Source:** Hanson, K.C. and Oakman, D.E., *Palestine in the Time of Jesus*, 2nd ed., Fortress, 2008; Udoh, F.E., *To Caesar What Is Caesar's: Tribute, Taxes, and Imperial Administration in Early Roman Palestine*, Brown Judaic Studies, 2005.

#### IA-2. Religion — Pharisaic sabbath halakha and voluntary fasting
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The disputes over plucking grain (2:23–24) and fasting (2:18) reflect live Second Temple debate. Defining the thirty-nine categories of prohibited sabbath "work" (later codified in *m. Shabbat* 7:2) was already under discussion; whether plucking counts as reaping is exactly the kind of boundary question the Pharisees pressed. Voluntary fasting beyond the one required fast (the Day of Atonement) was a recognized piety; the Pharisees were known to fast twice a week (cf. Luke 18:12), and John's disciples fasted too. The questions put to Yeshua are not hostile inventions but the ordinary friction of competing observance communities.

**Source:** Sanders, E.P., *Jewish Law from Jesus to the Mishnah*, SCM Press, 1990; Doering, L., *Schabbat: Sabbathalacha und -praxis im antiken Judentum und Urchristentum*, Mohr Siebeck, 1999.

---

### SCENARIO B — Post-70 CE (Temple destroyed)

#### IB-1. Religion — Sabbath disputes after the Temple
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

After 70 CE, with the sacrificial cult gone, sabbath observance and Torah study took on heightened importance as carriers of Jewish identity, and rabbinic *halakha* on the sabbath was systematized. Read in that setting, Mark's sabbath controversies (2:23–28) — preserved and circulated in communities increasingly distinct from the synagogue — would have read as stakes-claims about authority over the Torah's interpretation. "The Son of Man is lord even of the sabbath" (2:28) is, for a post-70 audience, a claim about who interprets the sacred day now that the Temple no longer anchors the calendar of worship.

**Source:** Cohen, S.J.D., *From the Maccabees to the Mishnah*, 3rd ed., Westminster John Knox, 2014; Marcus, J., *Mark 1–8* (Anchor Yale Bible), 2000.
