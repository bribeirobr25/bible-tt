## HOW TO USE THIS COMPANION

This file contains background material organized by topic. Each entry carries **two labels:**

**What kind of claim?** (tells you the source of the information)
- **TEXTUAL** — derived directly from the Greek text
- **STRONG INFERENCE** — logically entailed by the text, not stated explicitly
- **POSSIBLE INFERENCE** — one legitimate reading among others
- **COMPARATIVE PARALLEL** — attested in another ancient source
- **LATER RECEPTION** — post-biblical reading
- **HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL** — based on material or historical evidence
- **SPECULATION** — no strong textual or evidential basis

**How certain?** (tells you the confidence level)
- **VERIFIED** — strong evidence, wide agreement
- **PROBABLE** — well-supported but debated
- **POSSIBLE** — one reasonable reading among others
- **UNCERTAIN** — genuinely unclear
- **SPECULATIVE** — must be rare and clearly flagged
- **DOCUMENTED** — historically attested reception (for LATER RECEPTION entries)

**Reading principle:** Start with TEXTUAL entries — those come from the Greek itself. Then explore outward to parallels, reception, and comparison. The companion presents; you assess.

## A. Source Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. "Beginning of the good news" — *archē* and *euangelion*
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Mark opens **Ἀρχὴ τοῦ εὐαγγελίου** (*Archē tou euangeliou*), "Beginning of the good news" (1:1) — a verbless, anarthrous superscription. **Ἀρχή** (*archē*, "beginning") is the first word of the LXX of Genesis 1:1 (*en archē*) and of John 1:1; the echo signals a "beginning" of cosmic weight. **εὐαγγέλιον** (*euangelion*) the TT renders "good news," not the transliteration-by-tradition "gospel." In the first-century world the word also carried imperial associations — announcements of an emperor's accession or military victory were *euangelia* — so "good news of Yeshua the anointed one" sets a counter-claim against imperial proclamation. Whether *archē* governs only the prologue (1:1–13) or the whole book is deliberately open.

### A2. "Kingdom of God," not "kingdom of the skies"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Mark 1:15 reads **ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ** (*hē basileia tou theou*), "the kingdom of God." Matthew's characteristic "kingdom of the skies" (*basileia tōn ouranōn*) never appears in Mark. The TT renders each Gospel's own wording and does not harmonize; readers comparing Mark 1:15 with Matthew 4:17 will see the difference preserved rather than smoothed. **ἤγγικεν** (*ēngiken*, perfect) = "has drawn near" — arrived-and-near; whether "is arriving" or "has arrived" is left open.

### A3. The composite citation attributed to Isaiah
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Mark 1:2–3 is a fused citation: v.2 quotes **Malachi 3:1** (with wording from **Exodus 23:20**), v.3 quotes **Isaiah 40:3** — and Mark attributes the whole to "Isaiah the prophet." The TT preserves "Isaiah" (the better-attested, harder reading; the Byzantine "in the prophets" is a later harmonizing fix). The combination of a "messenger before your face" (Malachi) with "a voice in the wilderness" (Isaiah) frames Yochanan (John) as both the forerunner-messenger and the wilderness herald. The *kyrios* of Isaiah 40:3 renders YHWH; per GS Option C the TT reads "the Lord," noting the referent.

### A4. *Euthys* ("immediately") — the engine of the narrative
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The adverb **εὐθύς** (*euthys*, "immediately") recurs about eleven times in chapter 1 alone (1:10,12,18,20,21,23,28,29,30,42,43) and dozens of times across the Gospel — far denser than in Matthew or Luke. The TT preserves it at every occurrence rather than varying it stylistically. The cumulative effect is breathless momentum: scenes are stitched together with an urgency that is itself a feature of Mark's theology of the in-breaking kingdom.

### A5. The skies "torn open" — *schizō* and the 15:38 inclusio
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

At the immersion the skies are **σχιζομένους** (*schizomenous*, "being torn/split," 1:10) — a violent verb, unlike Matthew's "opened" (*ēneōchthēsan*). The same root returns at the crucifixion when the Temple curtain is "torn" (*eschisthē*, 15:38), framing the Gospel between two tearings: heaven opened at the start, the barrier of the holy place torn at the end. The TT keeps "torn," preserving the verbal link that "opened" would erase.

## B. Ancient Near Eastern and Second Temple Parallels

### B1. Ritual immersion in Second Temple Judaism — mikveh
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

Ritual immersion (*tevilah*) in a *mikveh* was widespread in the Second Temple period; dozens of stepped immersion pools have been excavated in Yerushalayim (Jerusalem) (near the Temple Mount, in priestly residences) and across Judea, dating to the Hasmonean and Herodian periods. Standard mikveh practice was self-administered, repeated, private, and tied to the Levitical purity categories (Lev 11–15). Yochanan (John)'s immersion differs: it is administered by another, one-time, public, and tied to a change of mind and the expectation of imminent divine judgment rather than to ritual-purity transitions.

**Source:** Reich, R., *Miqwa'ot in the Second Temple Period*, Israel Exploration Society, 2013; Sanders, E.P., *Judaism: Practice and Belief, 63 BCE – 66 CE*, SCM Press, 1992, pp. 214–227.

### B2. Qumran, the wilderness, and Isaiah 40:3
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The Qumran community (likely Essenes) settled in the Judean wilderness and took **Isaiah 40:3** as a charter text: "in the wilderness prepare the way of YHWH" (1QS 8:12–16) — the very verse Mark applies to Yochanan (John). The community combined water-immersion with inner moral transformation ("he shall not be purified... unless he turns from his wickedness," 1QS 5:13–14). Whether John had direct contact with Qumran is unknown, but the shared geography, the shared scriptural anchor, and the shared linkage of immersion to inner change make the conceptual overlap significant.

**Source:** VanderKam, J., *The Dead Sea Scrolls Today*, 2nd ed., Eerdmans, 2010, pp. 87–96; Taylor, J.E., *The Immerser: John the Baptist within Second Temple Judaism*, Eerdmans, 1997.

## C. Historical Context

### C1. The wilderness of Judah and the Jordan
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Mark places Yochanan (John) in "the wilderness" and immersing "in the Yarden (Jordan) river." The Judean wilderness is the arid region descending from the hill country to the Dead Sea — sparsely inhabited, traditionally associated with prophetic withdrawal (Eliyahu, David). The Jordan carried layered symbolic weight: Israel's crossing into the land (Josh 3–4), Eliyahu's translation site and Elisha's continuation (2 Kings 2:8–14), Na'aman's cleansing (2 Kings 5:10–14). Choosing the Jordan situated John's practice within this threshold-and-cleansing tradition.

**Source:** Murphy-O'Connor, J., *The Holy Land: An Oxford Archaeological Guide*, 5th ed., Oxford, 2008, pp. 131–134.

### C2. Kfar Nachum (Capernaum) — fishing town and synagogue
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Kfar Nachum (Capernaum), on the north shore of the sea of Galilee, becomes Yeshua's base of operations in Mark (1:21,29). Excavations have revealed a first-century fishing village: basalt houses, fishing implements, and an earlier synagogue foundation beneath the later (4th–5th c.) white limestone synagogue. Fishing was a taxed, regulated industry on the lake; the call of Shimon (Simon), Andreas (Andrew), Ya'aqov (James), and Yochanan (John) from their nets (1:16–20) — including hired men (1:20) — fits a real local economy, not a romanticized one.

**Source:** Reed, J.L., *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, Trinity Press International, 2000; Tzaferis, V., "Capernaum," in *The New Encyclopedia of Archaeological Excavations in the Holy Land*, vol. 1, 1993.

### C3. The "Galilee Boat" — a first-century hull from the lake
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

In 1986 a drought lowered the sea of Galilee and exposed a wooden hull in the mud near Kibbutz Ginosar (ancient Magdala/Migdal). Excavated and conserved, the **"Galilee Boat"** measures about **8.2 m long × 2.3 m wide × 1.25 m deep** — built of cedar planking on oak frames with pegged mortise-and-tenon joints, and able to carry roughly fifteen people (a crew plus passengers, or crew plus catch). Radiocarbon dating and associated pottery (a cooking pot and a lamp) place it around **40 BCE, give or take 80 years** — the late-Hellenistic to early-Roman period. It was assembled from **at least a dozen different wood types** and shows repeated repairs and reused timber: the signature of a working vessel kept going on a tight budget.

This is exactly the kind of modest, resource-constrained craft behind the fishing enterprises Mark describes (1:16–20) — the boat of Zavdai (Zebedee) and his sons, worked "with the hired men" (1:20). It gives physical texture to the lake economy the first disciples left.

**There is no evidence connecting this particular boat to Yeshua or to any disciple.** It is *type-representative* — an illustration of the kind of vessel in use on the lake — not "the boat of the gospels."

**Source:** Shelley Wachsmann, "The Galilee Boat — 2,000-Year-Old Hull Recovered Intact," *Biblical Archaeology Review* 14.5 (1988): 18–33 [PEER-REVIEWED]; Wachsmann (ed.), *The Excavations of an Ancient Boat in the Sea of Galilee (Lake Kinneret)*, *'Atiqot* 19 (1990).

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. *Pneuma* = wind/spirit — alignment with *ruach*
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Greek **πνεῦμα** (*pneuma*) carries the full range of Hebrew **רוּחַ** (*ruach*): wind, breath, spirit. Where the divine spirit is in view (1:8 "holy"; 1:10 descent; 1:12 driving-out), the TT keeps the wind/spirit slash to preserve the genuine ambiguity (Rule 2). The descent "like a dove" at 1:10 echoes the *ruach elohim* hovering over the waters in Genesis 1:2 — wind/spirit at a threshold of new beginning, over water. Where *pneuma* means an "unclean spirit" (1:23,26,27), the slash is dropped — "wind" is not a live sense there.

### D2. "Son of God" (1:1) — meaning and the textual question
**[TEXTUAL — UNCERTAIN]**

The phrase **υἱοῦ θεοῦ** (*huiou theou*, "of the son of God") at 1:1 is **bracketed in NA28**: absent from some major witnesses (א*, Θ, 28), present in others (א¹, B, D, L, W). It may be original to the title or an early scribal expansion. As a phrase, "son of God" in a first-century Jewish frame could denote the Davidic king (Ps 2:7; 2 Sam 7:14), Israel as God's son (Hos 11:1), or a uniquely filial relation; the Gospel's later usages (1:11; 15:39) develop it. The TT retains the NA28 reading and flags the dispute rather than deciding it silently.

## F. Later Reception in Other Traditions

### F1. Spirit-immersion and baptismal theology
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

John's contrast — "I immersed you in water, but he will immerse you in holy wind/spirit" (1:8) — became a seedbed for later Christian theologies of baptism and the Spirit. Acts develops "baptism in the holy spirit" (Acts 1:5; 2; 11:16); Paul links baptism to dying and rising with the anointed one (Rom 6:3–4) — categories that go well beyond what Mark 1 states. The TT renders John's words on their own terms; the later sacramental and pneumatological developments are documented reception, not the text's claim.

**Source:** Ferguson, E., *Baptism in the Early Church*, Eerdmans, 2009.

### F2. The "messianic secret" in modern scholarship
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

Mark's repeated silencing of the demons (1:25,34) and of the healed launched a major scholarly debate. William Wrede's *Das Messiasgeheimnis in den Evangelien* (1901) argued that the "messianic secret" was a literary-theological device of the evangelist rather than a historical memory. Subsequent scholarship has variously affirmed, modified, or rejected Wrede's thesis. The TT records each silencing plainly and does not adjudicate the motive; the "secret" as an interpretive category is post-biblical scholarship, noted here as reception.

**Source:** Wrede, W., *The Messianic Secret* (ET 1971 of the 1901 German); Tuckett, C. (ed.), *The Messianic Secret*, Fortress, 1983.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. Why is Yeshua immersed?
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE]**

John's immersion is "of change of mind toward release of sins" (1:4). Mark, unlike Matthew (Matt 3:14–15), records no dialogue explaining why Yeshua (Jesus) submits to it — he simply comes and is immersed (1:9). The question (sharpened by later claims of his sinlessness) is left entirely open by Mark. Options developed elsewhere include identification with the people, inauguration of mission (ratified by the voice, 1:11), and comprehensive obedience. The TT renders Mark's spare account without importing the explanations.

### G2. "With the wild animals" (1:13)
**[TEXTUAL — POSSIBLE]**

Mark alone notes that during the wilderness testing Yeshua "was with the wild animals" (**μετὰ τῶν θηρίων**, *meta tōn thēriōn*). The phrase is ambiguous: it may evoke Edenic peace with creation / the messianic-age harmony of Isaiah 11:6–9, or wilderness danger and threat, or both. Mark gives no interpretation; the TT preserves the bare, suggestive detail.

### G3. "You are my son" — second person
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

At the immersion Mark's voice says **σὺ εἶ ὁ υἱός μου** (*sy ei ho huios mou*), "**You** are my son" — addressed directly to Yeshua (Jesus). Matthew 3:17 has the third person ("**This** is my son"), addressed to bystanders; Luke 3:22 follows Mark. The TT preserves each Gospel's form without harmonizing. The declaration merges Psalm 2:7 (royal sonship) and Isaiah 42:1 (the servant), with a possible echo of Genesis 22:2 (LXX "your son, the beloved").

### SCENARIO A — Pre-70 CE (Temple still standing)

#### IA-1. Daily Life — The Galilean fishing economy
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The call of two pairs of brothers from their nets (1:16–20) is embedded in a real industry. Fishing on the sea of Galilee was organized, taxed, and licensed: fishers worked in cooperatives, leased fishing rights, and sold to processing centers (the town of Magdala/Tarichaea was a major fish-salting hub). The mention of Zavdai (Zebedee) employing "hired men" (1:20) indicates a family business of some substance, not subsistence poverty — leaving it was a real economic cost. First-century fishing boats carried crews of several, consistent with the named groups (for the recovered first-century hull, see §C3).

**Source:** Hanson, K.C., "The Galilean Fishing Economy and the Jesus Tradition," *Biblical Theology Bulletin* 27 (1997), pp. 99–111; Reed, J.L., *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, 2000.

#### IA-2. Religion — Synagogue, sabbath, and exorcism
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Yeshua teaching and exorcising in the Kfar Nachum (Capernaum) synagogue on the sabbath (1:21–28) reflects recognizable institutions. First-century synagogues were community assembly buildings for Torah reading and teaching; teaching "as one having authority and not as the scribes" (1:22) contrasts direct authority with the scribal practice of citing tradition. Belief in unclean spirits and the practice of exorcism were widespread; Jewish exorcists typically used adjurations, names, and ritual means (cf. Tobit; Josephus, *Ant.* 8.45–49 on Solomon-attributed techniques). Yeshua's exorcism by bare command — "Be muzzled, and come out of him" — would strike contemporaries as unusually direct (1:27, "a new teaching with authority").

**Source:** Levine, L.I., *The Ancient Synagogue: The First Thousand Years*, 2nd ed., Yale, 2005; Twelftree, G.H., *Jesus the Exorcist*, Mohr Siebeck, 1993.

#### IA-3. Daily Life — How urban (and how Greek) was Galilee? Recalibrating Sepphoris
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

A popular picture imagines the Galilee of Mark 1 as shaped by a cosmopolitan, heavily Hellenized city — Sepphoris, a few miles from Nazareth — making Yeshua a product of an urbane, Greek-speaking milieu. Recent scholarship recalibrates this. Nathan Schumer's demographic study argues early-Roman Sepphoris held only about **2,000–4,300 people** (roughly half of earlier estimates), reaching perhaps 8,000–8,500 only after 70 CE — so urbanization in early-Roman Galilee was "fairly moderate and not fundamentally transformative for the region." Mark A. Chancey's work likewise finds first-century Galilee predominantly Jewish and **Aramaic-speaking**, with Greek mainly an administrative-elite language rather than the vernacular of the villages. The frame that better fits Mark's opening is therefore a network of modest fishing and farming villages (Kfar Nachum, Bethsaida) alongside a smaller, more modestly Hellenized urban presence than the "cosmopolitan Sepphoris" image suggests. The degree of Galilean urbanization and Hellenization remains **actively debated**; this entry flags the debate rather than settling it. (No claim here links ancient Galileans to any modern population, nation, or ethnic group.)

**Source:** Nathan Schumer, "The Population of Sepphoris: Rethinking Urbanization in Early and Middle Roman Galilee," *Journal of Ancient Judaism* 8.1 (2017) [PEER-REVIEWED]; Mark A. Chancey, *The Myth of a Gentile Galilee* (Cambridge University Press, 2002) [PEER-REVIEWED].

### SCENARIO B — Post-70 CE (Temple destroyed)

#### IB-1. Religion — Reading the wilderness herald after the Temple's fall
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

If Mark was composed around 65–70 CE, its opening — a wilderness herald announcing that "the kingdom of God has drawn near" — would have been read amid or just after the catastrophe of the Jewish–Roman War. The Qumran community that had read Isaiah 40:3 in that same wilderness was destroyed by Rome during the war. For post-70 audiences, John's wilderness proclamation and the "good news" announced against imperial *euangelia* carried sharpened force: the kingdom was proclaimed near, the Temple has fallen, and the community must reckon with what nearness means on the far side of disaster.

**Source:** Marcus, J., *Mark 1–8* (Anchor Yale Bible), Yale, 2000, intro; Schürer, E., *History of the Jewish People*, vol. 1, T&T Clark, 1973.
