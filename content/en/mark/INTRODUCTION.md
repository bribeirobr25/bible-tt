## A. Overview

### A1. What the Gospel of Mark is
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The Gospel of Mark is the second book of the New Testament canon and the shortest of the four Gospels (16 chapters, approximately 670 verses). It moves quickly: the signature adverb *euthys* ("immediately") recurs dozens of times, stitching scenes into a rapid sequence of action. Mark has no genealogy and no birth narrative — it opens at the immersion of Yeshua (Jesus) by Yochanan (John) and drives toward the passion, which occupies a disproportionate share of the book ("a passion narrative with an extended introduction," in one well-known description).

Mark shares substantial material with Matthew and Luke — the three are the Synoptic Gospels (from Greek *syn* + *opsis*, "seen together"). Most scholars hold that Mark was written first and used as a source by the other two (Markan priority), though this is a reconstruction, not a textual statement. Mark is distinguished by its speed, its vivid concrete detail, its emphasis on Yeshua's deeds (especially exorcisms and healings), the "messianic secret" (Yeshua repeatedly silences testimony to his identity), and the consistent incomprehension of the disciples.

### A2. Place in the canon and among the Synoptics
**[HISTORICAL — PROBABLE]**

Mark is printed second but is widely regarded as the earliest of the four. Because Matthew and Luke appear to incorporate most of Mark while adding birth narratives, extended teaching, and resurrection appearances, Mark's distinctive material is comparatively small — which makes its particular emphases (the secret, the disciples' failure, the abrupt original ending at 16:8) stand out. The TT renders Mark on its own terms and does not harmonize its wording to the parallels (e.g. Mark's "kingdom of God," not Matthew's "kingdom of the skies").

## B. Authorship and Date

### B1. Traditional attribution
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

Early tradition (Papias, via Eusebius, early second century) associates the Gospel with "Mark," described as the interpreter of Peter who recorded Peter's recollections. The figure is often identified with the John Mark of Acts. The Gospel itself is anonymous; the attribution is traditional, not internal.

### B2. Date
**[HISTORICAL — PROBABLE]**

Mark is commonly dated to around 65–70 CE, near or around the time of the First Jewish–Roman War and the destruction of the Jerusalem Temple (70 CE) — partly on the basis of chapter 13. Both a slightly earlier and a slightly later date are defended. The TT takes no position; the date does not affect the translation.

## C. Audience and Purpose

### C1. A partly non-Jewish audience
**[STRONG INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**

Mark explains Jewish customs (e.g. handwashing practice, 7:3–4) and translates Aramaic expressions for readers (e.g. *talitha koum*, 5:41; *ephphatha*, 7:34), suggesting an audience not assumed to know them. Latin loanwords and administrative terms have been read as pointing toward a Roman setting, though this is debated.

### C2. Purpose
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The opening line states the purpose: "the good news of Yeshua the anointed one, son of God" (1:1, with the last phrase text-critically disputed). The Gospel presents who Yeshua is through what he does and what is done to him, culminating in the centurion's confession at the cross (15:39).

## D. Distinctive Features

### D1. The "messianic secret"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Repeatedly Yeshua silences testimony to his identity — demons (1:25,34; 3:12), the healed (1:44), and the disciples (8:30; 9:9). This recurring suppression is a structural feature of Mark unique in its intensity among the Gospels. The TT renders each instance plainly and does not explain the motive, which the text leaves open.

### D2. Speed, vividness, and the historical present
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

*Euthys* ("immediately") and frequent historical-present verbs ("he says," "they come") create urgency and immediacy. Mark also preserves vivid concrete details (the cushion in the boat, 4:38; the green grass, 6:39) often absent from the parallels. The TT preserves *euthys* at every occurrence and keeps the historical present where Mark uses it.

### D3. The disciples' incomprehension; the abrupt ending
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The disciples consistently fail to understand (4:13; 8:17–21). The earliest manuscripts end at 16:8 — the women flee the empty tomb "and said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid." The longer endings (16:9–20) are later additions, absent from the oldest witnesses; this is treated in the chapter notes where relevant (out of the chapter 1–3 scope).

## E. Sources and Composition

### E1. Markan priority (a scholarly reconstruction)
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**

The dominant scholarly model holds that Mark was the first written Gospel and a source for Matthew and Luke. This is an inference from patterns of agreement, not a claim the texts make about themselves. The TT notes it as background and does not let it govern any rendering.

---

## F. Later Reception

### F1. Reception across traditions
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

Long treated as an abbreviation of Matthew (Augustine), Mark was comparatively neglected until modern source criticism elevated it as the earliest Gospel. Its stark Christology, the secret motif, and the ending at 16:8 have been focal points of modern interpretation. These are documented post-biblical readings, recorded as reception, not as the text's own claims.

## G. Sources Consulted

- Nestle-Aland, *Novum Testamentum Graece*, 28th ed. (NA28), with critical apparatus — base text and variant data (e.g. 1:1 "son of God"; 1:2 "in Isaiah"; 1:41 "compassion"/"anger").
- A Greek–English Lexicon of the New Testament (BDAG) — lexical ranges for *euangelion*, *aphesis*, *exousia*, *splanchnizomai*, *lepra*.
- Standard reference grammars of New Testament Greek — for aspect, the historical present, and the periphrastic constructions characteristic of Mark.
- Septuagint (LXX) — for the wording of the composite citation (Mal 3:1 / Exod 23:20 / Isa 40:3) and the allusions at 1:11 (Ps 2:7; Isa 42:1; Gen 22:2).
- Hebrew Bible (BHS) — for the underlying *ruach*, *tsara'at*, *ha-satan*, and the Isaiah 40:3 divine name.
