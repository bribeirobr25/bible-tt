## A. Overview

### A1. What the Gospel of Mark is
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The Gospel of Mark is the second book of the New Testament canon and the shortest of the four Gospels (16 chapters, approximately 670 verses). It moves quickly: the signature adverb *euthys* ("immediately") recurs dozens of times, stitching scenes into a rapid sequence of action. Mark has no genealogy and no birth narrative — it opens at the immersion of Yeshua (Jesus) by Yochanan (John) and drives toward the passion, which occupies a disproportionate share of the book ("a passion narrative with an extended introduction," in one well-known description).

Mark is distinguished by its speed, its vivid concrete detail, its emphasis on Yeshua's deeds (especially exorcisms and healings), the "messianic secret" (Yeshua repeatedly silences testimony to his identity), and the consistent incomprehension of the disciples.

### A2. Place among the Synoptic Gospels
**[HISTORICAL — PROBABLE]**

Mark shares substantial material with Matthew and Luke — the three are the Synoptic Gospels (from Greek *syn* + *opsis*, "seen together"). Mark is printed second but is widely regarded as the earliest of the four. Because Matthew and Luke appear to incorporate most of Mark while adding birth narratives, extended teaching, and resurrection appearances, Mark's distinctive material is comparatively small — which makes its particular emphases (the secret, the disciples' failure, the abrupt original ending at 16:8) stand out. The TT renders Mark on its own terms and does not harmonize its wording to the parallels.

## B. Authorship and Composition

### B1. Traditional attribution
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

Early tradition (Papias, via Eusebius, early second century) associates the Gospel with "Mark," described as the interpreter of Peter who recorded Peter's recollections. The figure is often identified with the John Mark of Acts. The Gospel itself is **anonymous**; the attribution is traditional, not internal.

### B2. Markan priority (a scholarly reconstruction)
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**

The dominant scholarly model holds that Mark was the first written Gospel and a source for Matthew and Luke. This is an inference from patterns of agreement, not a claim the texts make about themselves. The TT notes it as background and does not let it govern any rendering.

## C. Dating

### C1. Date
**[HISTORICAL — PROBABLE]**

Mark is commonly dated to around 65–70 CE, near or around the time of the First Jewish–Roman War and the destruction of the Jerusalem Temple (70 CE) — partly on the basis of chapter 13. Both a slightly earlier and a slightly later date are defended. The TT takes no position; the date does not affect the translation.

## D. Historical Setting

### D1. A partly non-Jewish audience
**[STRONG INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**

Mark explains Jewish customs (e.g. handwashing practice, 7:3–4) and translates Aramaic expressions for readers (e.g. *talitha koum*, 5:41; *ephphatha*, 7:34), suggesting an audience not assumed to know them. Latin loanwords and administrative terms have been read as pointing toward a Roman setting, though this is debated.

### D2. Setting and circumstances
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE]**

The prominence of suffering, persecution, and the cross, together with chapter 13's discourse, has been read as addressing a community under pressure (often associated with the Jewish–Roman War or Neronian persecution). This is a reading of the emphases, not a stated setting; the TT records it as background only.

## E. Manuscript Transmission

### E1. The ending at 16:8
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The earliest and best manuscripts (א, B) end at **16:8** — the women flee the empty tomb "and said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid." The "longer ending" (16:9–20) and a "shorter ending" are later additions, absent from the oldest witnesses and marked or omitted in the critical editions. This is the single most significant text-critical fact about Mark. (It lies outside the chapter 1–3 scope but is noted here as background.)

### E2. Notable variants within chapters 1–3
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The opening chapters contain several variants that meet the RULES-GS significance threshold and are handled in the chapter notes:
- **1:1** — "son of God" (*huiou theou*) is **bracketed/disputed** in NA28 (absent from א*, Θ, 28; present in א¹, B, D, L, W).
- **1:2** — the citation is attributed "in Isaiah the prophet" (NA28) vs. the Byzantine "in the prophets," because the citation is **composite** (Malachi 3:1 / Exodus 23:20 + Isaiah 40:3).
- **1:41** — Yeshua "moved with compassion" (*splanchnistheis*, NA28) vs. "moved with anger" (*orgistheis*, Codex Bezae + Old Latin), the *lectio difficilior*.

## F. Reading Mark in the TT

### F1. "Kingdom of God" (not "kingdom of the skies")
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Mark uses βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ (*basileia tou theou*, "kingdom of God," 1:15 and throughout). Matthew's characteristic "kingdom of the skies" (*basileia tōn ouranōn*) does **not** appear in Mark. The TT renders each Gospel's actual wording and does not harmonize Mark to Matthew.

**Divine name (Rule 25 / GS Option C):** where *kyrios* renders YHWH in an OT quotation (e.g. 1:3, Isaiah 40:3), the TT reads "the Lord" in the main text, with the underlying YHWH referent noted in Tier 2.

### F4. Direct divine speech (Rule 30)
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The TT marks the direct speech of God and of Yeshua with a distinct colour. In Mark 1 this covers God's voice at the immersion (1:11, in the **second person** — "You are my son," contrast Matthew's third-person "This is") and Yeshua's sayings (1:15, 17, 25, 38, 41, 44). The speech of the unclean spirit (1:24), the narrator's OT citation (1:2–3), the crowd, the disciples, and the leper is **not** marked.

### F5. What to watch for in Mark
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

- **The messianic secret** — Yeshua repeatedly silences testimony to his identity (demons 1:25,34; the healed 1:44). The TT renders each instance plainly and does not explain the motive.
- **Speed and the historical present** — *euthys* and frequent present-tense verbs ("he says," "they come") create urgency; the TT preserves both.
- **Vivid concrete detail and forceful verbs** — e.g. the skies *torn* (not "opened," 1:10) and the Spirit *driving* Yeshua out (1:12); the TT keeps the force rather than smoothing it.
