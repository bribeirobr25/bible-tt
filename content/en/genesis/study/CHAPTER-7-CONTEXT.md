## A. Hebrew Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. "Seven seven" — distributive ambiguity preserved
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The TT renders שִׁבְעָה שִׁבְעָה (*shiv'ah shiv'ah*) as "seven seven" — copying how the Hebrew repeats the number. Most English translations smooth this to "seven pairs" (NIV, ESV) or "seven of each" (NRSV). But the Hebrew does not say "pairs." It simply repeats the number. Whether this means seven individual animals or seven pairs (fourteen) is genuinely ambiguous. The TT preserves the repetition and notes the ambiguity rather than resolving it.

### A2.  "Two from all" vs. "seven seven" — the central tension
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 6:19: "two from all you shall bring." Gen 7:2: "seven seven" of the clean, "two" of the unclean. Gen 7:9: "two two they came." The TT translates all three as the MT reads them, without harmonization (Rule 22). This is the most visible numerical tension in the flood narrative. Traditional harmonization reads 6:19 as a general statement refined by 7:2. Source-critical analysis assigns the passages to different documentary traditions. The TT presents the text without choosing between these frameworks.

### A3. Clean/unclean distinction — before Sinai
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The distinction between clean and unclean animals appears at 7:2 — centuries before the detailed laws of Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14. The text assumes the reader already knows the distinction or that it predates Moses. This is a textual feature the TT preserves without importing later Jewish legal categories.

### A4. Cosmological mechanism — Day 2 reversed
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 7:11: "all the fountains of the great deep burst open, and the windows of the sky were opened." This precisely reverses Gen 1:6–7, where the raqia (the solid dome of the sky) separated waters above from waters below. The flood dismantles the structure built on Day 2: the *tehom* (the deep, present since 1:2) breaks through from below, while the waters above the raqia pour down through sky-windows. The flood is not merely "a lot of rain." It is the undoing of creation's water-boundary.

### A5. "Standing thing" (*yequm*) — rare vocabulary
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 7:4,23: הַיְקוּם (*ha-yequm*) = "the standing thing" — everything that stands or exists. This word appears only here and at Deut 11:6 in the entire Hebrew Bible. Its rarity suggests it is either an old or a specialized term for "all living things on dry ground."

### A6. "Breath of the spirit of life" — triple compound
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 7:22: נִשְׁמַת רוּחַ חַיִּים (*nishmat ruach chayyim*) = "breath of the spirit/wind of life." This three-word combination appears nowhere else. It combines Gen 2:7 (*nishmat chayyim*, the breath of life blown into the first human) with Gen 6:17 (*ruach chayyim*, the spirit of life in all flesh). At the moment of total destruction, the text reaches for the fullest possible way to name what is being lost.

### A7. YHWH shuts the tebah — mid-verse name shift
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 7:16: "as God commanded him; and YHWH shut behind him." Both divine names appear in a single verse, doing different things. Elohim commands (cosmic authority); YHWH seals (personal protector). The act of closing the tebah is attributed to the personal, intimate divine name. The TT preserves both names exactly as the MT reads.

### A8. Obedience formula — four occurrences
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The phrase "Noach (Noah) did according to all that [God/YHWH] commanded" appears at 6:22, 7:5, 7:9, 7:16 — with alternating divine names. The repetition is a deliberate pattern. Noah's response to every command is silent obedience. He does not speak a recorded word until 9:25.

### A9. "A man and his woman" vs. "male and female" — two animal-pair idioms
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 7:2 uses **אִישׁ וְאִשְׁתּוֹ** (*ish ve-ishto*, "a man and his woman") — social/relational pair language — for the clean livestock. Gen 7:3 uses **זָכָר וּנְקֵבָה** (*zakhar u-neqevah*, "male and female") — biological-sex categories — for birds. Gen 7:9 and 7:16 use *zakhar u-neqevah* for the general animal entry. The Hebrew narrator uses two different idioms for two different animals in two different contexts. The TT renders each phrase as the MT reads it and does not harmonize them. Most translations use "male and female" throughout, collapsing the distinction. The social-pair idiom (*ish ve-ishto*) is particularly notable because it applies kinship/household language to animals — reflecting the clean-animal group's special status as sacrificially significant. The surplus of clean animals (seven of each) is preserved for sacrifice (8:20); the social-pair language may reflect their elevated ritual standing.

## B. Ancient Near Eastern Parallels

### B1. Mesopotamian flood — boarding and sealing
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

In Gilgamesh XI, Utnapishtim boards his vessel, and the craftsman Puzur-Amurri seals the door. In Atrahasis, the god Enki gives the boarding command. In Genesis, God/YHWH commands entry AND personally seals the vessel (7:16). The divine sealing is unique to the biblical account: no craftsman intermediary.

**Source:** George, A.R., *The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic*, Oxford, 2003, Tablet XI:93-96.

### B2. The flood mechanism — above and below
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

Mesopotamian flood traditions also describe water from multiple cosmic sources. Atrahasis and Gilgamesh reference storms, wind, and rising waters. But only Genesis specifies a precise cosmological mechanism: "fountains of the great deep" (subterranean) + "windows of the sky" (celestial), directly reversing the creation cosmology of Gen 1:6-7. The structural relationship between creation and de-creation is unique to the biblical text.

**Source:** Walton, J.H., *The Lost World of the Flood*, IVP Academic, 2018.

### B3. Duration — 7, 40, and 150
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The Gilgamesh flood lasts 7 days (Tablet XI:127-131). Genesis uses multiple overlapping durations: 7-day warning (7:4), 40-day rain (7:12), 150-day prevailing (7:24). The number 7 appears in both traditions; 40 and 150 are distinctive to the biblical account. Whether the different durations reflect different source traditions or a single complex chronology is debated.

**Source:** Finkel, I., *The Ark Before Noah*, Hodder & Stoughton, 2014.

### B4. Clean/unclean animals in ANE context
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — POSSIBLE]**

Mesopotamian flood traditions do not distinguish between clean and unclean animals aboard the vessel. The clean/unclean distinction in Gen 7:2 reflects a specifically Israelite ritual category. Some scholars argue this points to a later addition to the flood narrative. Others argue the distinction is pre-Mosaic and independent. The TT does not adjudicate.

**Source:** Milgrom, J., *Leviticus 1-16*, Anchor Bible Commentary, 1991.

### B5. The Eridu Genesis (Ziusudra) — the oldest flood tradition
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

The oldest surviving flood narrative is the Sumerian Eridu Genesis (c. 1600 BCE for the extant tablet, originating earlier). Ziusudra ("Life of Long Days") is the Sumerian flood hero, corresponding to the Akkadian Utnapishtim ("he found life") and Atrahasis ("exceeding wise"). Key features: the flood lasts seven days (matching Gen 7:4,10); after the flood Utu (the sun) appears and Ziusudra opens a window and offers sacrifice — structurally parallel to Noach (Noah) opening the *challon* (8:6) and building the altar (8:20); Ziusudra is taken to live forever in Dilmun (the Sumerian paradise) — Genesis gives Noah no immortality; he dies at 950. These parallels show a shared ancient flood tradition. The direction of influence and how the stories traveled between cultures are debated. The TT does not adjudicate.

**Source:** Jacobsen, T., *The Harps That Once: Sumerian Poetry in Translation*, Yale, 1987; ETCSL, University of Oxford, "The Flood Story" (1.7.4).

### B6. Atrahasis — closer parallel than Gilgamesh
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

Recent scholarship argues that Genesis is more directly related to the Atrahasis tradition than to Gilgamesh. The evidence: (1) both Atrahasis and Genesis place the flood a few generations after creation, while Gilgamesh embeds the flood in a quest-for-immortality story; (2) both use third-person narration, while Gilgamesh uses first-person retrospective; (3) in Atrahasis (3.1.37) the flood is announced "for the seventh night," directly paralleling Gen 7:4 ("in seven more days"); (4) Atrahasis covers creation, population growth, divine frustration, then flood, the same broad sequence as Genesis 1-9. The current companions' framing of Gilgamesh as the primary parallel should be understood as the more famous but not the closest textual relationship.

**Source:** Lambert, W.G. & Millard, A.R., *Atra-ḫasīs: The Babylonian Story of the Flood*, Oxford, 1969; Hendel, R.S., *The Text of Genesis 1–11*, Oxford, 1998.

## C. Historical and Archaeological Context

### C1. Flood chronology and ancient calendars
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The flood narrative's precise chronology (month/day markers at 7:11, 8:4, 8:5, 8:13, 8:14) totals approximately one solar year (370-371 days). Some scholars argue this reflects a 364-day calendar (known from the ancient Jewish texts Jubilees and the Qumran scrolls) adjusted to a solar year. Others see it as a literary framework. The precision itself is a textual feature regardless of which calendar system is used.

**Source:** Cassuto, U., *A Commentary on the Book of Genesis*, vol. 2, 1964, pp. 50-55.

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. *Arubbot ha-shamayim* — windows of the sky
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

אֲרֻבֹּת הַשָּׁמַיִם = "windows/sluices of the sky." The noun *arubbah* means a latticed opening or sluice-gate. It implies an architectural feature in the sky, not a metaphor but a cosmological model in which the raqia is a solid structure with openings. The same phrase appears at 2 Kgs 7:2,19 and Mal 3:10 ("windows of the sky" for abundant rain/blessing).

### D2. *Ma'yenot tehom rabbah* — fountains of the great deep
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

מַעְיְנֹת תְּהוֹם רַבָּה = "fountains/springs of the great deep." *Tehom* is the primordial deep from Gen 1:2, related to the Akkadian *Tiamat* (though not derived from it). *Rabbah* = "great/abundant." The subterranean waters are not ordinary groundwater. They are the primordial *tehom* beneath the earth's surface, held back since creation.

**Source:** Tsumura, D.T., *Creation and Destruction*, Eisenbrauns, 2005. Tsumura demonstrates that *tehom* is a common Semitic word for "deep waters," not a personified deity borrowed from Babylonian *Tiamat*. Genesis has demythologized the concept.

### D3. Clean/unclean as pre-Mosaic cultic category
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The distinction between "clean" (*tahor*) and "unclean" animals appears at Gen 7:2, long before the detailed rules of Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14. The text treats this as an already-known category, not as a new revelation. At this stage the purpose is sacrificial rather than dietary: clean animals are needed for the burnt offerings Noach (Noah) will make after the flood (8:20). This explains the numerical difference: "seven seven" of clean animals provides the surplus needed for sacrifice while preserving breeding pairs. The TT notes the distinction without importing later halakhic categories.

**Source:** Milgrom, J., *Leviticus 1-16*, Anchor Bible Commentary, 1991, pp. 718-736.

## E. Scientific Correspondence and Non-Correspondence

### E1. The flood mechanism and geological observation
**[SCIENTIFIC COMPARISON — POSSIBLE]**

Genesis 7:11 describes the flood as coming from two sources simultaneously: "all the fountains of the great deep (*tehom rabbah*) burst open, and the windows of the skies were opened." This is a cosmological reversal — the waters separated on Day 2 (Gen 1:6–7) reunite. The text presents this as a total event: waters cover "all the high mountains under all the skies" (7:19), with specific dates (second month, seventeenth day). Modern geology does not find evidence of a global flood in the stratigraphic record. There is no single depositional layer covering all continents from one event. However, significant regional flood events in Mesopotamia are well-attested archaeologically (see Chapter 6, C1). The text's cosmological mechanism (waters above + waters below reuniting) reflects the three-tier cosmology described in Gen 1, not a water-cycle model. What the text describes is a theological narrative of un-creation. What geology observes is evidence for devastating regional floods that likely gave rise to the flood tradition. Neither discipline answers the other's question.

### E2. The tebah's dimensions — naval architecture
**[SCIENTIFIC COMPARISON — POSSIBLE]**

Genesis 6:15 gives the tebah's dimensions: 300 cubits long, 50 wide, 30 high (approximately 137 x 23 x 14 meters using the standard cubit of ~45.7 cm). The length-to-beam ratio of 6:1 is within the range used by modern cargo vessels for stability. A 1993 study by the Korea Research Institute of Ships and Ocean Engineering (KRISO) found the proportions viable for flotation stability in open water, though not for self-propulsion — consistent with the text, which includes no sail, rudder, or steering mechanism. The word *tebah* itself means "box" or "chest" (see Chapter 6, A9), not "ship." The text describes a container designed to float and preserve, not to steer. Whether these proportions reflect ancient engineering knowledge, literary convention, or something else is not resolved by the text.

**Source:** Hong, S.W., et al., "Safety Investigation of Noah's Ark in a Seaway," *Journal of Creation* 8:1, 1994.

### E3. The "vapor canopy" hypothesis
**[SCIENTIFIC COMPARISON — SPECULATIVE]**

Some young-earth creationist popularizers have proposed that a "vapor canopy" — a layer of water, vapor, or ice above the pre-flood atmosphere — created higher atmospheric pressure and oxygen levels, causing giant animal sizes and human longevity, and that the flood occurred when this canopy collapsed. The hypothesis is rejected by mainstream science (a vapor canopy of the required size would cause a runaway greenhouse effect and toxic atmospheric pressure). It is also rejected by mainstream young-earth creationism: Answers in Genesis, the largest YEC organization, has explicitly disavowed it on physical grounds. The Carboniferous-era data sometimes cited (oxygen levels reaching ~35%, giant arthropods with wingspans up to 71 cm) is genuine, but those measurements are from ~300 million years ago in mainstream geologic chronology, not from any biblical-flood timeframe.

**Source:** Faulkner, D., "Did the Pre-Flood World Have a Vapor Canopy?", Answers in Genesis (ACADEMIC POPULAR, denominational refutation); Beerling, D., *The Emerald Planet*, Oxford University Press, 2007 (PEER-REVIEWED, on Carboniferous oxygen).

## F. Later Reception

### F1. Jewish reception — the righteous remnant
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

Rabbinic tradition (Genesis Rabbah 32:8) debates whether Noach (Noah) was righteous in an absolute sense or only relative to his corrupt generation. The phrase "in his generations" (6:9) is read as either praise (righteous even in a wicked age) or as a qualification (righteous only by comparison). The TT translates the phrase without adjudicating.

Midrash emphasizes the 7-day waiting period (7:4) as a week of mourning: God waited seven days for possible repentance before sending the flood.

**Source:** Freedman, H. & Simon, M. (trans.), *Midrash Rabbah: Genesis*, Soncino Press, 1939.

### F2. Christian reception — typological readings
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

1 Peter 3:20-21 reads the flood waters as a type of baptism. Early Church Fathers' interpretation (Augustine, *City of God* XV.26-27) reads the tebah as a type of the Church. The number of people saved (eight) is connected to resurrection symbolism (the eighth day as a symbol of new creation). These readings are post-biblical and not in the Hebrew text.

**Source:** Augustine, *City of God*, Book XV, chs. 26-27.

### F3. Islamic reception — Nuh in the Quran
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The Quran (Surahs 11:25-49, 23:23-30, 71:1-28) presents Nuh as a prophet who preaches to his people before the flood. A key difference: in the Quranic account, one of Nuh's sons refuses to board and drowns (11:42-43). The Genesis text records no such refusal. All three sons enter the tebah.

**Source:** Tottoli, R., *Biblical Prophets in the Qur'an and Muslim Literature*, 2002.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. Did fish survive?
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 7:21-22 specifies destruction of "all flesh that moves **upon the land**" and "everything which had breath of the spirit of life in its nostrils, from all which was on the **dry ground**." Fish and marine creatures are conspicuously absent from the destruction list. The flood destroys land-based life; the waters themselves are the instrument, not the target. Aquatic life appears to survive by omission.

### G2. "Two two" after "seven seven" — execution vs. instruction
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

At 7:2, YHWH instructs "seven seven" of clean animals. At 7:9, the execution describes "two two" entering. This could mean: (1) the "two two" is a summary description of the boarding process; (2) only two of each kind actually entered, with the "seven seven" instruction from a different tradition; (3) the "two two" refers to the unclean animals specifically. The TT translates both without reconciliation.

### G3. The date — second month, seventeenth day
**[TEXTUAL — POSSIBLE]**

Gen 7:11: the flood begins on Month 2, Day 17. Gen 8:4: the tebah rests on Month 7, Day 17 — exactly 150 days later (5 months × 30 days). Gen 8:14: the earth is fully dry on Month 2, Day 27 — almost exactly one year after the flood began. The symmetry suggests deliberate calendrical design, though whether this reflects literary artistry or historical precision is UNCERTAIN.

### G4. The flood chronology — calendrical precision
**[TEXTUAL — PROBABLE]**

The flood narrative provides exact calendar dates: onset on the second month, seventeenth day (7:11); waters prevail 150 days (7:24); ark rests on mountains of Ararat on the seventh month, seventeenth day (8:4); mountaintops visible on the tenth month, first day (8:5); earth dry on the second month, twenty-seventh day of Noah's 601st year (8:14). The total duration from onset to dry earth spans exactly one solar year plus ten days — or precisely one lunar year (354 days) plus one additional month, depending on the calendar system used. This level of calendrical precision in an ancient narrative raises questions about timekeeping: whether the flood chronology uses a solar calendar (365 days), a lunar calendar (354 days), or a schematic 360-day calendar (found in Mesopotamia and in the later Book of Jubilees) is debated. The precision itself is the textual feature. The narrative is not vague about time.

## I. The World at the Time

The entries below address the historical circumstances specifically foregrounded in Genesis 7 — the flood mechanism, measurement systems, animal management, water cosmology, and calendrical precision.

### Scenario A: If composed during the Mosaic period (~13th c. BCE)
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — DOCUMENTED]** *(Traditional attribution)*

**I-A1. Water cosmology in the Late Bronze Age**
Genesis 7:11's flood mechanism — "all the fountains of the great deep burst open, and the windows of the sky were opened" — reflects a three-tier cosmological model: waters above the sky-dome, the inhabitable earth, and subterranean waters below. This model was shared across the ancient Near East. Egyptian cosmology depicted a sky-goddess arching over the earth with waters above and below; Mesopotamian cosmology had the ocean-deep (*Apsu*) below and a solid heavenly vault above. In a Mosaic-period context, the flood's description would invoke a cosmological structure the audience already held.

**I-A2. Cubit measurements and their variation**
Genesis 6:15 gives the tebah's dimensions in cubits. The cubit (the length from elbow to fingertip) varied by culture and period: the standard Egyptian cubit was approximately 45.7 cm; the royal cubit approximately 52.5 cm; Mesopotamian cubits also varied. By the 13th century BCE, standardized cubit measurements were in use for monumental construction across Egypt and the Levant. The text's use of cubits situates the account in a world of practical measurement and organized building.

**I-A3. Animal management and large herds**
The instruction to bring animals "two from all" or "seven seven" of the clean (Gen 7:2-3) assumes familiarity with large-scale animal husbandry. The Late Bronze Age Levant maintained herds of cattle, sheep, goats, and donkeys as primary sources of food, wool, and traction. Egyptian administrative papyri record palace stables with hundreds of horses and herds of cattle. The management of multiple animal species aboard a single large vessel would be understood against this background of estate-level animal management, even if the scale in Genesis exceeds anything attested.

**I-A4. Seven-day periods and lunar reckoning**
The seven-day waiting period before the flood (7:4, 7:10) and the flood's multiple time-markers reflect a world organized by seven-day cycles and lunar months. The Mesopotamian calendar already used a seven-day structure with the seventh day as significant. Egyptian lunar calendars tracked monthly cycles. The calendrical precision of Genesis 7 — month and day markers — reflects administrative timekeeping practices familiar across the ancient Near East.

### Scenario B: If composed during the Monarchic period (~10th-9th c. BCE)
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — POSSIBLE]**

**I-B1. Flood experience in Mesopotamia and the Levant**
The Tigris and Euphrates regularly flooded, sometimes catastrophically. Archaeological flood layers at Shuruppak and Kish (c. 2900-2800 BCE) correspond to the period when Mesopotamian flood traditions were taking literary form. In the Levant, winter rains and flash floods in wadis were annual events. The Jordan River's seasonal flooding threatened Jordan Valley settlements. An audience in the monarchic period would understand the flood narrative against personal and cultural knowledge of what rising waters actually did to settlements, crops, and livestock.

**I-B2. Clean/unclean distinctions in pre-exilic Israel**
The distinction between clean and unclean animals (7:2) appearing before Sinai suggests a pre-Mosaic cultic category. In the monarchic period, sacrificial practice was active at multiple sites — Jerusalem, Bethel, Dan, and rural high places. Priests and worshippers would know immediately which animals were acceptable for sacrifice. The "seven seven" of clean animals in the flood account would be understood as providing the surplus needed for post-flood sacrifice, consistent with everyday cultic practice.

**I-B3. Divine sealing as unique protection**
Genesis 7:16's statement that "YHWH shut behind him" — God personally sealing the tebah — has no parallel in Mesopotamian flood accounts, where a craftsman or gate-keeper performs the sealing. In a monarchic context where YHWH's personal involvement in Israel's protection was a live theological claim (YHWH as warrior, protector of the king, guardian of Jerusalem), the detail that YHWH himself closes the door carries the weight of divine personal commitment to the covenant survivor.

### Scenario C: If composed/finalized during the Exilic/Post-Exilic period (~6th-5th c. BCE)
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]** *(Scholarly consensus for final form)*

**I-C1. Mesopotamian flood experience as lived context**
Jewish exiles in Babylon lived in a river civilization defined by flooding. The Euphrates and Tigris could rise dramatically with spring snowmelt from the Taurus and Zagros mountains. Babylonian cities maintained dike systems, canal networks, and flood-management infrastructure. An exilic audience reading Genesis 7 would understand the flood's mechanism — "fountains of the deep" + "windows of the sky" — against direct experience of a flood-prone environment and the cosmological language their neighbors used to explain it.

**I-C2. Babylonian measurement systems**
Babylonian scribal education included systematic training in measurement: the cubit (*ammatu*), the reed (six cubits), and larger units for field measurement. Astronomical calculation used the same base-60 system for time and distance. The flood chronology's precision (month and day markers, 150-day periods corresponding to five 30-day months) reflects exactly the 30-day administrative months and 360-day schematic calendar used in Babylonian administrative practice. The exilic community would read this precision against their knowledge of Babylonian timekeeping.

**I-C3. Cosmological de-creation and re-creation**
The flood's mechanism in Genesis 7:11 — fountains of the deep + windows of the sky — precisely reverses Genesis 1:6-7's Day 2 separation. In an exilic context, where the destruction of Jerusalem could be experienced as cosmic de-creation (the temple gone, the holy city burned, the covenant community scattered), the flood narrative's structure — de-creation followed by re-creation — would carry immediate theological resonance. The exiles lived in the aftermath of their own world's undoing.

**I-C4. Animal preservation and the continuity of creation**
The emphasis on preserving every species — "two from all," clean and unclean — reflects a theology of creation's inherent worth. In Babylonian flood accounts, animals board the vessel primarily as food supply. Genesis 7 presents their preservation as a divine imperative: the categories and diversity of creation must survive. In an exilic community anxious about whether their identity, their practices, and their God's purposes would survive, the image of divine care for every creature's continuity carried specific weight.

---

### Scenario D: If redacted during the Persian/early-Hellenistic period (~4th-3rd c. BCE)
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — POSSIBLE]** *(Associated with final Pentateuchal shaping)*

**I-D1. Comparative flood chronology and calendar disputes**
By the 4th-3rd centuries BCE, different Jewish groups used different calendars: the lunar calendar standard in Babylon, the 364-day calendar reflected in Jubilees and 1 Enoch, and the solar calendar that may underlie the flood chronology. The flood narrative's precise dates (Gen 7:11, 8:4, 8:5, 8:13, 8:14) were apparently read differently depending on which calendar system one used. The precision itself may have been designed to anchor the flood narrative within a specific calendrical tradition — a question alive in the scribal communities of this period.

**I-D2. Universal flood traditions in a Hellenistic comparative context**
Berossus's *Babyloniaca* (c. 278 BCE) and the Greek Deucalion tradition were circulating simultaneously with the Torah. In a Hellenistic context where flood narratives were recognized as a cross-cultural genre, Genesis 7's distinctive features — cosmological mechanism, precise dates, divine sealing — would be read as marking the Israelite tradition's theological claims against the comparative backdrop. The question "whose flood account is authoritative?" was live in a world where multiple traditions competed.

**I-D3. Water cosmology in late antique Jewish thought**
The three-tier cosmological model (waters above, earth, waters below) was receiving increasing theological attention in late Second Temple Jewish literature. The *Book of Jubilees* (~2nd c. BCE) retells the flood with the same cosmological assumptions. *1 Enoch*'s astronomical sections describe the windows of heaven as physical sluice-gates. The Genesis 7 account's cosmological language — *tehom rabbah*, *arubbot ha-shamayim* — was not simply inherited text but living cosmological vocabulary in active use.
