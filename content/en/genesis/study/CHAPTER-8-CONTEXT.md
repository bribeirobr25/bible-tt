## A. Hebrew Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. *Ruach* over the waters — re-creation begins
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 8:1: "God caused a *ruach* to pass over the land." Direct structural echo of Gen 1:2: "*ruach* of God hovering over the face of the waters." The TT preserves the slash (wind/spirit) per Rule 2. The re-creation after the flood begins with the same element as the original creation — *ruach* over water. The flood narrative is framed as de-creation (ch.7) followed by re-creation (ch.8).

### A2. "God remembered" — not forgetting but acting
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

וַיִּזְכֹּר אֱלֹהִים (*vayyizkor elohim*) = "And God remembered." In Hebrew, divine "remembering" (*zakhar*) does not imply prior forgetting. It means God turning his attention to act on someone's behalf. Same verb at Gen 19:29 (God "remembers" Abraham and saves Lot), Gen 30:22 (God "remembers" Rachel), Exod 2:24 (God "remembers" his covenant). The verb *zakhar* in these divine uses is consistently performative: to remember is to act. In Gen 9:15-16, the bow functions as a divine mnemonic: God sees the bow → remembers → restrains. The divine self-reminder (8:21 "said in his heart"; 9:16 "I will see it to remember") has no direct ANE parallel. The TT renders "remembered" literally.

### A3. Fountains and windows — closed, not destroyed
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 7:11: fountains "burst open" (*nivqe'u*), windows "opened" (*niftchu*). Gen 8:2: fountains and windows "were stopped" (*vayyissakru*). The cosmic water-sources are stopped (sealed again), not eliminated. The raqia architecture remains in place; the Day 2 separation is re-established. The cosmological model of Gen 1 continues to operate after the flood.

### A4. The tebah "rested" (*tanach*) — n-w-ch root
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 8:4: וַתָּנַח הַתֵּבָה (*vattanach ha-tebah*) = "the tebah rested." Root: נ-ו-ח (*n-w-ch*), the same root as Noach (Noah)'s name. The tebah "Noach-ed" on the mountains. This is the second n-w-ch occurrence in the flood narrative, following the name itself.

### A5. N-W-CH root saturation — four occurrences
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The root n-w-ch appears four times in Genesis 8:
1. **Noach** (the name — "rest/comfort")
2. **8:4** — the tebah *tanach* (rested) on Ararat
3. **8:9** — the dove finds no *manoach* (resting place)
4. **8:21** — YHWH smells the *nichoach* (pleasing/soothing aroma)

This concentration is unique. The man named "rest" is the one whose vessel rests, whose dove seeks rest, and whose sacrifice produces a "resting" aroma. The TT exposes this by transliterating consistently and flagging the root connections.

### A6. Two verbs for "dried" — surface vs. complete
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 8:13: חָרְבוּ (*charvu*) = surface-dried (from *charav*). Gen 8:14: יָבְשָׁה (*yavshah*) = fully dried (from *yavesh*). Two different Hebrew verbs describe two stages of drying: the surface water is gone (8:13, Month 1 Day 1), then the ground is thoroughly dry (8:14, Month 2 Day 27). The TT does not conflate these. Both are rendered as "dried," but the cross-chapter notes distinguish them.

### A7. "Be fruitful and multiply" — Gen 1:22 repeated
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 8:17: "be fruitful and multiply upon the land." Identical language to Gen 1:22 (blessing to creatures) and 1:28 (blessing to humans). The re-creation is made explicit. The post-flood world receives the same generative blessing as the first creation.

### A8. Same diagnosis, opposite conclusion — 6:5 vs 8:21
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 6:5: "every inclination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil all the day" → flood (judgment). Gen 8:21: "the inclination of the heart of the human is evil from his youth" → "I will not again curse the ground" (mercy). The 8:21 version drops three intensifiers (*kol/every*, *raq/only*, *kol ha-yom/all the day*) and adds "from his youth" (*mine'urav*). The human condition is essentially the same, but softer language leads to the opposite divine response. The TT preserves both verses with exact fidelity to the Hebrew.

The word *yetser* (inclination) itself comes from the root י-צ-ר (*y-ts-r*, "to form/fashion"), the same root used in Gen 2:7 when God "formed" (*vayyitser*) the human from the *adamah*. The human's inclination is, at its root, something *shaped*. In the Hebrew Bible *yetser* is a neutral term for "thought/plan" (Ps 103:14; Isa 26:3). The later rabbinic concept *yetzer hara* (evil inclination), distinguished from *yetzer hatov* (good inclination), is a post-biblical development, labeled here as reception, not as a meaning found in Genesis itself.

### A9. The macro-chiasm of Gen 6:9–9:29
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The flood narrative is organized as a large-scale chiasm with "God remembered Noach (Noah)" (8:1) as the precise structural turning point. This literary structure, documented by Wenham and Radday, shows that every unit before 8:1 mirrors a corresponding unit after it:

| Before 8:1 | Pivot | After 8:1 |
|---|---|---|
| Divine speech of judgment (6:13–21) | **God remembered Noah (8:1)** | Divine speech of commitment (8:21–22; 9:1–17) |
| "It grieved him to his heart" (6:6) | | "YHWH said in his heart" (8:21) |
| Covenant announced, no terms (6:18) | | Covenant established with terms (9:9–17) |
| "Go into the tebah" (7:1) | | "Go out from the tebah" (8:16) |
| Fountains burst open (7:11) | | Fountains stopped (8:2) |
| Waters rise (7:17–24) | | Waters recede (8:1–5) |
| 7-day waiting before flood (7:4,10) | | 7-day waiting for dove (8:10,12) |

The chiasm shows that 8:1 is not merely a narrative transition but the structural and theological center of the entire flood unit. God's "remembering" is the turning point where destruction reverses into restoration. The chiasm is a literary-structural observation; it neither confirms nor denies single authorship.

**Source:** Wenham, G.J., "The Coherence of the Flood Narrative," *VT* 28 (1978), 336-348; Radday, Y.T., "Chiasm in Joshua, Judges, and Others," *Linguistica Biblica* 3 (1973).

## B. Ancient Near Eastern Parallels

### B1. Bird-sending — Gilgamesh vs Genesis
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

Gilgamesh XI:145-154: Utnapishtim sends out a dove (returns), a swallow (returns), a raven (does not return — finds food). Genesis 8:7-12: Noach (Noah) sends a raven (goes back and forth), then a dove three times (returns empty → returns with olive leaf → does not return).

Key differences: (1) reversed bird order; (2) Genesis has three dove-missions with progressive results; (3) in Gilgamesh, the raven's non-return signals success, while in Genesis, the raven's behavior is inconclusive. The three-stage dove sequence in Genesis is structurally more developed.

**Source:** George, A.R., *The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic*, Oxford, 2003, Tablet XI:145-154.

### B2. Post-flood sacrifice — divine response compared
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

Gilgamesh XI:159-161: "The gods smelled the savor, the gods smelled the sweet savor, the gods gathered like flies over the sacrifice." Genesis 8:21: "YHWH smelled the pleasing aroma (*reach ha-nichoach*)."

The contrast is striking: multiple gods gathering "like flies" (hungry, undignified) versus a single deity responding with internal resolve (dignified, purposeful). In Gilgamesh, the gods regret the flood because it nearly cut off their food supply of human sacrifices. In Genesis, YHWH resolves never again to curse the ground, a moral commitment, not a practical calculation.

**Source:** George, A.R., *The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic*, Oxford, 2003, Tablet XI:159-164.

### B3. Ararat — the kingdom of Urartu
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

"Mountains of Ararat" (8:4) corresponds to the ancient kingdom of Urartu, a major power in eastern Anatolia/Armenia (c. 860-590 BCE). The Hebrew אֲרָרָט (*Ararat*) = Akkadian *Urartu*. The text says "mountains" (plural), meaning a mountain range, not a single peak. The identification with the modern "Mount Ararat" (Ağrı Dağı, 5,137 m) postdates the biblical text.

**Source:** Zimansky, P., *Ecology and Empire: The Structure of the Urartian State*, 1985.

### B4. Flood hero character comparison
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

| Feature | Ziusudra (Sumerian) | Utnapishtim (Akkadian) | Atrahasis | Noach (Noah) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| How warned | Divine vision | God speaks through wall | God speaks directly | God speaks directly |
| Role | King and priest | King | Wise man | Righteous man (*tsaddiq*) |
| Post-flood fate | Eternal life in Dilmun | Eternal life at world's edge | Unclear | Mortal — dies at 950 |
| Post-flood speech | Worship of sun god | Narrative to Gilgamesh | None recorded | Curse on Kenaan (Canaan) (9:25) |
| Name meaning | "Life of Long Days" | "He found life" | "Exceeding wise" | "Rest/comfort" (*n-w-ch*) |

Genesis is distinctive: Noah does not achieve immortality, his name is rooted in human experience rather than a divine attribute, and his only recorded speech is a curse, not praise. The TT's observation that Noah's silence (6:9–9:24) and his first speech (9:25) are structurally deliberate is reinforced by this comparative context.

**Source:** George, A.R., *The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic*, Oxford, 2003; Jacobsen, T., *The Harps That Once*, Yale, 1987.

## C. Historical and Archaeological Context

### C1. The olive leaf — botanical evidence
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The olive (*zayit*) is native to the eastern Mediterranean and thrives in the Levant. The detail of a "freshly plucked olive leaf" (8:11) locates the narrative in a Mediterranean ecological zone. Olive trees can survive extended flooding — they regenerate from rootstock even after submersion. The detail is botanically plausible for a regional flood in the Near East.

**Source:** Zohary, D. & Hopf, M., *Domestication of Plants in the Old World*, Oxford, 3rd ed., 2000.

### C2. First altar — *mizbeach* in archaeological context
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Gen 8:20: Noach (Noah) builds the first *mizbeach* (altar) in the biblical narrative. The earliest archaeological evidence of structured sacrificial installations in the Near East dates to the Pre-Pottery Neolithic (c. 9000-7000 BCE). Open-air altars made of unhewn stone are the simplest and oldest form.

**Source:** Mazar, A., *Archaeology of the Land of the Bible: 10,000–586 B.C.E.*, Doubleday, 1990.

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. *Nichoach* — the "resting" aroma
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 8:21: רֵיחַ הַנִּיחֹחַ (*reach ha-nichoach*). The word *nichoach* comes from root נ-ו-ח, the same root as Noach (Noah)'s name. The pleasing/soothing aroma is literally a "resting" aroma. The sacrifice of Noah produces a *Noach-aroma*. This wordplay is invisible in most translations that render "pleasing aroma" without connecting it to the protagonist's name.

### D2. *Manoach* — where the dove cannot rest
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 8:9: "the dove did not find a *manoach* for the sole of her foot." *Manoach* = resting place, from root n-w-ch. The dove seeks a "Noach-place" and cannot find one. The wordplay is layered: the man named Rest (*Noach*) sends a bird that cannot find rest (*manoach*) in a world where the vessel has rested (*tanach*) but the ground has not yet dried.

## E. Scientific Correspondence and Non-Correspondence

### E1. Post-flood ecological recovery — the olive leaf
**[SCIENTIFIC COMPARISON — POSSIBLE]**

Genesis 8:11 states the dove returned with "a freshly plucked olive leaf (*aleh zayit taraf*) in its mouth." In post-catastrophic ecology, vegetation recovery follows a predictable succession: pioneer species (grasses, weeds) appear first, followed by shrubs, then trees. Olive trees (*Olea europaea*) are notably resilient. They can regrow from their root systems even after severe damage, including flooding, and produce new shoots within months. The olive is also salt-tolerant. If the narrative reflects regional Mesopotamian flooding, the olive leaf is a botanically plausible early sign of recovery. The text uses the olive leaf as a sign that "the waters had lightened from upon the land" (8:11), a narrative marker, not a botanical report. The overlap with olive biology is real but beside the point of what the text is doing.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. The raven — what happened?
**[TEXTUAL — POSSIBLE]**

Gen 8:7: the raven "went out, going out and returning, until the waters dried from upon the land." What does "going out and returning" mean? (1) It flew back and forth without landing — circling but never settling; (2) it flew out and came back to the tebah repeatedly; (3) it left and did not return to the tebah — "returning" refers to the waters returning/receding. The Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous. The raven produces no useful information; it is the dove that reveals the state of the land.

### G2. Why the olive leaf?
**[TEXTUAL — POSSIBLE]**

The olive leaf (8:11) has become an enduring symbol of peace. But the text does not attribute this meaning. In context, the olive leaf proves: (1) the waters have receded enough for lowland vegetation to be exposed; (2) living plant material survives — the biological world is recovering. The peace symbolism is a later cultural accretion, not present in the Hebrew text.

### G3. "Said in his heart" — divine interiority
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Gen 8:21: "YHWH said in his heart" (*el-libbo*). This is internal divine speech: God resolving within himself, not speaking to Noach (Noah). The human reader is given access to God's inner thought. This resolves privately; the public covenant follows in Gen 9. The internal resolve precedes and grounds the external commitment.

Divine interior monologue (God thinking to himself in the narrative) is a recognized literary convention in ANE texts. In the Egyptian Memphite Theology, the god Ptah creates through thought "in the heart" before verbal command. The technique portrays divine decision-making as deliberate and reasoned, not impulsive. The TT renders it literally, preserving the human-like but dignified portrayal.

### G4. The flood as exactly one year
**[TEXTUAL — PROBABLE]**

The flood begins on Month 2, Day 17 (7:11) and the earth is fully dry on Month 2, Day 27 (8:14) — roughly one solar year (370-371 days). Some scholars argue this exactly fits a 364-day calendar (known from the ancient Jewish texts Jubilees and 1 Enoch) plus adjustments. Whether the chronology is historical, literary, or liturgical, the near-exact-year framing is a structural feature of the narrative.

### G5. "Never again" — divine self-limitation as an ethical concept
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**

Genesis 8:21-22 presents YHWH making an internal commitment ("said in his heart") never again to curse the ground or strike all life because of humanity. The stated reason is startling: "for the inclination of the human heart is evil from his youth." The same diagnosis that prompted destruction (6:5) now prompts restraint. This is a case of divine self-limitation: God voluntarily holds back from future destruction, accepting the flawed human condition rather than repeatedly wiping the slate clean. Can God change strategy? Does this represent learning, compassion, or sovereign freedom? The text does not resolve these questions. It presents the reversal ("evil inclination" as reason to destroy becomes "evil inclination" as reason to hold back) and lets the paradox stand.

### G6. Raven and dove as scouts — ornithological behavior
**[SCIENTIFIC COMPARISON — POSSIBLE]**

Genesis 8:6–12 sends two birds: the raven (*orev*) "went out, going and returning" (8:7) without resolution; the dove (*yonah*) returns finding no rest (8:8–9), then returns with an olive leaf (8:10–11), then does not return (8:12). In ornithology, ravens (*Corvus corax*) are omnivorous scavengers that can feed on floating carrion and debris, so they can survive without dry land. Doves (*Columba*) eat seeds and grain, and need dry ground with vegetation for food. The different behavior of each bird (the raven sustaining itself over water, the dove needing land) matches their actual ways of life. Ancient Near Eastern parallels show similar bird-sending motifs (Gilgamesh XI sends dove, swallow, raven), suggesting a shared narrative tradition. Whether the Genesis author knew bird ecology empirically, inherited the motif from tradition, or both, the text selects birds whose real behavior matches their narrative function.

### Scenario A: If composed during the Mosaic period (~13th c. BCE) — *Traditional attribution*
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — DOCUMENTED]**

#### I-A1. Bird augury in the Late Bronze Age
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — DOCUMENTED]**
Sending birds to determine conditions was a recognized practice across the ancient Near East. Mesopotamian omen texts included bird augury — observing the flight, behavior, and return of birds as signs of divine communication. Egyptian and Hittite ritual texts also used bird behavior as divinatory indicators. The raven and dove sequence in Genesis 8:6-12 would be immediately intelligible to a Late Bronze Age audience as a method of environmental reconnaissance — one the text depicts as practical observation, not ritual divination.

#### I-A2. Sacrifice and altar-building after a journey
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — DOCUMENTED]**
In the Late Bronze Age, offering sacrifice at the conclusion of a significant journey, military campaign, or deliverance was standard practice. Egyptian rulers offered to Amun after victories; Hittite kings sacrificed after treaty confirmations; Canaanite texts (the Baal Cycle) include post-victory feasts and offerings. Noach (Noah)'s first act after leaving the tebah — building an altar and offering burnt offerings of every clean animal and bird (8:20) — would be recognized as the appropriate response to divine rescue.

#### I-A3. Covenant and agricultural blessing in treaty conventions
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — DOCUMENTED]**
Late Bronze Age treaties (Hittite suzerainty treaties) typically concluded with blessing formulas: fertility, rainfall, and harvest promised to the faithful vassal. YHWH's commitment in Genesis 8:22 — "seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, day and night, shall not cease" — parallels the structural position of blessings in ANE treaty conventions. The promise is specifically agricultural and cosmic, addressing the two things an ancient Near Eastern farmer needed guaranteed: regular seasons and reliable harvests.

#### I-A4. Agricultural recovery after catastrophe
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — DOCUMENTED]**
The Nile flood cycle (annual flooding followed by agricultural recovery) was the defining rhythm of Egyptian life. Mesopotamian agricultural manuals (the Sumerian Farmer's Almanac, c. 1700 BCE but reflecting older practice) detailed the sequence of post-flood preparation: breaking up clods, clearing channels, planting barley. Genesis 8's transition from flood to "seedtime and harvest" (8:22) would resonate with an audience for whom agricultural recovery after water catastrophe was a lived annual reality.

### Scenario B: If composed during the Monarchic period (~10th-9th c. BCE)
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — POSSIBLE]**

#### I-B1. Altar-building as covenant founding act
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**
In the monarchic period, altars marked places of divine encounter and covenant. Abraham built altars at Shechem, Bethel, and Hebron (Gen 12:7-8, 13:18); Isaac at Beersheba (26:25); Jacob at Bethel (35:7). Noach (Noah)'s altar at 8:20 is the first in the biblical narrative. In a context where legitimate altar-sites were politically contested (the rival shrines at Bethel and Dan vs. Jerusalem), the founding of the altar tradition in Noah — outside any political or tribal claim — carries theological weight: the first altar belongs to no dynasty.

#### I-B2. Divine self-limitation as a monarchic theological claim
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**
Genesis 8:21's statement — "YHWH said in his heart" and resolved never again to destroy — presents God voluntarily limiting his own judgment in response to human moral reality. In a monarchic context where prophets challenged royal power in the name of YHWH's justice, this passage presents a God who has already committed himself to restraint. The divine resolve of 8:21-22 grounds the world's stability in an internal divine decision, not in human obedience or royal merit.

#### I-B3. The seasons as theological category
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**
The promise of "seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, day and night" (8:22) corresponds to the agricultural calendar that structured Israelite life. The Gezer Calendar (c. 10th c. BCE) documents a twelve-month agricultural cycle: two months of harvest, two of planting, two of late planting, a month of flax, a month of barley, a month of harvest and measure, two months of vine-tending, a month of summer fruit. The seasons promised in 8:22 are not abstractions — they are the specific cycles on which Israelite survival depended.

### Scenario C: If composed/finalized during the Exilic/Post-Exilic period (~6th-5th c. BCE) — *Scholarly consensus for final form*
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

#### I-C1. Sacrifice without a temple
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**
The exilic community faced a defining crisis: how to worship YHWH without the Jerusalem temple. Noach (Noah)'s altar in 8:20 — built in the open, with no temple, no priesthood, no established cultus — provided a theological precedent: sacrifice to YHWH was possible before the temple existed and outside of any sacred building. The divine response to Noah's sacrifice ("YHWH smelled the pleasing aroma") grounds worship in divine acceptance, not in the performance of ritual in the correct building.

#### I-C2. Agricultural promise as post-catastrophe hope
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**
After 586 BCE, the exilic community faced the collapse of everything that had structured their existence. The divine promise of 8:22 — "seedtime and harvest shall not cease" — would carry enormous weight as a commitment that the created order itself would remain stable, regardless of political catastrophe. The world that YHWH would not destroy again includes the regular cycles of agriculture and day and night. The covenant is cosmic, not merely personal.

#### I-C3. The re-creation pattern and return from exile
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**
Genesis 8's sequence — *ruach* over waters (8:1, echoing 1:2), dry land appearing, creatures released to "be fruitful and multiply" (8:17, echoing 1:22, 1:28) — structures the flood's aftermath as a new creation. For the exilic community anticipating return from Babylon, this pattern would resonate with Second Isaiah's re-creation language (Isa 43:16-21: "I am doing a new thing… a way in the wilderness"). The flood's aftermath is the template for restoration after catastrophe.

#### I-C4. Bird augury and Babylonian omen culture
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — DOCUMENTED]**
Babylonian omen science was highly developed. Extispicy (examining animal entrails) and augury (observing birds) were practiced by specialist priests. Omen tablets classified hundreds of bird behaviors as positive or negative signs. Jewish exiles living in Babylon would have been surrounded by this culture. Genesis 8's bird-sending is practical — Noah uses birds to determine the state of the land — not divinatory. The contrast with Babylonian augury culture may be deliberate: Noah reads the birds as ecological reporters, not oracular signs.

### Scenario D: If redacted during the Persian/early-Hellenistic period (~4th-3rd c. BCE) — *Associated with final Pentateuchal shaping*
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — POSSIBLE]**

#### I-D1. Covenant as constitutional document
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — DOCUMENTED]**
In the Persian province of Yehud, the Torah was functioning as a constitutional document for the Jewish community under imperial governance. Genesis 8:21-22's divine internal resolve — "I will not again" — precedes and grounds the explicit covenant of Genesis 9. In a community whose political existence depended on the continuity of Persian imperial permission, the cosmic divine commitment to not destroy the world again would be read as the deepest possible foundation for communal stability.

#### I-D2. Comparative sacrifice traditions
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — DOCUMENTED]**
In the Hellenistic period, sacrificial practice was being compared and discussed across cultures. Greek philosophical traditions (Plato, Theophrastus) discussed whether the gods needed sacrifices or whether sacrifice was a human institution. Berossus's account of Xisuthros (the Babylonian flood hero) includes post-flood sacrifice parallel to Genesis 8:20. The specificity of Genesis's account — burnt offerings of clean animals, divine response of resolve rather than hunger — marks a theological position in a world where the meaning and purpose of sacrifice was an open question.

#### I-D3. Calendar reckoning and the end of the flood
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — DOCUMENTED]**
The flood's end date (Month 2, Day 27 of Noah's 601st year) and its complex chronology were subjects of active debate in Second Temple calendar disputes. The book of Jubilees (~2nd c. BCE) recalculates the flood chronology according to its 364-day solar calendar, arriving at different dates. The precision of Genesis 8:13-14's two-stage drying (surface-dry on Month 1, Day 1; fully dry on Month 2, Day 27) reflects a tradition insisting on exactness — a precision that later communities would argue over as evidence for their preferred calendar system.
