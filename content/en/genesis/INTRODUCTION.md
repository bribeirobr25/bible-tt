## A. Overview

### A1. What Genesis is
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis is the first book of the Torah (the first five books of Moses, also called the Pentateuch) and of the Hebrew Bible (the Tanakh — the Jewish scriptural collection of Law, Prophets, and Writings). Its Hebrew title בְּרֵאשִׁית (*Bereshit*) is taken from its opening word, meaning "in the beginning of." Its English title "Genesis" derives from the Greek Γένεσις (*Genesis*, "origin, coming into being"), which is how the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures, often abbreviated LXX) described the book's subject matter rather than translating its Hebrew name.

The book contains fifty chapters and functions as the narrative prologue to the Torah's legal corpus. It narrates no law codes and prescribes no rituals. It establishes the narrative world into which the Sinai legislation will be delivered.

**Source:** Carr, *Reading the Fractures of Genesis* (1996), 1–15; Wenham, *Genesis 1–15* (WBC, 1987), xxi–xlv.

### A2. Place in the Torah
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

In the canonical sequence, Genesis precedes Exodus and provides the backstory for Israel's presence in Egypt. The book ends with Jacob's family settled in Goshen (Gen 46–50), and Exodus 1:1–7 resumes from exactly that point. Within the Torah's five-book structure, Genesis is the only book that contains no Sinaitic legislation (laws given at Mount Sinai). Its relationship to the other four books is narrative foundation: it answers who Israel is, how they came to Egypt, and what promises stand behind the covenant that Exodus–Deuteronomy will elaborate.

**Source:** Rendtorff, *The Problem of the Process of Transmission in the Pentateuch* (1977/ET 1990), 18–40.

### A3. Literary structure
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis divides into two major sections that differ in scope, style, and narrative world:

**Primeval history (chapters 1–11):** Universal in scope. Narrates creation, the first human pair, the first murder, genealogical expansion, the flood, and the Babel episode. Characters are archetypal. Geography is cosmic (Eden, the mountains of Ararat, Shinar). The time scale is immeasurably long or left undefined.

**Ancestral narratives (chapters 12–50):** Particular in scope. Narrates the lives of Abraham and Sarah (12–25:18), Isaac and Rebekah (25:19–26:35), Jacob and his wives (27–36), and Joseph and his brothers (37–50). Characters are individuated. Geography is locatable (Haran, Canaan, Egypt). The time scale is generational.

The transition occurs at 11:27–12:9, where Terah's genealogy narrows the universal scope down to a single family, and the divine speech to Abram (12:1–3) reorients the narrative from humanity to a particular lineage.

**Source:** Westermann, *Genesis 1–11* (CC, 1984), 1–20; von Rad, *Genesis* (OTL, 1961), 13–30.

### A4. The *toledot* formula as structural spine
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The recurring phrase אֵלֶּה תוֹלְדוֹת (*ʾelleh toledot*, "these are the generations of" or "this is the account of") appears throughout Genesis and functions as a structural marker. The word תוֹלְדוֹת (*toledot*) means "begettings" or "generations," that is, what is generated or produced from a given starting point. Each occurrence introduces a new narrative or genealogical section.

The occurrences:

| # | Reference | Formula | Content introduced |
|---|-----------|---------|-------------------|
| 1 | 2:4 | *toledot* of the skies and the land | Eden narrative and its aftermath |
| 2 | 5:1 | *toledot* of Adam | Adam-to-Noah genealogy |
| 3 | 6:9 | *toledot* of Noah | Flood narrative |
| 4 | 10:1 | *toledot* of the sons of Noah | Table of nations |
| 5 | 11:10 | *toledot* of Shem | Shem-to-Terah genealogy |
| 6 | 11:27 | *toledot* of Terah | Abraham cycle |
| 7 | 25:12 | *toledot* of Ishmael | Ishmael genealogy |
| 8 | 25:19 | *toledot* of Isaac | Jacob cycle |
| 9 | 36:1 | *toledot* of Esau | Esau genealogy |
| 10 | 36:9 | *toledot* of Esau (repeated) | Esau in Seir |
| 11 | 37:2 | *toledot* of Jacob | Joseph narrative |

Whether 36:1 and 36:9 count as one or two occurrences is debated, yielding either ten or eleven total. The formula does not appear outside Genesis within the Pentateuch in this narrative-structuring role (Numbers 3:1 uses the word differently).

**Source:** Cross, *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* (1973), 301–305; Tengström, *Die Toledotformel und die literarische Struktur der priesterlichen Erweiterungsschicht im Pentateuch* (1982).

## B. Authorship and Composition

### B1. What the text itself says
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis does not name its author. No verse in Genesis attributes its composition to any individual. This is a textual fact, not an argument for or against any authorship theory. Elsewhere in the Pentateuch, certain passages refer to Moses writing specific texts (Exod 17:14; 24:4; 34:27; Num 33:2; Deut 31:9, 22, 24), but none of these references concern Genesis. The book itself is anonymous.

### B2. Traditional Mosaic attribution
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The attribution of Genesis (and the entire Pentateuch) to Moses is a post-biblical tradition, not a claim made within Genesis itself. Principal witnesses to this tradition:

- **Jewish tradition:** The Babylonian Talmud (*Bava Batra* 14b–15a) attributes the writing of "his book" (the Torah) to Moses, including Genesis. This represents a received tradition passed down through the community, not a reasoned argument derived from what Genesis itself actually says.
- **Philo of Alexandria** (1st century CE) refers to Moses as the lawgiver and author of the Pentateuch throughout his works (*De Vita Mosis* 2.45–47).
- **Josephus** (*Contra Apionem* 1.39) attributes the five books to Moses.
- **New Testament** texts refer to "the book of Moses" (Mark 12:26) and "Moses wrote" (John 5:46–47), reflecting the received attribution of the 1st century CE.
- **Islamic tradition:** The Quran refers to the *Tawrat* (Torah) as given to Musa (Moses), though this addresses Sinai legislation more than the Genesis narratives.

This attribution was essentially universal in Jewish, Christian, and Islamic tradition until the early modern period. It remains held by many communities today.

**Source:** Leiman, *The Canonization of Hebrew Scripture* (1976); McDonald, *The Biblical Canon* (2007), 56–82.

### B3. Early doubts
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Observations that complicate simple Mosaic authorship were noted by pre-modern readers:

- **Abraham ibn Ezra** (12th century) made cryptic comments (on Gen 12:6, Deut 1:1, and Deut 34) implying that certain passages were written after Moses's time. He hinted rather than stated. Later readers (notably Spinoza) unpacked his meaning.
- **Baruch Spinoza** (*Tractatus Theologico-Politicus*, 1670) argued explicitly that Moses did not write the Pentateuch as a whole, pointing to third-person narration, the death of Moses (Deut 34), and anachronistic references.
- **Jean Astruc** (1753) observed that Genesis alternates between the divine names Elohim and YHWH in ways suggesting two underlying source documents, which he still attributed to Moses as compiler.
- **Richard Simon** (*Histoire critique du Vieux Testament*, 1678) proposed that Moses used earlier written sources and that later editors compiled the Pentateuch.

These observations did not come from hostility to the text. They came from careful reading of its features.

**Source:** Spinoza, *Tractatus* (1670), ch. 8; Astruc, *Conjectures sur les mémoires originaux dont il parait que Moïse s'est servi pour composer le livre de la Genèse* (1753).

### B4. The Documentary Hypothesis and its successors
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

Building on Astruc's observation about divine names, 19th-century scholars developed models that tried to identify the separate written sources behind the Pentateuch's final form. The classical formulation:

**The Wellhausen Documentary Hypothesis (JEDP):**

| Source | Approximate date | Key features |
|--------|-----------------|--------------|
| J (Yahwist) | 10th–9th c. BCE | Uses YHWH from the start; depicts God with human emotions and actions (anthropomorphic); Judahite perspective |
| E (Elohist) | 9th–8th c. BCE | Uses Elohim until Exodus 3; dreams and angels as mediators; Northern perspective |
| D (Deuteronomist) | 7th c. BCE | Concentrated in Deuteronomy; centralization of worship; covenant theology |
| P (Priestly) | 6th–5th c. BCE | Genealogies, dates, ritual instructions; transcendent deity; systematic structure |

Julius Wellhausen (*Prolegomena zur Geschichte Israels*, 1878) synthesized earlier work into this model. It dominated scholarship for a century and remains influential in modified forms.

**Current models diverge significantly:** **[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

- **Supplementary models** (Blum, Carr): A base document was progressively expanded rather than four independent sources being mechanically spliced together. Emphasizes editorial growth over source separation.
- **Neo-documentary models** (Baden, Stackert): Defend the existence of originally independent, continuous sources but revise their dating and scope. Argue that the sources can still be isolated as coherent narratives.
- **European tradition** (Schmid, Gertz, Römer): Questions the existence of a pre-Priestly continuous source (the old "J"). Proposes that the earliest connected narrative may be Priestly, with non-Priestly material added later.
- **Fragmentary approaches** (Van Seters, in part): Some material entered the Pentateuch as independent fragments rather than as parts of continuous documents.

No single model commands consensus. The field is in active ferment.

**Source:** Wellhausen, *Prolegomena* (1878/ET 1885); Blum, *Studien zur Komposition des Pentateuch* (1990); Baden, *The Composition of the Pentateuch* (2012); Schmid, *The Old Testament: A Literary History* (2012).

### B5. What we can say with confidence
**[TEXTUAL — PROBABLE]**

Several observations about Genesis's composition are widely (though not universally) accepted across the scholarly spectrum:

1. Genesis contains material from more than one hand or tradition. The doublets (passages that appear twice with variation — two creation accounts, two genealogies from Adam, two namings of Beersheba), differences in word choice (Elohim/YHWH, Sinai/Horeb), and stylistic differences are features of the text itself, not artifacts of a particular theory.
2. The book reached its present form through a process of composition and editing that extended over a considerable period. The precise dates and mechanisms are debated.
3. Genesis contains material that reflects different historical periods. Some elements fit a 2nd-millennium setting, while others presuppose 1st-millennium realities.
4. The final form of the text is a coherent literary work, regardless of its compositional history. Reading the text as a whole and analyzing its sources are complementary approaches, not mutually exclusive.

The TT translates the final Masoretic Text (the standard Hebrew text preserved by Jewish scholars from the 6th–10th centuries CE, abbreviated MT). It does not adopt any particular composition theory.

## C. Dating

### C1. Composition dating
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Dating Genesis's composition is inseparable from the broader Pentateuchal question. Proposed models:

| Model | Genesis material dated to | Basis |
|-------|--------------------------|-------|
| Traditional | Mosaic period (15th or 13th c. BCE) | Mosaic authorship tradition |
| Classical Documentary | J: 10th–9th c.; E: 9th–8th c.; P: 6th–5th c. | Source analysis, divine names, style |
| Neo-Documentary | J: 10th–8th c.; E: 9th–8th c.; P: 6th–5th c. | Revised source isolation |
| Supplementary | Base (non-P): 8th–7th c.; P layer: 6th–5th c. | Editorial growth model |
| Late/Persian-period | Most composition: 5th–4th c. BCE | Persian-period redaction as primary creative act |

No model can be dated with precision. The latest possible date (terminus ad quem) for Genesis in roughly its current form is approximately the 3rd century BCE, when the Septuagint translation was produced. The translators were working from a Hebrew text recognizably close to the Masoretic version.

**Source:** Schmid, *Genesis and the Moses Story* (2010); Römer, *The Invention of God* (2015), 23–45.

### C2. Narrated period
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The narrated time span of Genesis encompasses two distinct domains:

**Primeval period (chapters 1–11):** No datable historical period corresponds to the events narrated. The genealogical numbers in chapters 5 and 11 have been used to calculate chronologies (Ussher's 4004 BCE being the most famous), but these calculations depend on reading the genealogies as strict chronological records — a reading of the literary genre that is itself debated. The primeval narratives describe a narrative world, not a recoverable historical period.

**Patriarchal period (chapters 12–50):** Traditionally placed in the 2nd millennium BCE (Middle Bronze Age, roughly 2000–1550 BCE) based on personal names, social customs (bride-price, inheritance practices, treaty forms), and geographical references. However, many of these features are not exclusively Middle Bronze. The "patriarchal period" as a historical era remains a working hypothesis, not a demonstrated fact.
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — POSSIBLE]**

**Source:** Thompson, *The Historicity of the Patriarchal Narratives* (1974); Millard and Wiseman, eds., *Essays on the Patriarchal Narratives* (1980).

## D. Historical Setting

### D1. The ANE world of the narrated text
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

Genesis narrates events within a world that shares features with the broader ancient Near East (ANE — Mesopotamia, Egypt, Canaan, and surrounding regions in antiquity). These parallels do not prove that one text borrowed from another. They indicate a shared cultural environment.

**Cosmology:** The cosmos described in Genesis 1 (waters above and below, a *raqiaʿ* separating them, luminaries set within it, dry land emerging from water) corresponds in broad outline to cosmological concepts attested in Mesopotamian, Egyptian, and Ugaritic (ancient Syrian city-state) texts. The *Enuma Elish* (a Babylonian creation story, perhaps 12th century BCE in written form) describes creation through separation and ordering of primordial elements. The Egyptian Hermopolitan tradition describes a primordial mound emerging from water. These are parallel conceptions, not necessarily sources.

**Flood traditions:** The Genesis flood narrative (chapters 6–9) shares structural elements with Mesopotamian flood accounts, notably the *Atrahasis Epic* (an Old Babylonian text, roughly 1700 BCE) and Tablet XI of the *Epic of Gilgamesh* (a later Standard Babylonian version, roughly 1200 BCE). Common elements: divine decision to destroy, one family warned, a boat, birds sent out, sacrifice after landing. The direction and mechanism of any literary relationship remain debated.

**Genealogical traditions:** The long lifespans in Genesis 5 and 11 have structural parallels in the Sumerian King List (an ancient Mesopotamian document listing kings before and after a great flood), which assigns reigns of tens of thousands of years to kings before the flood (antediluvian kings). Both traditions use implausibly large numbers for the pre-flood period, with a sharp reduction afterward.

**Semi-nomadic pastoralism:** The patriarchs' lifestyle (tent-dwelling, flocks and herds, movement between grazing areas, interaction with settled populations) is consistent with herding and seasonal-migration patterns attested throughout the 2nd and 1st millennia BCE in the Levant (the region including modern Israel, Lebanon, Jordan, and Syria).

**City-state politics:** Genesis narrates interactions with city-based rulers (the king of Sodom, Pharaoh, Abimelech of Gerar) whose political authority is local rather than imperial. This is consistent with Levantine political structures across a broad period.

**Source:** Dalley, *Myths from Mesopotamia* (1989); Lambert, *Babylonian Creation Myths* (2013); Hess and Tsumura, eds., *I Studied Inscriptions from Before the Flood* (1994).

### D2. Candidate composition periods
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

If Genesis contains material from different periods, three candidate eras are most frequently proposed:

**D2a. Monarchy period (10th–8th century BCE):**
Royal courts in Jerusalem and Samaria maintained scribal institutions capable of producing literary texts. The "Solomonic Enlightenment" hypothesis (von Rad) proposed the Yahwist source as a product of the Davidic-Solomonic royal court. This dating is now less widely held but not abandoned. Monarchic-period composition would explain the interest in Judah (Gen 38; 49:8–12) and in supporting territorial claims.
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — POSSIBLE]**

**D2b. Late monarchy and exilic period (7th–6th century BCE):**
The Deuteronomic reform under King Josiah (621 BCE — a period of religious centralization recorded in 2 Kings 22–23) and the Babylonian exile (586–539 BCE, when Judah's population was deported to Babylon) are widely considered catalysts for major literary activity. An exilic date for Priestly material would explain its concern with maintaining identity (genealogy, circumcision, dietary distinction, Sabbath) in a context where the temple was destroyed and the land was lost.
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

**D2c. Persian period (5th–4th century BCE):**
The Persian imperial authorization of local religious law (the so-called "imperial authorization" thesis, Frei) has been proposed as the context for the Pentateuch's final editing and assembly. Persian-period Yehud (the province of Judah under Persian rule) had scribal capacity, temple infrastructure, and political motivation to produce an authoritative Torah. The extent of Persian influence on the content (as opposed to the final compilation) is debated.
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — POSSIBLE]**

**Source:** von Rad, *The Problem of the Hexateuch* (1938/ET 1966); Albertz, *Israel in Exile* (2003); Frei, "Persian Imperial Authorization," in Watts, ed., *Persia and Torah* (2001).

### D3. Governance structures in Genesis
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Genesis depicts several forms of social and political organization:

**Patriarchal household (*bet ʾab*, "father's house"):** The primary social unit is the extended family headed by the senior male. Authority is domestic and familial, not institutional. The patriarch controls property, arranges marriages, administers justice within the family (Gen 38:24), and serves as the family's ritual officiant (Gen 12:7–8; 22:9–13).

**Covenant as political instrument:** Genesis presents covenants (*berit* — binding formal agreements) as structuring relationships both divine-human (Gen 9:8–17; 15:7–21; 17:1–27) and inter-human (Gen 21:22–34; 26:26–33; 31:43–54). These function as binding agreements that define obligations, boundaries, and mutual commitments, similar to treaty forms attested across the ancient Near East.

**City-based kingdoms:** The narrative world includes city-kings (Gen 14; 20; 26) who exercise authority over limited territories. Their governance is local, personal, and often depicted in terms of direct negotiation with the patriarchal household.

### D4. Intellectual currents reflected in Genesis
**[STRONG INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**

Genesis engages with questions that were active across ancient Near Eastern literary cultures, though it addresses them narratively and concretely rather than abstractly or systematically:

**Origins of the world:** How did the ordered world come to be? Genesis 1–2 presents two distinct accounts: one proceeding by divine speech and separation (ch. 1), the other by divine craftsmanship and planting (ch. 2). Neither is a philosophical treatise; both are narrative.

**Human nature:** What are human beings, and what is their place? Humans are made in the *tselem elohim* ("image of God," 1:26–27) and formed from *ʾadamah* (ground, 2:7). They are simultaneously exalted and earthbound.

**The problem of suffering:** Why does the world contain suffering and disorder? Genesis 3–11 narrates a sequence of transgressions and consequences (the garden, Cain, the flood generation, Babel) without offering a systematic theory of evil. The narrative shows rather than explains.

**Ethics of violence:** Genesis narrates murder (4:8), vengeance (4:23–24), divine destruction (6–9), and intergroup conflict (34) without always providing explicit moral commentary. The text presents; the reader evaluates.

**Source:** Westermann, *Genesis 1–11*, 18–47; Levenson, *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* (1988).

## E. Manuscript Transmission

### E1. How the text reached us
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The Hebrew text of Genesis was transmitted through a chain of copying and editorial care spanning over two millennia:

- Composition (uncertain date)
- Oral and/or written traditions
- Editorial compilation (pre-exilic through Persian period)
- Second Temple period copies (3rd c. BCE – 1st c. CE):
  - Dead Sea Scrolls (Qumran, ca. 250 BCE – 68 CE)
  - Samaritan Pentateuch tradition (diverges ca. 2nd c. BCE)
  - Septuagint translation (3rd c. BCE, from a Hebrew source text)
- Proto-Masoretic standardization (1st–2nd c. CE)
- Masoretic tradition (6th–10th c. CE):
  - Aleppo Codex (ca. 930 CE)
  - Codex Leningrad (1009 CE) → BHS → standard critical editions

### E3. The Masoretic Text and BHS
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The Transparent Translation works from the Masoretic Text (MT) as represented in the *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* (BHS — the standard critical edition of the Hebrew Bible used by scholars), which is based on Codex Leningrad (B19A, dated 1009 CE). The Masoretes (6th–10th century CE) did not compose the text. They preserved and annotated a consonantal tradition (Hebrew written with only consonants) that was already ancient. Their contributions include:

- **Vocalization:** The vowel pointing (*nikkud* — small marks added above and below letters) that guides pronunciation and, in some cases, interpretation.
- **Accentuation:** The *te'amim* (cantillation marks — accent symbols indicating both liturgical chanting and grammatical structure).
- **Masorah:** Marginal and final notes recording word counts, variant spellings, and exceptional features to prevent scribal error.

Where the MT is ambiguous or apparently corrupt, the TT follows the MT reading and notes the ambiguity rather than silently emending.

### E4. Discovery history
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Key discoveries that shaped the textual evidence for Genesis:

**Dead Sea Scrolls (1947–1956):** Discovered in caves near Qumran by the Dead Sea. The Genesis scrolls demonstrate that the consonantal text was substantially stable by the late Second Temple period, though minor variants exist. The scrolls pushed the textual evidence for Genesis back by roughly a millennium compared to the previously earliest Hebrew manuscripts.

**Cairo Genizah (1896–1898):** Discovered in the Ben Ezra Synagogue storeroom in Old Cairo, primarily through the efforts of Solomon Schechter. Approximately 300,000 fragments, including biblical manuscripts, some with Palestinian vocalization systems that predate or differ from the Tiberian Masoretic standard.

**Codex Leningrad (acquired 1863):** Purchased by Abraham Firkovitch and housed in the Russian National Library, St. Petersburg. Copied in 1009 CE. It became the base text for BHK (1937) and BHS (1977) because it is the oldest complete manuscript of the Hebrew Bible.

**Aleppo Codex (surfaced 1958):** Vocalized by Aaron ben Asher (ca. 930 CE). Kept in Aleppo, Syria, for centuries. Partially damaged in riots in 1947; brought to Israel in 1958. Missing most of the Torah, including much of Genesis. Where extant, it is considered the most authoritative Masoretic witness.

### E5. Was Genesis found as a separate book?
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

In the Qumran library, Genesis appears on scrolls that contain Genesis alone, consistent with the practical limits of scroll length (a scroll containing the entire Pentateuch would be unwieldy). However, there is no evidence that Genesis ever circulated as an independent literary work separate from the Torah. The Qumran community treated the Torah as a unified five-book collection, as did all known Second Temple Jewish communities.

The division into five books is ancient (the Septuagint already reflects it), but the books were understood as parts of a single work. Genesis was always the first part of the Torah, not a standalone text that was later joined to others.

**Source:** Tov, *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible* (3rd ed., 2012); Ulrich, *The Dead Sea Scrolls and the Origins of the Bible* (1999).

### E6. Canon variation across Christian traditions
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The Hebrew Bible canon used as the TT's base text (BHS / Masoretic Text) reflects the Jewish rabbinic canon, also followed by Protestant Christianity. Other Christian traditions include additional books: Catholic and Eastern Orthodox canons include the Deuterocanonical books (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Baruch, 1-2 Maccabees, additions to Esther and Daniel). The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo broader canon additionally includes *1 Enoch*, *Jubilees*, and several other texts. "What counts as Bible" is not settled across global Christianity. Pseudepigraphic literature (ancient texts attributed to a biblical figure — like Enoch or Adam — but composed centuries after that figure lived, such as *Life of Adam and Eve*, *1 Enoch*, *Cave of Treasures*) is widely read in scholarly and popular contexts but is not canonical in most traditions. For readers wanting to engage these texts directly, the standard peer-reviewed scholarly editions are Charlesworth's *The Old Testament Pseudepigrapha* (Doubleday, 1983-85), Nickelsburg-VanderKam's *1 Enoch* (Fortress Press, 2004), and Stone-Anderson's synopsis of the Adam and Eve literature (SBL, 1999).

### F2. Translation challenges specific to Genesis
**[TEXTUAL — PROBABLE]**

**Genesis 1:1–3 — Three readings:**
The opening of Genesis admits at least three syntactically viable readings:

1. **Independent sentence:** "In the beginning, God created the skies and the land." (Traditional; takes *bereshit* as absolute.)
2. **Temporal clause:** "In the beginning of God's creating the skies and the land, the land was formless..." (Takes *bereshit* as construct; Gen 1:1 is subordinate to 1:2.)
3. **Temporal clause with 1:3 as main clause:** "In the beginning of God's creating... — the land being formless... — God said, 'Let there be light.'" (1:1 subordinate, 1:2 parenthetical, 1:3 main clause.)

The TT does not resolve this ambiguity. The translation preserves the syntax in a way that allows the reader to consider all three.

**Source:** Holmstedt, "The Restrictive Syntax of Genesis i 1," *VT* 58 (2008): 56–67; Wenham, *Genesis 1–15*, 11–13.

**The *waw*-consecutive system:**
Hebrew narrative relies on the *waw*-consecutive (or *wayyiqtol*) verbal form — a verb construction that chains sequential actions together with the word "and" (*waw*). English has no direct equivalent. Rendering every *wayyiqtol* as "and... and... and..." produces a monotonous English result. But breaking the chain into independent sentences loses the Hebrew's flowing, additive style. The TT seeks a middle path that preserves the additive, sequential character of Hebrew narrative prose without producing unreadable English.

**Proper names:**
Many names in Genesis carry etymological significance that the narrative exploits (e.g., Adam / *ʾadamah*, Eve / *chavvah* / "living," Babel / *balal*). The TT retains the standard English forms of proper names but provides etymological notes where the narrative depends on the wordplay.
