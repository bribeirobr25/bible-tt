## 1. Wordplay double-meanings — John's signature linguistic device

**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 1, 2, 3

For the more strictly etymological treatment of one specific term, see G-J2 (*Monogenēs*).

The Fourth Gospel uses words with two simultaneous meanings as a deliberate literary device. The reader is expected to hold both senses at once; the narrative often resolves on which sense was operative only retrospectively. Within John 1–3 the in-scope cases:

**John 1:5 — *katelaben***. "The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not *katelaben* it." The Greek verb means both "overcome" and "comprehend/grasp." Both readings are theologically loaded — has the darkness failed to extinguish the light, or failed to understand it? The Greek word holds both; the TT preserves the ambiguity with a Tier 2 note rather than choosing one English gloss.

**John 2:9 — water-to-wine as an absent mechanism**. The narrator says the chief steward "did not know where it had come from" — the mechanism of the sign is deliberately withheld. This is not wordplay in the strict sense, but it shares the same literary technique: a textual hole inviting two readings (miraculous transformation vs. unspoken steward-substitution).

**John 3:3 — *anōthen***. Yeshua tells Nikodemos "unless one is born *anōthen*..." The adverb means both "from above" and "again." Nikodemos hears "again" and is confused; Yeshua means "from above." The chapter rests on the misreading. The Locked Glossary preserves the dual sense.

**John 3:8 — *pneuma***. "The *pneuma* blows where it wishes, and you hear its sound, but you do not know where it comes from or where it goes. So it is with everyone born of the *pneuma*." The Greek means "wind" AND "spirit"; the verse depends on both senses being active simultaneously. The TT preserves *pneuma* as one word and notes both senses.

**John 3:14 — *hypsōthēnai***. "As Mosheh lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so the Son of Man must be lifted up" — the Greek verb means both "lifted up physically" (the bronze serpent on a pole; Yeshua on the cross) AND "exalted" (raised to glory). The double meaning is the chapter's typological hinge.

The cumulative weight matters: five double-meanings in three chapters is denser than the comparable rate in the Synoptics. The motif entry documents John's project-level commitment to writing-with-two-meanings as a feature of the Greek, not as a translation artifact.

**Source:** Brown, R.E., *The Gospel According to John I–XII*, Anchor Bible, Doubleday, 1966, esp. introduction §VIII on Johannine misunderstandings and double meanings.

## 2. *Monogenēs* — "only-born" across John 1 and 3

**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 1, 3

The Greek term *monogenēs* (μονογενής, "only-born" / "only of its kind") appears four times in John 1–3 (1:14, 1:18, 3:16, 3:18), making it one of the most recurring theologically-loaded terms in the opening chapters. The TT renders it consistently across all four locales: EN "only-born", PT-BR "único-nascido", DE "einziggeborenen", ES "único-nacido" — the cross-language alignment preserves the begetting/generation reading (mono- + -genēs from *gennaō*, "to beget") rather than the alternative semantic reading ("unique"; from *genos*, "kind"), per the project-level decision (`docs/audit/PENDING.md` flagged this as a cross-language consistency item).

The four occurrences track an arc. **John 1:14** introduces *monogenēs* in the Logos prologue: "we have seen his glory, glory as of the *monogenēs* from the father." The term first applies to the Word-made-flesh in the cosmological frame. **John 1:18** then occurs with a textual variant — manuscripts read either *monogenēs theos* ("only-born God") or *monogenēs huios* ("only-born Son"). The TT preserves the variant with a Tier 2 textual-critical note rather than silently resolving it.

**John 3:16** uses *monogenēs* in the most-quoted sentence in the New Testament: "for God so loved the world that he gave his *monogenēs* son." Here the term sits inside the conditional-promise structure of the verse. **John 3:18** then escalates: "he who does not believe is already judged, because he has not believed in the name of the *monogenēs* son of God." The same word becomes the criterion of judgment.

The cross-chapter pattern matters because the term migrates from cosmological introduction (Jn 1) to soteriological centerpiece (Jn 3:16) to judicial criterion (Jn 3:18) across just three chapters. The TT's locked rendering keeps this trajectory visible — a reader following the term sees it in the same form four times and can register the contextual shift without translation-variation noise.

**Source:** Pendrick, G., "*Monogenēs*," *New Testament Studies* 41 (1995): 587–600, on the etymological case for the begetting reading.

## 3. *Sēmeion* — the sign sequence opens at Cana

**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 2

This pattern begins in John 2 within the currently-authored range; its development across John 4 (royal official's son), John 6 (loaves), John 9 (man born blind), and John 11 (Lazaros raised) is noted here as forward-trajectory for when those chapters are authored.

The Greek noun *sēmeion* (σημεῖον, "sign") is John's distinctive vocabulary for Yeshua's mighty works. The Synoptic Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke) use *dynamis* (δύναμις, "power" / "powerful deed") as their standard term; John never uses *dynamis* for Yeshua's works. The vocabulary choice is deliberate and consistent — what the Synoptics call powers John calls signs.

John 2:11 introduces the term and self-consciously numbers it: "this *archēn* (beginning) of the *sēmeia* (signs) Yeshua did at Cana of the Galil." The water-to-wine is the first; the narrative announces a series. Modern scholarship has traditionally counted seven public signs in the Fourth Gospel, though the count and selection are debated (some scholars include the post-resurrection catch of 153 fish in John 21; others stop at the resurrection itself).

The motif's distinctness from the Synoptic *dynamis*: *sēmeion* is built on the root for "to mark" or "to point to" — a sign POINTS to something beyond itself. The Johannine signs are revelatory more than utilitarian — they reveal Yeshua's identity to those positioned to read them. This contrasts with the *dynamis*-framing where the act of power is itself the event.

The trajectory within the authored range is one-chapter, but the term's labeling at 2:11 ("the beginning of signs") makes the forward-trajectory unmissable. The motif belongs on the Book Context surface precisely because the single in-scope chapter is the introduction to a pattern that develops over the full Gospel.

**Source:** Brown, R.E., *The Gospel According to John I–XII*, esp. on the structure of the "Book of Signs" (Jn 1–12).

## 4. The *hōra* / "hour" — a trajectory from "not yet come" to "has come"

**[STRONG INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**
**Chapters:** 2

This pattern begins in John 2:4 within the currently-authored range; its trajectory across John 7:30, 8:20, 12:23, 13:1, and 17:1 is noted here as forward-trajectory for when those chapters are authored.

The noun *hōra* (ὥρα, "hour") functions as a technical term in the Fourth Gospel. In John 2:4 Yeshua tells his mother: "what is that to me and to you? my *hōra* has not yet come." The phrase "has not yet come" recurs in John 7:30 ("they sought to arrest him, but no one laid hands on him, because his hour had not yet come"), John 7:8, John 7:30, and John 8:20. Then in John 12:23 the trajectory turns: "the hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified." From John 13:1 onward, the hour is consistently arrived: "Yeshua, knowing that his hour had come..." (13:1); "Father, the hour has come" (17:1).

The motif's structure: the *hōra* is the temporal frame of Yeshua's death-and-glorification. The Fourth Gospel collapses these two events into one — the crucifixion IS the glorification. The "hour" is the moment when the *hypsōthēnai* (lifting up — see G-J1) occurs, and the cosmic-narrative tension across the Gospel is the build-up to it.

Within John 1–3 only the first occurrence is in scope, but the labeling at 2:4 ("has NOT YET come") is intelligible only against the future "has come." The motif entry belongs on the Book Context surface because the meaning of the single in-scope occurrence is structurally dependent on chapters not yet authored. This is the legitimate case for a forward-trajectory motif: the surface that surfaces patterns chapter-by-chapter cannot see this; the cross-chapter view names it.

**Source:** Dodd, C.H., *The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel*, Cambridge University Press, 1953, esp. on the structural function of the *hōra* in John's narrative time.

## 5. Light and darkness — the contrast that runs through the prologue and the Nikodemos discourse

**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 1, 3

Forward-trajectory: the pattern continues in John 8:12 ("I am the light of the world"), John 9 (the man born blind), and John 12:35–36 ("walk while you have the light"). The body-text references to those chapters are forward-pointers; only the John 1 and John 3 occurrences are in scope.

John 1:4–5 opens the contrast in the prologue: "in him was life, and the life was the light of humans; and the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not *katelaben* it" — with the verb's double meaning (see G-J1) holding overcome AND comprehend in tension. The light-darkness pair returns in John 1:9 ("the true light, which enlightens every person, was coming into the world") and is the prologue's organizing theological frame.

John 3:19–21 develops the same contrast in narrative-ethical mode: "this is the judgment, that the light has come into the world and people loved the darkness more than the light, because their works were evil. For everyone who does wicked things hates the light and does not come to the light, lest his works should be exposed. But the one who does the truth comes to the light, so that his works may be seen as worked in God." The metaphor that opened the Gospel as a cosmological-creation frame here becomes a moral-judicial one.

The chapter between them — John 2 — contains a striking absence of light-darkness language despite its dramatic events (the wedding sign, the temple cleansing). The motif drops out of the narrative entirely in the middle chapter, then returns in John 3 inside the Nikodemos episode. The Nikodemos-by-night setting (3:2) thematically inverts the motif: the inquirer comes IN darkness to ask the light a question, and the Gospel doesn't say what he did with the answer.

**Source:** Bultmann, R., *The Gospel of John: A Commentary* (trans. Beasley-Murray), Westminster Press, 1971, esp. on light/darkness as the dualistic frame of the Fourth Gospel.

## 6. Water, spirit, rebirth — the three-chapter progression

**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**
**Chapters:** 1, 2, 3

The three opening chapters of John develop a tightly interlocking water-spirit-rebirth pattern. Each chapter handles the pair differently, and the three chapters together form one of the cleanest typological arcs in John 1–3.

**John 1:26–33 — water vs. spirit as the contrast between two immersions**. Yochanan (John) the Immerser tells the questioning delegation: "I baptize with water; among you stands one whom you do not know" (1:26). At 1:33 the contrast is sharpened: "the one who sent me to immerse with water said to me, 'on whom you see the spirit descending and remaining, this is the one who immerses with the holy spirit.'" The water rite is provisional; the spirit-immersion is the eschatological reality.

**John 2:1–11 — water transformed at Cana**. Six stone purification jars hold "two or three *metretai* each" (2:6) of ritual water. Yeshua's instruction is "fill the jars with water" (2:7); the steward then tastes "the water having become wine" (2:9). The chapter does not say *pneuma* explicitly, but the trajectory water-as-ritual → water-transformed sets up the typological framing. The water moves from purification to celebration; the absent mechanism of the transformation (see G-J1 on John 2:9) leaves the *pneuma* implicit.

**John 3:5 — water AND spirit as the rebirth formula**. "Unless one is born of *water and spirit*, one cannot enter the kingdom of God." The chapter resolves what the prior two chapters held in tension: water and spirit are not opposed (as in John 1's contrast) and not transformed (as in John 2's sign) — they are paired in the rebirth formula. The *anōthen* double meaning (see G-J1) makes this rebirth "from above" as well as "again."

The arc water-as-rite → water-as-medium → water-and-spirit-as-rebirth is structurally significant because each chapter could be read alone but the cross-chapter movement is where the typology resolves. The Phase 9 plan's §4.4 originally listed this motif; it was restored at step 9.2 user-review.

**Source:** Schnackenburg, R., *The Gospel According to St John, Volume One*, trans. Smith et al., Crossroad, 1980, esp. on John's water symbolism.
