## A. Greek Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. *Sēmeion* as "sign" — John's distinctive vocabulary
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 2:11: ταύτην ἐποίησεν ἀρχὴν τῶν σημείων ὁ Ἰησοῦς — "this beginning of the signs Yeshua (Jesus) did." The noun σημεῖον (*sēmeion*) = "sign," something that points beyond itself to a deeper reality. The Synoptic Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke — the three Gospels that share much narrative material) use δύναμις (*dynamis*, "power/mighty work") as their primary term for Jesus's extraordinary acts (Matt 11:20, Mark 6:2, Luke 10:13). John never uses *dynamis* for Jesus's acts. The word choice is consistent and deliberate: John's *sēmeia* (signs) are not displays of raw power but pointers that reveal. They "reveal his glory" (2:11), disclose who Jesus is, and invite trust. The traditional English rendering "miracle" collapses the distinction between John's *sēmeion* (revelatory pointer) and the Synoptic *dynamis* (powerful act). The TT preserves John's term.

### A2. *Naos* versus *hieron* — the two temples
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 2:14 uses ἱερόν (*hieron*) for the temple complex where commerce occurs. John 2:19–21 uses ναός (*naos*) for the sanctuary Yeshua (Jesus) tells them to destroy. The distinction is consistent in the New Testament: *hieron* (from ἱερός, "sacred") refers to the entire sacred precinct, including all courts, porticoes, and external areas; *naos* (from ναίω, "to dwell") refers to the inner sanctuary where the divine presence dwells. In the Jerusalem temple, the *naos* was the structure containing the Holy Place and the Holy of Holies, accessible only to priests and (in the innermost chamber) only to the high priest once per year. The shift from *hieron* to *naos* is theologically loaded: Jesus disrupts the *hieron* (the institutional complex) but offers his body as the *naos* (the dwelling place of God). The TT marks this by rendering *hieron* as "temple complex" and *naos* as "sanctuary."

### A3. "My hour has not yet come" — the *hōra* motif
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 2:4: οὔπω ἥκει ἡ ὥρα μου (*oupō hēkei hē hōra mou*) — "my hour has not yet come." The "hour" (*hōra*) functions as a technical term in the Fourth Gospel, appearing at pivotal moments: 2:4 (not yet come), 7:30 and 8:20 (no one seized him because his hour had not yet come), 12:23 ("the hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified"), 12:27 ("for this purpose I came to this hour"), 13:1 ("knowing that his hour had come to depart from this world to the Father"), 17:1 ("Father, the hour has come"). The trajectory moves from "not yet" to "has come," from Qanah to the cross. The *hōra* is the appointed time of Yeshua (Jesus)'s death, glorification, and return to the Father. At the wedding, the hour has not yet arrived — yet Jesus acts anyway. The relationship between the "not yet" and the action that follows is deliberately unexplained.

### A4. Water-to-wine — the absent mechanism
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 2:9: τὸ ὕδωρ οἶνον γεγενημένον (*to hydōr oinon gegenēmenon*) — "the water having become wine." The perfect participle presents the transformation as an accomplished fact. The narrator describes neither the moment nor the method of transformation. No gesture, no prayer, no formula, no touch is recorded. The text moves from "fill the jars with water" (v.7) to "draw out now" (v.8) to "the water having become wine" (v.9). The transformation happens in the narrative gap, between command and discovery. This narrative reticence is characteristic of John's sign accounts: the focus is not on the act of power but on its revelatory significance ("revealed his glory," v.11).

### A5. The narrator's retrospective in vv.21–22
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 2:21–22 contains two layers of retrospective interpretation. First, the narrator explains (v.21) that Yeshua (Jesus) was speaking about "the sanctuary of his body" — an explanation unavailable to the characters within the story. Second, the narrator reports (v.22) that the followers understood only after Jesus was raised from the dead, at which point they "remembered" and "trusted the scripture and the word." This two-stage pattern (event, then retrospective understanding) is a structural feature of the Fourth Gospel (cf. 12:16, "his followers did not understand these things at first, but when Jesus was glorified, then they remembered"). The TT preserves the narrator's temporal markers without collapsing the gap between event and understanding.

## B. Ancient Parallels

### B1. Dionysus and wine miracle traditions
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The transformation of water into wine has parallels in Greco-Roman religious traditions, most notably in the cult of Dionysus (Roman: Bacchus), the god of wine and ecstasy. Several accounts describe miraculous wine production associated with Dionysian festivals: Euripides (*Bacchae* 704–707) describes wine flowing spontaneously from the ground at the touch of a Bacchic thyrsus. Pliny the Elder (*Natural History* 2.231, 31.16) reports traditions of springs flowing with wine on January 5–6 (later the Christian feast of Epiphany) on the islands of Teos and Andros. Pausanias (*Description of Greece* 6.26.1–2) records a tradition at Elis in which empty vessels placed in a sealed room were found full of wine on the feast day. Whether John's audience would have recognized the Qanah sign as a deliberate counterpoint to Dionysian claims is debated. Some scholars (C.H. Dodd, R. Bultmann) argued that John appropriates and supersedes the Dionysian tradition — the true vine-god replaces the mythological one. Others (C.K. Barrett, D.A. Carson) argue that the Jewish context (wedding, purification jars, Galilee) is sufficient without recourse to Hellenistic parallels. The word-level overlap is limited. The structural parallel (a divine figure producing wine miraculously) is real but broad.

**Source:** Dodd, C.H., *The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel*, Cambridge University Press, 1953; Bultmann, R., *The Gospel of John: A Commentary*, trans. Beasley-Murray, Westminster, 1971; Barrett, C.K., *The Gospel According to St John*, 2nd ed., SPCK, 1978.

## C. Historical and Archaeological Context

### C1. Qanah — location and archaeology
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Two sites compete for identification with biblical Qanah: (1) Kafr Kanna, a village approximately 6 km northeast of Natseret (Nazareth) on the road to Tiberias, which has been the traditional pilgrimage site since at least the Crusader period; and (2) Khirbet Qana, a ruin approximately 14 km north of Nazareth on a hilltop overlooking the Bet Netofa Valley. Excavations at Khirbet Qana (directed by Douglas Edwards, University of Puget Sound, 1998–2009) uncovered a substantial Jewish village with stone vessel fragments, miqva'ot (ritual baths), and evidence of habitation from the Hellenistic through Byzantine periods. A cave system beneath the village shows signs of veneration as the site of the water-to-wine sign from the late Roman period onward. The name match (Khirbet *Qana* preserves the consonants of the biblical name) and the archaeological profile favor Khirbet Qana over Kafr Kanna, though certainty is not possible.

**Source:** Edwards, D.R., "Khirbet Qana: From Jewish Village to Christian Pilgrim Site," in *The Roman and Byzantine Near East*, vol. 3, JRA Supplement 49, 2002; Richardson, P., *Building Jewish in the Roman East*, Baylor University Press, 2004.

### C2. Wedding customs in first-century Galilee
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Jewish weddings in first-century Galilee typically lasted seven days (cf. Judges 14:12, Tobit 11:19). The host family was responsible for providing food and wine for the entire celebration. Running out of wine was a serious social failure. It brought shame on the family and could create legal liability (in rabbinic sources, inadequate wedding provision is discussed in terms of communal obligation). The chief steward (*architriklinos*) managed the feast, controlled the wine service, and ensured hospitality standards. Stone vessels were preferred for ritual purity reasons (Mishnah Kelim 10.1: stone vessels do not contract impurity). The six stone jars described in John 2:6, each holding 2–3 *metrētai* (approximately 75–115 liters each), are consistent with the stone vessel industry attested archaeologically in Judea and Galilee — large stone vessels (called *kalal* in rabbinic Hebrew) made primarily at workshops near Yerushalayim (Jerusalem) and in the Galilee.

**Source:** Safrai, S. and Stern, M., eds., *The Jewish People in the First Century*, vol. 2, Van Gorcum, 1976; Reed, J.L., *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, Trinity Press International, 2000.

### C3. The temple economy and money-changing
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The temple in Yerushalayim (Jerusalem) functioned as both a religious center and an economic institution. Pilgrims needed to purchase sacrificial animals (cattle, sheep, doves) that met the standards of ritual fitness (*temimt*, unblemished). Money-changers (*shulchani* in rabbinic sources, *kermatistēs* or *kollybistēs* in Greek) were necessary because the half-shekel temple tax (Exod 30:13) had to be paid in Tyrian silver — the only coinage of sufficient purity accepted by temple authorities. Tyrian shekels contained approximately 14 grams of silver at 94% purity. The exchange rate included a fee (*kolbon*), discussed in Mishnah Shekalim 1.6-7. Whether the commerce Yeshua (Jesus) disrupted was inherently exploitative or simply commercially necessary is debated by historians. Josephus describes the temple's vast economic infrastructure (*Antiquities* 20.219–222; *War* 5.184–227). Archaeological evidence from the Herodian street south of the Temple Mount — excavated by Benjamin Mazar (1968–1978) — includes shops and commercial installations consistent with a temple-centered market economy.

**Source:** Mazar, B., *The Mountain of the Lord*, Doubleday, 1975; Sanders, E.P., *Judaism: Practice and Belief, 63 BCE–66 CE*, SCM Press, 1992; Bauckham, R., "Jesus' Demonstration in the Temple," in *Law and Religion*, ed. B. Lindars, James Clarke, 1988.

### C4. Passover timing and pilgrim traffic
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Passover (*Pesach*) fell on 14–15 Nisan (March–April). As one of the three pilgrimage festivals (*shalosh regalim*: Pesach, Shavuot, Sukkot), it drew massive crowds to Yerushalayim (Jerusalem). Josephus estimates Passover attendance at various points as 2.5 to 3 million (*War* 6.425) — likely exaggerated, but even conservative estimates suggest 300,000–500,000 pilgrims in a city with a normal population of perhaps 40,000–80,000. The influx created enormous practical demands: housing, food, sacrificial animals, currency exchange, water, and sanitation. The temple operations that Yeshua (Jesus) disrupted were integral to managing this annual surge. John's placement of the temple clearing at Passover — the festival commemorating liberation from Egypt — may carry ironic force: the institution celebrating freedom has become an institution of commerce.

**Source:** Jeremias, J., *Jerusalem in the Time of Jesus*, Fortress Press, 1969; Safrai, S., "Pilgrimage to Jerusalem at the Time of the Second Temple," in *Die Pilgerfahrt nach Jerusalem*, ed. W. Böhme, Calwer Verlag, 1991.

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. τί ἐμοὶ καὶ σοί — the Semitic disengagement idiom
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The phrase τί ἐμοὶ καὶ σοί (*ti emoi kai soi*) = "what to me and to you?" is a calque (a word-for-word borrowing) of the Hebrew מַה־לִּי וָלָךְ (*mah-li valakh*). The idiom appears in the Hebrew Bible in several contexts: Judges 11:12 (Yiftach to the king of Ammon — military confrontation), 2 Samuel 16:10 and 19:23 (David to the sons of Tseruyah — "what do I have in common with you?"), 1 Kings 17:18 (the widow of Tsarfat to Eliyahu (Elijah) — distress and accusation), 2 Kings 3:13 (Elisha to the king of Yisrael — refusal of involvement), 2 Chronicles 35:21 (Pharaoh Nekho to King Yoshiyahu — "what quarrel is between us?"). The range of usage spans from mild disengagement ("this is not my concern") to sharp rebuff ("leave me alone"). In every case, the idiom creates distance between the speaker and the addressee. In John 2:4, Yeshua (Jesus) uses it to establish a boundary: his action will proceed on his own terms and timing, not on his mother's initiative. Translations that soften the idiom ("What concern is that to you and to me?" — NRSV) or redirect it ("Why do you involve me?" — NIV) obscure the Semitic formula. The TT renders literally: "What to me and to you, woman?"

### D2. γύναι — "woman" as vocative address
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The vocative γύναι (*gynai*) is used by Yeshua (Jesus) to address his mother both at Qanah (2:4) and at the cross (19:26). In classical and Koine Greek, *gynai* was a respectful form of address — Odysseus addresses Penelope as *gynai* (Homer, *Odyssey* 23.174); the term carries no inherent disrespect. However, the social context matters: no known parallel exists in ancient Greek, Latin, or Jewish literature of a son addressing his biological mother as "woman" in a face-to-face conversation. The address is not rude, but it creates distance. It redefines the relationship from the familial (mother-son) to something else. In John's narrative, the address appears at the two moments when Jesus acts in his public/revelatory capacity rather than as a family member. Whether this signals a theological point (his mission supersedes family bonds) or reflects compositional convention is debated. The TT preserves the address without domesticating it.

**Source:** Brown, R.E., *The Gospel According to John I–XII*, Anchor Bible 29, Doubleday, 1966; Moloney, F.J., *The Gospel of John*, Sacra Pagina 4, Liturgical Press, 1998.

## E. Scientific Correspondence and Non-Correspondence

### E1. Wine fermentation — the text describes transformation, not creation from nothing
**[SCIENTIFIC COMPARISON — POSSIBLE]**

Natural wine production involves the fermentation of grape sugars by yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*), converting glucose and fructose into ethanol and carbon dioxide over a period of days to weeks. The process is biochemical: enzymes produced by yeast catalyze the breakdown of sugars in an anaerobic environment. The Qanah sign describes water becoming wine — a transformation that bypasses the entire biological process of viticulture, harvesting, crushing, and fermentation. The text does not describe creation *ex nihilo* (from nothing). Water already exists as the starting material. It describes transformation of one substance into another. Whether one frames this as a compression of natural processes or as a categorical discontinuity with natural processes depends on one's metaphysical framework, not on the text itself. The text describes the result ("the water having become wine") without theorizing about mechanism. Modern chemistry can describe what wine is (a complex mixture of ethanol, water, organic acids, tannins, color compounds, and hundreds of aromatic molecules), but it cannot describe a mechanism for the instantaneous conversion of water into wine. The TT notes the gap between the described result and any known natural process without attempting to fill it.

## F. Later Reception

### F1. Marian theology and the wedding at Qanah
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox traditions have drawn extensive theological conclusions from Mary's role at Qanah. The Council of Ephesus (431 CE) defined Mary as Theotokos ("God-bearer"), and while this definition does not depend on John 2, the Qanah narrative has been read as supporting Mary's intercessory role: she presents a human need to her son, and despite the apparent refusal, the need is met. Later Marian theology (particularly in Counter-Reformation Catholicism) developed the image of Mary as mediatrix, one who intercedes with Christ on behalf of humanity. Pope John Paul II's encyclical *Redemptoris Mater* (1987) interprets the Qanah episode as the paradigm of Mary's maternal mediation. Protestant interpreters have generally resisted this reading, arguing that the text shows Yeshua (Jesus) establishing independence from his mother's initiative ("What to me and to you?"), not affirming her mediating role. The TT notes that the text records the mother's statement, Jesus's distancing response, and the mother's instruction to the servants. It does not use the word "intercession" or describe the mother's role as a go-between. The theological framework is later reception, not text.

**Source:** Brown, R.E., et al., *Mary in the New Testament*, Fortress Press, 1978; Gaventa, B.R., *Mary: Glimpses of the Mother of Jesus*, University of South Carolina Press, 1995.

### F2. Sacramental reading of water-to-wine
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

From at least the third century CE, Christian interpreters have read the water-to-wine sign as a prefiguration of the Eucharist. Cyprian of Carthage (*Epistle* 63.12–13, c. 253 CE) argued that the wine at Qanah represented the blood of Christ in the eucharistic cup. The medieval liturgical tradition associated the Qanah miracle with Epiphany (January 6) — the manifestation of divine glory — linking it to the baptism of Jesus and the visit of the Magi as three "manifestations." The Fourth Gospel itself may support a sacramental reading at other points (6:53–56, the bread of life discourse), but at 2:1–11 the text does not mention blood, covenant, or ritual consumption. The sacramental reading is an interpretive tradition, not something the text itself says. The TT records the sign without sacramental overlay.

**Source:** Cullmann, O., *Early Christian Worship*, SCM Press, 1953; Koester, C.R., *Symbolism in the Fourth Gospel*, 2nd ed., Fortress Press, 2003.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. Why is Yeshua's mother unnamed in John?
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE]**

The Fourth Gospel refers to Yeshua (Jesus)'s mother four times (2:1, 2:3, 2:5, 2:12; 6:42; 19:25–27) but never gives her name. The Synoptics identify her as Μαριάμ/Μαρία (Mariam/Maria — Matt 1:16, 2:11, 13:55; Mark 6:3; Luke 1:27, etc.). Several explanations have been proposed: (1) The author assumes the reader already knows her name from other traditions. (2) The anonymity is theologically motivated — in John, she functions relationally (as mother of Jesus) rather than as an independent character. (3) The author belongs to a tradition in which her personal name was not central to the narrative. (4) The anonymity creates a literary effect — she is defined entirely by her relationship to Jesus, which is itself redefined at the cross (19:26–27) when Jesus gives her to the beloved disciple as mother. None of these explanations excludes the others. The TT follows the text: "the mother of Jesus."

### G2. Six stone jars — ritual purification vessels
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The six stone water jars (λίθιναι ὑδρίαι, *lithinai hydriai*) described in 2:6 are consistent with a well-attested class of archaeological artifacts. Large stone vessels (*kalal*) have been found throughout Judea and Galilee, manufactured from soft limestone (chalk) on lathes. Production centers have been identified at Hizma (near Yerushalayim (Jerusalem)) and at several Galilean sites. The preference for stone over clay is grounded in Jewish purity law: Mishnah Kelim 10.1 states that stone vessels do not become ritually impure (*tum'ah*), unlike clay pots, which become permanently impure when contaminated and must be broken (Lev 11:33). Stone vessels thus served the purity-conscious Jewish household by providing reusable containers that could never become ritually unclean. John specifies the jars were "according to the purification of the Yehudim," connecting the vessels to the Jewish purity system, the very system the sign is about to reshape.

**Source:** Magen, Y., *The Stone Vessel Industry in the Second Temple Period*, Judea and Samaria Publications 1, Israel Antiquities Authority, 2002; Deines, R., *Jüdische Steingefässe und pharisäische Frömmigkeit*, WUNT 2/52, Mohr Siebeck, 1993.

### SCENARIO A — Pre-70 CE (Temple still standing)

#### IA-1. Economy — Temple commerce, stone vessel industry, and festival pilgrimage
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The two scenes of John 2 — the wedding at Qanah (vv.1–11) and the temple clearing (vv.13–22) — sit within distinct but connected economic worlds. At Qanah, the stone water jars (2:6) represent a specialized industry serving Jewish purity needs. Large chalk-stone vessels (*kalal*) were manufactured at workshops near Jerusalem and in the Galilee, valued because stone (unlike pottery) cannot contract ritual impurity (Mishnah Kelim 10.1). Their presence at a Galilean wedding reflects both purity observance and the reach of a trade network centered on Jerusalem. At the Temple, the commerce Yeshua (Jesus) disrupts is the essential infrastructure of pilgrimage Judaism: the half-shekel tax required Tyrian silver (exchanged at a fee by money-changers, the *kolbon* discussed in Mishnah Shekalim 1.6–7); sacrificial animals had to be ritually certified; and all of this was concentrated in the one place pilgrims had traveled to reach. Wine trade also connected these worlds — Galilee produced wine that moved south to Jerusalem and out through Phoenician ports.

**Source:** Magen, Y., *The Stone Vessel Industry in the Second Temple Period*, Israel Antiquities Authority, 2002; Sanders, E.P., *Judaism: Practice and Belief, 63 BCE–66 CE*, SCM Press, 1992.

#### IA-2. Daily Life — Purification customs, festival pilgrimage, and the mikveh network
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Passover (2:13) was one of the *shalosh regalim*, the three pilgrimage festivals requiring attendance at Jerusalem. The journey, the crowding, and the combination of impurity risks (contact with death, various discharges, non-kosher objects) along the way made the mikveh network essential for pilgrims arriving in Jerusalem. Stepped immersion pools have been excavated by the dozens at the south end of the Temple Mount (the main pilgrim entry point), confirming that large-scale purification was expected before entering the Temple precincts. John 2:6's six stone jars "according to the purification of the Yehudim" situate the Qanah wedding within the same purity culture that would drive pilgrims to immerse before ascending to the Temple. The chapter moves between these two sites of purification — domestic (water jars at the wedding) and institutional (the Temple courts) — and Yeshua (Jesus) acts at both.

**Source:** Reich, R., *Miqwa'ot*, Israel Exploration Society, 2013; Jeremias, J., *Jerusalem in the Time of Jesus*, Fortress Press, 1969.

#### IA-3. Religion — The Temple as YHWH's house and the question of zeal
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The disciples' recollection after the temple clearing — "zeal for your house will consume me" (2:17, citing Psalm 69:9) — places the action within a tradition of prophetic critique of the Temple establishment. Prophetic critique of Temple commerce was not novel: Jeremiah 7:11 ("a den of robbers"), Zechariah 14:21 ("there shall no longer be a trader in the house of YHWH"). The Qumran community withdrew from the Temple precisely because they considered its priesthood corrupt. What distinguishes Yeshua (Jesus)'s action is its embodied, public, physical character — he acts within the temple complex itself, not from a distance. The pre-70 reader encounters this scene with the Temple still standing: the challenge is live, the institution is real, and the stakes of questioning it are immediate.

**Source:** Wright, N.T., *Jesus and the Victory of God*, Fortress Press, 1996; Sanders, E.P., *Jesus and Judaism*, Fortress Press, 1985.

#### IA-4. Social Structure — Wedding honor culture and the chief steward's role
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The figure of the ἀρχιτρίκλινος (*architriklinos*, "chief steward" or "master of the feast," 2:8–9) reflects Greco-Roman banquet customs: a designated person, often a friend rather than a servant, managed the meal, controlled the wine mixing (wine was almost always diluted with water in ancient Mediterranean custom, typically 1:3 wine to water or 1:2 for stronger mixes), and adjudicated disputes about quality and service. The statement "everyone serves the good wine first, and when people have drunk freely, the inferior one" (2:10) is a realistic description of ancient banquet logic — the palate dulls, and guests become less discriminating. By reversing this — producing the best wine last — the sign inverts normal social expectation. Honor runs the opposite direction: the host's reputation is not rescued quietly but vindicated publicly by a guest's act.

**Source:** Smith, D.E., *From Symposium to Eucharist: The Banquet in the Early Christian World*, Fortress Press, 2003; Safrai, S. and Stern, M. (eds.), *The Jewish People in the First Century*, vol. 2, Van Gorcum, 1976.

#### IA-7. Arts — Galilean stone vessel industry as purity-driven craft
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The six stone water jars at Qanah (Cana) (2:6: "for the purification rites of the Yehudim") are an attested industrial product of Late Second Temple Galilee, not merely a narrative prop. Archaeological surveys at Hizma, Reina (near Nazareth), and the Mount Scopus / Mount Hatzofim workshop have documented stone-vessel production sites where soft limestone (qatlīt) was lathed into kraters, mugs, dishes, and large jars. The craft was driven by Pharisaic halakhic rulings that stone vessels — unlike pottery — are insusceptible to ritual impurity (m. Kelim 10:1; later codified in Mishnah, but the underlying ruling is attested in earlier Second Temple practice). Demand for stone vessels declined sharply after 70 CE as the Temple-centered purity system collapsed; the industry effectively ended by the mid-2nd century. The Qanah jars in John 2 are therefore precisely datable cultural-material artifacts of pre-70 Galilee — products of an industry that lasted barely a century. The narrative's specification that they hold "two or three *metretai* each" (roughly 75–115 liters) matches the largest jar sizes documented archaeologically, suggesting Cana was a venue with substantial purity-water needs (likely a Pharisaic-aligned household preparing for festival pilgrimage to Yerushalayim). **Source:** Magen, Y., *The Stone Vessel Industry in the Second Temple Period: Excavations at Hizma and the Jerusalem Temple Mount*, Israel Antiquities Authority, 2002; Deines, R., *Jüdische Steingefäße und pharisäische Frömmigkeit*, Mohr Siebeck, 1993; Reed, J.L., *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, Trinity Press International, 2000, ch. 3. **[PEER-REVIEWED — VERIFIED]**

### SCENARIO B — Post-70 CE (Temple destroyed)

#### IB-1. Religion — The Temple clearing read after the Temple's destruction
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The temple clearing in John 2:13–22, placed at the beginning of Jesus's ministry rather than (as in the Synoptics) at the end, carries particular force for post-70 readers. The disciples' retrospective understanding — "they remembered... after he was raised from the dead" (2:22) — mirrors the post-70 community's own retrospective position. The Temple they knew is gone; the body of Yeshua (Jesus) as the new *naos* (dwelling of God) has become the operative claim. For the community that composed or received John's Gospel after 70 CE, Yeshua's replacement-of-the-Temple theology was not a future prediction but a present reality: there was no other Temple. The offer of his body as the dwelling place of God was the only category left. The question "Destroy this sanctuary, and in three days I will raise it up" (2:19) is read not as a challenge to a still-standing building but as an explanation for the building's permanent absence.

**Source:** Coloe, M.L., *God Dwells with Us: Temple Symbolism in the Fourth Gospel*, Liturgical Press, 2001; Kerr, A.R., *The Temple of Jesus' Body: The Temple Theme in the Gospel of John*, JSNT Supplement 220, Sheffield Academic Press, 2002.

#### IB-2. Economy — Wine trade and the Galilean economy after the War
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The Galilean wine industry survived the Jewish War of 66–70 CE more intact than the Judean economy. Galilee had been the first theater of the war (Josephus commanded there), but its towns were not destroyed on the scale of Jerusalem and the surrounding region. The wine trade continued, connecting Galilean vineyards to Phoenician ports and Mediterranean markets. For post-70 readers, the wedding at Qanah in a prosperous Galilean household represents a world that still functioned — in contrast to the devastated Judean economy centered on the now-absent Temple. The stone vessel industry, however, had its production centers near Jerusalem, and the disruption to that supply chain would have been real. Post-70 purity practice adapted: without the Temple's graded purity zones, the relevance of large-scale stone vessel production shifted. **[PROBABLE that post-70 readers would note the contrast between the functioning Galilean social world and the absent Jerusalem world.]**

**Source:** Freyne, S., *Galilee, Jesus, and the Gospels*, Fortress Press, 1988; Safrai, Z., *The Economy of Roman Palestine*, Routledge, 1994.

#### IB-3. Daily Life — Purification without the Temple
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

After 70 CE, the elaborate purity architecture of the Jerusalem Temple — its graduated courts, its required immersions, its priestly oversight — no longer existed. Rabbinic authorities faced the question of how to maintain purity practice in a world without the Temple's spatial logic. The Mishnah tractates on purity (Tohorot, the largest of the six Mishnah orders) preserved the legal framework even though the Temple context for much of it was gone. Mikveh practice continued and was extended. The stone vessel industry effectively became obsolete as a Temple-purity enterprise, though stone vessels continued in use. For a post-70 reader, the six stone jars "according to the purification of the Yehudim" (2:6) are artifacts of a purity system that is now being reconstructed from memory and legal text, not lived practice at the Temple. The sign at Qanah — water for purification transformed into wine for celebration — reads as a comment on a world where the old purity architecture has been replaced.

**Source:** Neusner, J., *A History of the Mishnaic Law of Purities*, Brill, 1974–1977; Cohen, S.J.D., *From the Maccabees to the Mishnah*, 3rd ed., Westminster John Knox, 2014.
