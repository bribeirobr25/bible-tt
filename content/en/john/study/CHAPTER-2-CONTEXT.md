## A. Greek Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. *Sēmeion* as "sign" — John's distinctive vocabulary
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 2:11: ταύτην ἐποίησεν ἀρχὴν τῶν σημείων ὁ Ἰησοῦς — "this beginning of the signs Yeshua did." The noun σημεῖον (*sēmeion*) = "sign" — something that points beyond itself to a signified reality. The Synoptic Gospels use δύναμις (*dynamis*, "power/mighty work") as their primary term for Yeshua's extraordinary acts (Matt 11:20, Mark 6:2, Luke 10:13). John never uses *dynamis* for Yeshua's acts. The terminological choice is consistent and deliberate: John's *sēmeia* (signs) are not displays of raw power but revelatory pointers — they "reveal his glory" (2:11), disclose who Yeshua is, and invite trust. The traditional English rendering "miracle" collapses the distinction between John's *sēmeion* (revelatory pointer) and the Synoptic *dynamis* (powerful act). The TT preserves John's term.

### A2. *Naos* versus *hieron* — the two temples
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 2:14 uses ἱερόν (*hieron*) for the temple complex where commerce occurs. John 2:19–21 uses ναός (*naos*) for the sanctuary Yeshua tells them to destroy. The distinction is consistent in the New Testament: *hieron* (from ἱερός, "sacred") = the entire sacred precinct, including all courts, porticoes, and external areas; *naos* (from ναίω, "to dwell") = the inner sanctuary where the divine presence dwells. In the Jerusalem temple, the *naos* was the structure containing the Holy Place and the Holy of Holies — accessible only to priests and (in the innermost chamber) only to the high priest once per year. The shift from *hieron* to *naos* is theologically loaded: Yeshua disrupts the *hieron* (the institutional complex) but offers his body as the *naos* (the dwelling place of God). The TT marks this by rendering *hieron* as "temple complex" and *naos* as "sanctuary."

### A3. "My hour has not yet come" — the *hōra* motif
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 2:4: οὔπω ἥκει ἡ ὥρα μου (*oupō hēkei hē hōra mou*) — "my hour has not yet come." The "hour" (*hōra*) functions as a technical term in the Fourth Gospel, appearing at pivotal moments: 2:4 (not yet come), 7:30 and 8:20 (no one seized him because his hour had not yet come), 12:23 ("the hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified"), 12:27 ("for this purpose I came to this hour"), 13:1 ("knowing that his hour had come to depart from this world to the Father"), 17:1 ("Father, the hour has come"). The trajectory moves from "not yet" to "has come" — from Qanah to the cross. The *hōra* is the appointed time of Yeshua's death, glorification, and return to the Father. At the wedding, the hour has not yet arrived — yet Yeshua acts anyway. The relationship between the "not yet" and the action that follows is deliberately unexplained.

### A4. Water-to-wine — the absent mechanism
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 2:9: τὸ ὕδωρ οἶνον γεγενημένον (*to hydōr oinon gegenēmenon*) — "the water having become wine." The perfect participle presents the transformation as an accomplished fact. The narrator describes neither the moment nor the method of transformation. No gesture, no prayer, no formula, no touch is recorded. The text moves from "fill the jars with water" (v.7) to "draw out now" (v.8) to "the water having become wine" (v.9). The transformation happens in the narrative gap — between command and discovery. This narrative reticence is characteristic of John's sign accounts: the focus is not on the act of power but on its revelatory significance ("revealed his glory," v.11).

### A5. The narrator's retrospective in vv.21–22
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

John 2:21–22 contains two layers of retrospective interpretation. First, the narrator explains (v.21) that Yeshua was speaking about "the sanctuary of his body" — an explanation unavailable to the characters within the story. Second, the narrator reports (v.22) that the followers understood only after Yeshua was raised from the dead, at which point they "remembered" and "trusted the scripture and the word." This two-stage hermeneutic — event → retrospective understanding — is a structural feature of the Fourth Gospel (cf. 12:16, "his followers did not understand these things at first, but when Yeshua was glorified, then they remembered"). The TT preserves the narrator's temporal markers without collapsing the gap between event and understanding.

## B. Ancient Parallels

### B1. Dionysus and wine miracle traditions
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

The transformation of water into wine has parallels in Greco-Roman religious traditions, most notably in the cult of Dionysus (Roman: Bacchus), the god of wine and ecstasy. Several accounts describe miraculous wine production associated with Dionysian festivals: Euripides (*Bacchae* 704–707) describes wine flowing spontaneously from the ground at the touch of a Bacchic thyrsus. Pliny the Elder (*Natural History* 2.231, 31.16) reports traditions of springs flowing with wine on January 5–6 (later the Christian feast of Epiphany) on the islands of Teos and Andros. Pausanias (*Description of Greece* 6.26.1–2) records a tradition at Elis in which empty vessels placed in a sealed room were found full of wine on the feast day. Whether John's audience would have recognized the Qanah sign as a deliberate counterpoint to Dionysian claims is debated. Some scholars (C.H. Dodd, R. Bultmann) argued that John appropriates and supersedes the Dionysian tradition — the true vine-god replaces the mythological one. Others (C.K. Barrett, D.A. Carson) argue that the Jewish context (wedding, purification jars, Galilee) is sufficient without recourse to Hellenistic parallels. The verbal overlap is limited; the structural parallel (a divine figure producing wine miraculously) is real but generic.

**Source:** Dodd, C.H., *The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel*, Cambridge University Press, 1953; Bultmann, R., *The Gospel of John: A Commentary*, trans. Beasley-Murray, Westminster, 1971; Barrett, C.K., *The Gospel According to St John*, 2nd ed., SPCK, 1978.

## C. Historical and Archaeological Context

### C1. Qanah — location and archaeology
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Two sites compete for identification with biblical Qanah: (1) Kafr Kanna, a village approximately 6 km northeast of Natseret on the road to Tiberias, which has been the traditional pilgrimage site since at least the Crusader period; and (2) Khirbet Qana, a ruin approximately 14 km north of Natseret on a hilltop overlooking the Bet Netofa Valley. Excavations at Khirbet Qana (directed by Douglas Edwards, University of Puget Sound, 1998–2009) uncovered a substantial Jewish village with stone vessel fragments, miqva'ot (ritual baths), and evidence of habitation from the Hellenistic through Byzantine periods. A cave system beneath the village shows signs of veneration as the site of the water-to-wine sign from the late Roman period onward. The toponymic match (Khirbet *Qana* preserves the consonants of the biblical name) and the archaeological profile favor Khirbet Qana over Kafr Kanna, though certainty is not possible.

**Source:** Edwards, D.R., "Khirbet Qana: From Jewish Village to Christian Pilgrim Site," in *The Roman and Byzantine Near East*, vol. 3, JRA Supplement 49, 2002; Richardson, P., *Building Jewish in the Roman East*, Baylor University Press, 2004.

### C2. Wedding customs in first-century Galilee
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Jewish weddings in first-century Galilee typically lasted seven days (cf. Judges 14:12, Tobit 11:19). The host family was responsible for providing food and wine for the entire celebration. Running out of wine was a serious social failure — it brought shame on the family and could generate legal liability (in rabbinic sources, inadequate wedding provision is discussed in terms of communal obligation). The chief steward (*architriklinos*) managed the feast, controlled the wine service, and ensured hospitality standards. Stone vessels were preferred for ritual purity reasons (Mishnah Kelim 10.1: stone vessels do not contract impurity). The six stone jars described in John 2:6, each holding 2–3 *metrētai* (approximately 75–115 liters each), are consistent with the stone vessel industry attested archaeologically in Judea and Galilee — large stone vessels (called *kalal* in rabbinic Hebrew) manufactured primarily at workshops near Yerushalayim and in the Galilee.

**Source:** Safrai, S. and Stern, M., eds., *The Jewish People in the First Century*, vol. 2, Van Gorcum, 1976; Reed, J.L., *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, Trinity Press International, 2000.

### C3. The temple economy and money-changing
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The temple in Yerushalayim functioned as both a religious center and an economic institution. Pilgrims needed to purchase sacrificial animals (cattle, sheep, doves) that met the standards of ritual fitness (*temimt*, unblemished). Money-changers (*shulchani* in rabbinic sources, *kermatistēs* or *kollybistēs* in Greek) were necessary because the half-shekel temple tax (Exod 30:13) had to be paid in Tyrian silver — the only coinage of sufficient purity accepted by temple authorities. Tyrian shekels contained approximately 14 grams of silver at 94% purity. The exchange rate included a surcharge (*kolbon*), which is discussed in Mishnah Shekalim 1.6–7. Whether the commerce Yeshua disrupted was inherently exploitative or simply commercially necessary is debated by historians. Josephus describes the temple's vast economic infrastructure (*Antiquities* 20.219–222; *War* 5.184–227). Archaeological evidence from the Herodian street south of the Temple Mount — excavated by Benjamin Mazar (1968–1978) — includes shops and commercial installations consistent with a temple-centered market economy.

**Source:** Mazar, B., *The Mountain of the Lord*, Doubleday, 1975; Sanders, E.P., *Judaism: Practice and Belief, 63 BCE–66 CE*, SCM Press, 1992; Bauckham, R., "Jesus' Demonstration in the Temple," in *Law and Religion*, ed. B. Lindars, James Clarke, 1988.

### C4. Passover timing and pilgrim traffic
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Passover (*Pesach*) fell on 14–15 Nisan (March–April). As one of the three pilgrimage festivals (*shalosh regalim*: Pesach, Shavuot, Sukkot), it drew massive crowds to Yerushalayim. Josephus estimates Passover attendance at various points as 2.5 to 3 million (*War* 6.425) — likely exaggerated, but even conservative estimates suggest 300,000–500,000 pilgrims in a city with a normal population of perhaps 40,000–80,000. The influx created enormous logistical demands: accommodation, food, sacrificial animals, currency exchange, water, and sanitation. The temple operations that Yeshua disrupted were integral to managing this annual surge. John's placement of the temple clearing at Passover — the festival commemorating liberation from Egypt — may carry ironic force: the institution celebrating freedom has become an institution of commerce.

**Source:** Jeremias, J., *Jerusalem in the Time of Jesus*, Fortress Press, 1969; Safrai, S., "Pilgrimage to Jerusalem at the Time of the Second Temple," in *Die Pilgerfahrt nach Jerusalem*, ed. W. Böhme, Calwer Verlag, 1991.

## D. Linguistic and Philological Deep Dives

### D1. τί ἐμοὶ καὶ σοί — the Semitic disengagement idiom
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The phrase τί ἐμοὶ καὶ σοί (*ti emoi kai soi*) = "what to me and to you?" is a calque (loan-translation) of the Hebrew מַה־לִּי וָלָךְ (*mah-li valakh*). The idiom appears in the Hebrew Bible in several contexts: Judges 11:12 (Yiftach to the king of Ammon — military confrontation), 2 Samuel 16:10 and 19:23 (David to the sons of Tseruyah — "what do I have in common with you?"), 1 Kings 17:18 (the widow of Tsarfat to Eliyahu — distress and accusation), 2 Kings 3:13 (Elisha to the king of Yisrael — refusal of involvement), 2 Chronicles 35:21 (Pharaoh Nekho to King Yoshiyahu — "what quarrel is between us?"). The range of usage spans from mild disengagement ("this is not my concern") to sharp rebuff ("leave me alone"). In every case, the idiom creates distance between the speaker and the addressee. In John 2:4, Yeshua uses it to establish a boundary — his action will proceed on his own terms and timing, not on his mother's initiative. Translations that soften the idiom ("What concern is that to you and to me?" — NRSV) or redirect it ("Why do you involve me?" — NIV) obscure the Semitic formula. The TT renders literally: "What to me and to you, woman?"

### D2. γύναι — "woman" as vocative address
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The vocative γύναι (*gynai*) is used by Yeshua to address his mother both at Qanah (2:4) and at the cross (19:26). In classical and Koine Greek, *gynai* was a respectful form of address — Odysseus addresses Penelope as *gynai* (Homer, *Odyssey* 23.174); the term carries no inherent disrespect. However, the social context matters: no known parallel exists in ancient Greek, Latin, or Jewish literature of a son addressing his biological mother as "woman" in a face-to-face conversation. The address is not rude but it is distancing — it redefines the relationship from the familial (mother-son) to something else. In John's narrative, the address appears at the two moments when Yeshua acts in his public/revelatory capacity rather than as a family member. Whether this signals a theological point (his mission supersedes family bonds) or reflects compositional convention is debated. The TT preserves the address without domesticating it.

**Source:** Brown, R.E., *The Gospel According to John I–XII*, Anchor Bible 29, Doubleday, 1966; Moloney, F.J., *The Gospel of John*, Sacra Pagina 4, Liturgical Press, 1998.

## E. Scientific Correspondence and Non-Correspondence

### E1. Wine fermentation — the text describes transformation, not creation from nothing
**[SCIENTIFIC COMPARISON — POSSIBLE]**

Natural wine production involves the fermentation of grape sugars by yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*), converting glucose and fructose into ethanol and carbon dioxide over a period of days to weeks. The process is biochemical: enzymes produced by yeast catalyze the breakdown of sugars in an anaerobic environment. The Qanah sign describes water becoming wine — a transformation that bypasses the entire biological process of viticulture, harvesting, crushing, and fermentation. The text does not describe creation *ex nihilo* (from nothing) — water already exists as the substrate. It describes transformation of one substance into another. Whether one frames this as a compression of natural processes or as a categorical discontinuity with natural processes depends on one's metaphysical framework, not on the text itself. The text describes the result ("the water having become wine") without theorizing about mechanism. Modern chemistry can describe what wine is (a complex solution of ethanol, water, organic acids, tannins, anthocyanins, and hundreds of aromatic compounds); it cannot describe a mechanism for the instantaneous conversion of water into wine. The TT notes the gap between the described result and any known natural process without attempting to fill it.

## F. Later Reception

### F1. Marian theology and the wedding at Qanah
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox traditions have drawn extensive theological conclusions from Mary's role at Qanah. The Council of Ephesus (431 CE) defined Mary as Theotokos ("God-bearer"), and while this definition does not depend on John 2, the Qanah narrative has been read as supporting Mary's intercessory role: she presents a human need to her son, and despite the apparent refusal, the need is met. Later Marian theology (particularly in Counter-Reformation Catholicism) developed the image of Mary as mediatrix — one who intercedes with Christ on behalf of humanity. Pope John Paul II's encyclical *Redemptoris Mater* (1987) interprets the Qanah episode as the paradigm of Mary's maternal mediation. Protestant interpreters have generally resisted this reading, arguing that the text shows Yeshua establishing independence from his mother's initiative ("What to me and to you?"), not affirming her mediating role. The TT notes: the text records the mother's statement, Yeshua's distancing response, and the mother's instruction to the servants. It does not use the word "intercession" or describe the mother's role in mediatorial terms. The theological framework is later reception, not text.

**Source:** Brown, R.E., et al., *Mary in the New Testament*, Fortress Press, 1978; Gaventa, B.R., *Mary: Glimpses of the Mother of Jesus*, University of South Carolina Press, 1995.

### F2. Sacramental reading of water-to-wine
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

From at least the third century CE, Christian interpreters have read the water-to-wine sign as a prefiguration of the Eucharist. Cyprian of Carthage (*Epistle* 63.12–13, c. 253 CE) argued that the wine at Qanah represented the blood of Christ in the eucharistic cup. The medieval liturgical tradition associated the Qanah miracle with Epiphany (January 6) — the manifestation of divine glory — linking it to the baptism of Jesus and the visit of the Magi as three "manifestations." The Fourth Gospel itself may support a sacramental reading at other points (6:53–56, the bread of life discourse), but at 2:1–11 the text does not mention blood, covenant, or ritual consumption. The sacramental reading is an interpretive tradition, not a textual datum. The TT records the sign without sacramental overlay.

**Source:** Cullmann, O., *Early Christian Worship*, SCM Press, 1953; Koester, C.R., *Symbolism in the Fourth Gospel*, 2nd ed., Fortress Press, 2003.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. Why is Yeshua's mother unnamed in John?
**[POSSIBLE INFERENCE — POSSIBLE]**

The Fourth Gospel refers to Yeshua's mother four times (2:1, 2:3, 2:5, 2:12; 6:42; 19:25–27) but never gives her name. The Synoptics identify her as Μαριάμ/Μαρία (Mariam/Maria — Matt 1:16, 2:11, 13:55; Mark 6:3; Luke 1:27, etc.). Several explanations have been proposed: (1) The author assumes the reader already knows her name from other traditions. (2) The anonymity is theologically motivated — in John, she functions relationally (as mother of Yeshua) rather than as an independent character. (3) The author belongs to a tradition in which her personal name was not central to the narrative. (4) The anonymity creates a literary effect — she is defined entirely by her relationship to Yeshua, which is itself redefined at the cross (19:26–27) when Yeshua gives her to the beloved disciple as mother. None of these explanations excludes the others. The TT follows the text: "the mother of Yeshua."

### G2. Six stone jars — ritual purification vessels
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The six stone water jars (λίθιναι ὑδρίαι, *lithinai hydriai*) described in 2:6 are consistent with a well-attested class of archaeological artifacts. Large stone vessels (*kalal*) have been found throughout Judea and Galilee, manufactured from soft limestone (chalk) on lathes. Production centers have been identified at Hizma (near Yerushalayim) and at several Galilean sites. The preference for stone over clay is halakhically grounded: Mishnah Kelim 10.1 states that stone vessels do not contract ritual impurity (*tum'ah*), unlike earthenware, which becomes permanently impure upon contamination and must be broken (Lev 11:33). Stone vessels thus served the purity-conscious Jewish household by providing reusable containers that could never become ritually unclean. The fact that John specifies the jars were "according to the purification of the Yehudim" connects the vessels to the Jewish purity system — the very system the sign is about to recontextualize.

**Source:** Magen, Y., *The Stone Vessel Industry in the Second Temple Period*, Judea and Samaria Publications 1, Israel Antiquities Authority, 2002; Deines, R., *Jüdische Steingefässe und pharisäische Frömmigkeit*, WUNT 2/52, Mohr Siebeck, 1993.
