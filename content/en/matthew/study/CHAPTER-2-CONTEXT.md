## A. Source Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. Formula quotations -- "so that what was spoken might be fulfilled"
**[TEXTUAL -- VERIFIED]**

Matthew 2 contains four OT quotations, each introduced by a formula. The formulas are not identical:

| Verse | Formula | Source | Type |
|-------|---------|--------|------|
| 2:5-6 | "thus it has been written through the prophet" | Micah 5:1 + 2 Sam 5:2 | Scripture citation by characters |
| 2:15 | "so that what was spoken by the Lord through the prophet might be fulfilled" | Hosea 11:1 | Purpose clause (*hina*) -- narrator's comment |
| 2:17-18 | "then was fulfilled what was spoken through Yirmeyahu the prophet" | Jeremiah 31:15 | Temporal clause (*tote*) -- narrator's comment |
| 2:23 | "so that what was spoken through the prophets might be fulfilled" | No single source | Purpose clause (*hina*) -- narrator's comment |

The TT preserves the differences in formula wording. The shift from purpose clause (*hina*, "so that") at 2:15 and 2:23 to temporal clause (*tote*, "then") at 2:17 is significant: the massacre is not presented as divinely purposed to fulfill Jeremiah -- it is an event that, having occurred, corresponds to the prophetic lament. The distinction between "the prophet" (singular, 2:15, 2:17) and "the prophets" (plural, 2:23) is also preserved.

### A2. Four OT citations -- modified and composite
**[TEXTUAL -- VERIFIED]**

None of Matthew's four OT quotations in chapter 2 matches the source text exactly:

- **2:6 (Micah 5:1):** Reverses "too small to be among the clans" to "by no means least among the rulers." Adds "who will shepherd my people Yisra'el" from 2 Samuel 5:2. A composite quotation.
- **2:15 (Hosea 11:1):** Close to the source but applies a national-retrospective text (Israel called out of Mitsrayim) to an individual (Yeshua called out of Mitsrayim).
- **2:18 (Jeremiah 31:15):** Relatively close to the source. The application shifts from exilic deportation to infant massacre.
- **2:23 ("the prophets"):** No identifiable single OT verse. The formula itself acknowledges this by citing "the prophets" (plural) rather than a named prophet.

The TT translates each quotation as Matthew presents it, noting the differences from the source texts without harmonizing or explaining away the modifications. Second Temple Jewish citation practice regularly adapted, combined, and recontextualized scriptural texts.

### A3. *Tote eplērōthē* vs. *hina plērōthē* -- two kinds of fulfillment
**[TEXTUAL -- VERIFIED]**

The fulfillment formula at 2:17 uses **τότε ἐπληρώθη** (*tote eplērōthē*) = "then was fulfilled" -- a simple temporal past tense. This differs from the purpose clause used at 1:22, 2:15, and 2:23: **ἵνα πληρωθῇ** (*hina plērōthē*) = "so that it might be fulfilled." The *hina* formulas present events as occurring for the purpose of fulfilling prophecy. The *tote* formula at 2:17 presents the correspondence as retrospective: the event happened, and then the prophetic text was seen to apply.

This distinction is theologically consequential. The massacre of children is not presented as divinely orchestrated to fulfill Jeremiah's words. It is presented as an act of human violence that, after the fact, resonates with an earlier prophetic lament.

### A4. The "Natsri" mystery -- no single OT source
**[TEXTUAL -- VERIFIED]**

Matthew 2:23 claims that Yeshua's settlement in Natseret fulfills "what was spoken through the prophets: 'He shall be called a Natsri.'" No known OT verse contains this exact phrase. The formula cites "the prophets" (plural), not a single prophet -- the only such usage in Matthew's fulfillment formulas. This plural attribution may signal that no single verse is being quoted but rather a thematic claim drawn from multiple sources.

Proposed connections:
- **נֵצֶר** (*netser*), "branch" (Isaiah 11:1): "A branch (*netser*) shall grow from the roots of Yishai." The phonetic similarity between *netser* and *Natsri* is the most commonly proposed link. The messianic "branch" tradition also appears in Jeremiah 23:5 and Zechariah 3:8, 6:12 (using the synonym **צֶמַח**, *tsemach*).
- **נָזִיר** (*nazir*), "Nazirite/consecrated one" (Judges 13:5, 7): Shimshon (Samson) is called a *nazir* from the womb. The phonetic overlap with *Nazōraios* is partial.
- A general theme of lowliness or despised status associated with Natseret (cf. John 1:46, "Can anything good come from Natseret?").

The TT translates the text's claim without resolving the source question. The mystery is part of the text.

## B. Ancient Near Eastern and Ancient World Parallels

### B1. Threatened-infant-king motif
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL -- VERIFIED]**

The pattern of a king threatening infant lives and one child surviving to fulfill a great destiny is attested across ancient literature:

- **Moses and Pharaoh** (Exodus 1:15-2:10): Pharaoh orders the killing of Hebrew male children. Moses is saved by being hidden and adopted by Pharaoh's daughter. Matthew's narrative presents the closest parallel: a ruler orders the killing of male children in a specific region; the chosen child escapes to/from Mitsrayim.
- **Sargon of Akkad** (ca. 2300 BCE): The Sargon birth legend describes a child of humble origins placed in a basket on a river, rescued, and raised to become king.
- **Romulus and Remus** (Roman foundation myth): Twin infants are ordered killed by a usurping king, survive exposure, and found Rome.
- **Perseus** (Greek mythology): King Acrisius, warned his grandson will kill him, sets the infant Perseus adrift.

The Moses parallel is the most relevant for Matthew's narrative. Matthew appears to present Yeshua's infancy as a recapitulation of Moses' story: a threatened child, a murderous ruler, a sojourn in Mitsrayim, a return after the death of "those seeking his life" (cf. Ex 4:19 and Matt 2:20).

**Source:** Pritchard, J.B. (ed.), *ANET*, 3rd ed., 1969, pp. 119 (Sargon). Redford, D.B., "The Literary Motif of the Exposed Child," *Numen* 14 (1967), pp. 209-228.

## C. Historical Context

### C1. Herodes' character in Josephus
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL -- VERIFIED]**

Josephus provides extensive documentation of Herodes' paranoia and violence:
- He executed his wife Mariamne I, her mother Alexandra, and three of his own sons (Alexander, Aristobulus, Antipater) on suspicion of conspiracy (*Antiquities* 15-17; *War* 1).
- Augustus reportedly quipped that it was better to be Herodes' pig (*hys*) than his son (*huios*) -- Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 2.4.11.
- His final illness and death (4 BCE) involved ordering the arrest of notable men who were to be executed at his death so that there would be mourning (*Antiquities* 17.6.5) -- though the order was not carried out.

The massacre at Beyt-Lechem, while unattested in Josephus, is consistent with Herodes' documented character. Given the village's small population (perhaps 300-1000), the number of male children under two would have been small (estimated 10-20) -- a localised atrocity that might not register in Josephus's broader catalogue of Herodian violence.

**Source:** Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities*, books 14-17, Loeb Classical Library. Josephus, *Jewish War*, book 1, Loeb Classical Library. Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, 2.4.11.

### C2. Beyt-Lechem -- size and significance
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL -- PROBABLE]**

Beyt-Lechem was a small agricultural settlement in the first century BCE. Archaeological evidence for the period is limited. The town's significance was primarily literary and traditional: David's city (1 Sam 16), the setting of Ruth, the location of Rachel's tomb (Gen 35:19). Micah 5:1 [5:2] identifies it as the origin of the future ruler of Yisra'el -- precisely the text cited by the chief priests and scribes in Matthew 2:5-6.

The small size of Beyt-Lechem is essential context for assessing the massacre account. A village of several hundred inhabitants would have had a very limited number of male children under two. The event, if it occurred, would have been devastating locally but invisible in wider historical records.

**Source:** Finkelstein, I. and Magen, Y. (eds.), *Archaeological Survey of the Hill Country of Benjamin*, 1993. Tsafrir, Y. et al., *Tabula Imperii Romani: Iudaea-Palaestina*, 1994.

### C3. Egyptian Jewish communities
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL -- VERIFIED]**

Jewish communities in Mitsrayim were well established by the first century BCE. The Elephantine papyri (5th century BCE) attest a Jewish military colony in upper Egypt with its own temple. The LXX was produced in Alexandria (3rd-2nd century BCE), indicating a large Greek-speaking Jewish population. Philo of Alexandria (ca. 20 BCE - 50 CE) is the most prominent representative. The temple at Leontopolis (established ca. 160 BCE) served Egyptian Jews.

A Jewish family fleeing to Mitsrayim was historically plausible -- Jewish communities existed there that could provide shelter and integration. The flight narrative does not require a miraculous provision in a foreign land; it requires a known destination with established diaspora infrastructure.

**Source:** Modrzejewski, J.M., *The Jews of Egypt: From Rameses II to Emperor Hadrian*, 1995. Tcherikover, V., *Hellenistic Civilization and the Jews*, 1959.

## F. Later Reception in Other Traditions

### F1. Flight to Egypt in Coptic tradition
**[LATER RECEPTION -- DOCUMENTED]**

The Coptic Orthodox Church preserves an elaborate tradition of the Holy Family's journey through Egypt. The *Maymar* (Homily) attributed to Theophilus of Alexandria (late 4th century) identifies specific sites where the family rested, including Hermopolis, Ashmunein, and Mount Qussqam (where the Muharraq Monastery stands). The Coptic liturgical calendar celebrates the "Entry of the Lord into Egypt" on June 1 (Bashans 24).

These traditions developed over centuries and reflect local Egyptian Christian piety. They cannot be verified historically but are important as cultural-religious reception history. The Matthew text provides no geographic details beyond "Mitsrayim."

**Source:** Gabra, G., "The Holy Family in Egypt," in *Christianity and Monasticism in Upper Egypt*, ed. Gabra and Takla, 2008.

### F2. "Natsri" etymology debate in later tradition
**[LATER RECEPTION -- DOCUMENTED]**

The designation *Nazōraios* / *Natsri* generated early debate:
- **Jerome** (4th-5th century): connected it to *netser* ("branch," Isa 11:1) in his commentary on Matthew.
- **Eusebius** (*Onomasticon*): identified Natseret geographically.
- **Epiphanius** (*Panarion*): described a pre-Christian Jewish sect called "Nasaraioi/Nazoraioi" -- though the reliability of this report is debated.
- In **rabbinic literature**, *notsri* (נוצרי) became the standard Hebrew term for "Christian," derived from Natseret. In modern Hebrew, *Natsrut* = Christianity.

The etymological question remains open in scholarship. No single solution has achieved consensus.

**Source:** France, R.T., *The Gospel of Matthew*, NICNT, 2007, pp. 93-95. Schaeder, H.H., "Ναζαρηνός, Ναζωραῖος," in *TDNT*, vol. 4, pp. 874-879.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. No biblical source for "He shall be called a Natsri"
**[TEXTUAL -- VERIFIED]**

This is the most distinctive feature of Matthew 2:23. Every other fulfillment formula in Matthew cites a known OT passage (often modified or combined, but identifiable). The "Natsri" quotation has no clear single source. The plural "the prophets" may be Matthew's signal that this is a thematic summary rather than a direct quotation. The mystery has generated centuries of scholarly discussion without resolution.

### G2. The star -- narrated without explanation
**[TEXTUAL -- VERIFIED]**

The star "goes before them" and "stands over" the place where the child is (v.9). The text narrates this behavior without explanation or astronomical comment. Various identifications have been proposed in modern discussion:
- A conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn in Pisces (7 BCE) -- calculated by Kepler in 1604
- A comet -- Halley's comet appeared in 12 BCE (too early for most chronologies)
- A supernova
- A non-natural phenomenon narrated within a theological framework

The text shows no interest in the mechanism. The star functions narratively as divine guidance for the *magoi*. The TT translates what the text says without scientific rationalization.

### G3. "Two years and under" -- timeline implications
**[STRONG INFERENCE -- PROBABLE]**

Herodes' order to kill children "from two years old and under, according to the time which he had determined from the *magoi*" (v.16) implies that the star appeared up to two years before the *magoi* arrive in Yerushalayim. This suggests an interval between the star's first appearance and the *magoi*'s arrival. Combined with the term *paidion* ("child," not "baby") at v.11, and the setting in a "house" (not a manger), the text places the *magoi*'s visit some time after the birth -- possibly months or even a year or more.
