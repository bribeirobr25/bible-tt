## A. Source Text Features Exposed by TT

### A1. Formula quotations — "so that what was spoken might be fulfilled"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Matthew 2 contains four OT quotations, each introduced by a formula. The formulas are not identical:

| Verse | Formula | Source | Type |
|-------|---------|--------|------|
| 2:5-6 | "thus it has been written through the prophet" | Micah 5:1 + 2 Sam 5:2 | Scripture citation by characters |
| 2:15 | "so that what was spoken by the Lord through the prophet might be fulfilled" | Hosea 11:1 | Purpose clause (*hina*) — narrator's comment |
| 2:17-18 | "then was fulfilled what was spoken through Yirmeyahu the prophet" | Jeremiah 31:15 | Temporal clause (*tote*) — narrator's comment |
| 2:23 | "so that what was spoken through the prophets might be fulfilled" | No single source | Purpose clause (*hina*) — narrator's comment |

The TT preserves the differences in formula wording. The shift from purpose clause (*hina*, "so that") at 2:15 and 2:23 to temporal clause (*tote*, "then") at 2:17 is significant. The massacre is not presented as divinely intended to fulfill Jeremiah. It is an event that, having occurred, corresponds to the prophetic lament. The distinction between "the prophet" (singular, 2:15, 2:17) and "the prophets" (plural, 2:23) is also preserved.

### A2. Four OT citations — modified and composite
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

None of Matthew's four OT quotations in chapter 2 matches the source text exactly:

- **2:6 (Micah 5:1):** Reverses "too small to be among the clans" to "by no means least among the rulers." Adds "who will shepherd my people Yisra'el" from 2 Samuel 5:2. A composite quotation.
- **2:15 (Hosea 11:1):** Close to the source but applies a national-retrospective text (Israel called out of Mitsrayim (Egypt)) to an individual (Yeshua (Jesus) called out of Egypt).
- **2:18 (Jeremiah 31:15):** Relatively close to the source. The application shifts from exilic deportation to infant massacre.
- **2:23 ("the prophets"):** No identifiable single OT verse. The formula itself acknowledges this by citing "the prophets" (plural) rather than a named prophet.

The TT translates each quotation as Matthew presents it, noting the differences from the source texts without smoothing over or explaining away the modifications. Jewish citation practice in the Second Temple period regularly adapted, combined, and reapplied scriptural texts in new contexts.

### A3. *Tote eplērōthē* vs. *hina plērōthē* — two kinds of fulfillment
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The fulfillment formula at 2:17 uses **τότε ἐπληρώθη** (*tote eplērōthē*) = "then was fulfilled" — a simple temporal past tense. This differs from the purpose clause used at 1:22, 2:15, and 2:23: **ἵνα πληρωθῇ** (*hina plērōthē*) = "so that it might be fulfilled." The *hina* formulas present events as occurring for the purpose of fulfilling prophecy. The *tote* formula at 2:17 presents the correspondence as retrospective: the event happened, and then the prophetic text was seen to apply.

This distinction matters theologically. The massacre of children is not presented as divinely orchestrated to fulfill Jeremiah's words. It is presented as an act of human violence that, after the fact, echoes an earlier prophetic lament.

### A4. The "Natsri" mystery — no single OT source
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

Matthew 2:23 claims that Yeshua (Jesus)'s settlement in Natseret (Nazareth) fulfills "what was spoken through the prophets: 'He shall be called a Natsri.'" No known OT verse contains this exact phrase. The formula cites "the prophets" (plural), not a single prophet — the only such usage in Matthew's fulfillment formulas. This plural attribution may signal that no single verse is being quoted but rather a thematic claim drawn from multiple sources.

Proposed connections:
- **נֵצֶר** (*netser*), "branch" (Isaiah 11:1): "A branch (*netser*) shall grow from the roots of Yishai." The phonetic similarity between *netser* and *Natsri* is the most commonly proposed link. The messianic "branch" tradition also appears in Jeremiah 23:5 and Zechariah 3:8, 6:12 (using the synonym **צֶמַח**, *tsemach*).
- **נָזִיר** (*nazir*), "Nazirite/consecrated one" (Judges 13:5, 7): Shimshon (Samson) is called a *nazir* from the womb. The phonetic overlap with *Nazōraios* is partial.
- A general theme of lowliness or despised status associated with Nazareth (cf. John 1:46, "Can anything good come from Nazareth?").

The TT translates the text's claim without resolving the source question. The mystery is part of the text.

## B. Ancient Near Eastern and Ancient World Parallels

### B1. Threatened-infant-king motif
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — VERIFIED]**

The pattern of a king threatening infant lives and one child surviving to fulfill a great destiny is attested across ancient literature:

- **Moses and Pharaoh** (Exodus 1:15-2:10): Pharaoh orders the killing of Hebrew male children. Moses is saved by being hidden and adopted by Pharaoh's daughter. Matthew's narrative presents the closest parallel: a ruler orders the killing of male children in a specific region; the chosen child escapes to/from Mitsrayim (Egypt).
- **Sargon of Akkad** (ca. 2300 BCE): The Sargon birth legend describes a child of humble origins placed in a basket on a river, rescued, and raised to become king.
- **Romulus and Remus** (Roman foundation myth): Twin infants are ordered killed by a usurping king, survive exposure, and found Rome.
- **Perseus** (Greek mythology): King Acrisius, warned his grandson will kill him, sets the infant Perseus adrift.

The Moses parallel is the most relevant for Matthew's narrative. Matthew appears to present Yeshua (Jesus)'s infancy as a retelling of Moses' story: a threatened child, a murderous ruler, a stay in Egypt, a return after the death of "those seeking his life" (cf. Ex 4:19 and Matt 2:20).

**Source:** Pritchard, J.B. (ed.), *ANET*, 3rd ed., 1969, pp. 119 (Sargon). Redford, D.B., "The Literary Motif of the Exposed Child," *Numen* 14 (1967), pp. 209-228.

---

### B2. Numbers 24:17 ("a star out of Jacob") read messianically — Qumran to Bar Kokhba
**[COMPARATIVE PARALLEL — PROBABLE]**

Balaam's oracle in **Numbers 24:17** — "a star shall come out of Jacob, and a scepter shall rise out of Israel" — was widely read as a **messianic** prophecy in Second Temple Judaism, well before and around Matthew's time. The Qumran community applied it to its expected deliverer(s) (the *Damascus Document* CD-A VII 18–20; the *War Scroll* 1QM XI; the *Testimonia*, 4Q175), and Philo gives it a royal-messianic rendering (*De praemiis et poenis* 95). Decades after Matthew, Rabbi Akiva is reported to have applied the same "star" oracle to Simon ben Kosiba, nicknaming him **Bar Kokhba**, "son of the star" (y. Ta'anit 4:5 [68d]).

So a *star* was a recognized sign of a coming royal deliverer in the Jewish world Matthew wrote within — useful background for why a star and "the king of the Yehudim" (2:2) belong together in the narrative. Two cautions: Matthew 2 **does not quote Numbers 24:17**, so the connection is **a shared interpretive milieu, not a demonstrated literary dependence**; and the Bar Kokhba application post-dates Matthew, illustrating the *currency* of the reading rather than serving as a source for the Gospel. (On the eastern-seer figure of Balaam himself, attested archaeologically, see §C4.)

**Source:** George H. van Kooten & Peter Barthel (eds.), *The Star of Bethlehem and the Magi* (Brill, 2015), esp. the essay on Balaam's star oracle in the Dead Sea Scrolls, pp. 399–429 [PEER-REVIEWED]; primary texts CD-A VII; 1QM XI; 4Q175; y. Ta'anit 4:5 [68d].

## C. Historical Context

### C1. Herodes' character in Josephus
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Josephus provides extensive documentation of Herodes (Herod)' paranoia and violence:
- He executed his wife Mariamne I, her mother Alexandra, and three of his own sons (Alexander, Aristobulus, Antipater) on suspicion of conspiracy (*Antiquities* 15-17; *War* 1).
- Augustus reportedly joked that it was better to be Herod' pig (*hys*) than his son (*huios*) (Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 2.4.11).
- His final illness and death (4 BCE) involved ordering the arrest of notable men who were to be executed at his death so that there would be mourning (*Antiquities* 17.6.5). The order was not carried out.

The massacre at Beyt-Lechem (Bethlehem), while not mentioned by Josephus, is consistent with Herod' documented character. Given the village's small population (perhaps 300-1000), the number of male children under two would have been small (estimated 10-20). A local atrocity of this scale might not have registered in Josephus's broader catalogue of Herodian violence.

**Source:** Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities*, books 14-17, Loeb Classical Library. Josephus, *Jewish War*, book 1, Loeb Classical Library. Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, 2.4.11.

### C2. Beyt-Lechem — size and significance
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Beyt-Lechem (Bethlehem) was a small agricultural settlement in the first century BCE. Archaeological evidence for the period is limited. The town's significance was primarily literary and traditional: David's city (1 Sam 16), the setting of Ruth, the location of Rachel's tomb (Gen 35:19). Micah 5:1 [5:2] identifies it as the origin of the future ruler of Yisra'el. This is precisely the text cited by the chief priests and scribes in Matthew 2:5-6.

The small size of Bethlehem is essential context for assessing the massacre account. A village of several hundred inhabitants would have had a very limited number of male children under two. The event, if it occurred, would have been devastating locally but invisible in wider historical records.

**Source:** Finkelstein, I. and Magen, Y. (eds.), *Archaeological Survey of the Hill Country of Benjamin*, 1993. Tsafrir, Y. et al., *Tabula Imperii Romani: Iudaea-Palaestina*, 1994.

### C3. Egyptian Jewish communities
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Jewish communities in Mitsrayim (Egypt) were well established by the first century BCE. The Elephantine papyri (5th century BCE) attest a Jewish military colony in upper Egypt with its own temple. The LXX (the ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible) was produced in Alexandria (3rd-2nd century BCE), indicating a large Greek-speaking Jewish population. Philo of Alexandria (ca. 20 BCE - 50 CE) is the most prominent representative. The temple at Leontopolis (established ca. 160 BCE) served Egyptian Jews.

A Jewish family fleeing to Egypt was historically plausible. Jewish communities existed there that could provide shelter and help with settling in. The flight narrative does not require miraculous provision in a foreign land. It requires a known destination with established Jewish communities abroad.

**Source:** Modrzejewski, J.M., *The Jews of Egypt: From Rameses II to Emperor Hadrian*, 1995. Tcherikover, V., *Hellenistic Civilization and the Jews*, 1959.

### C4. The Deir 'Alla inscription — "Balaam son of Beor" outside the Bible
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

In 1967, excavators at Tell Deir 'Alla in the Jordan Valley (ancient Transjordan) recovered an ink-on-plaster text, ca. 880–770 BCE, fallen from a wall. It opens by naming **"Balaam son of Beor, a seer of the gods"** (*bil'am bar be'or*) — the same Balaam son of Beor who appears in Numbers 22–24 as the non-Israelite seer hired to curse Israel. This is one of very few extrabiblical attestations of a named Hebrew Bible figure, and it shows that a **Transjordanian tradition about a seer named Balaam** circulated independently of the biblical text.

Two cautions are essential. **First, the inscription does not contain the "star out of Jacob" oracle** (Numbers 24:17) — or any star: it attests the existence of a Balaam-seer tradition, not the specific oracle later associated with him. **Second**, its religious world is polytheistic (it invokes the *shaddayin* and *elohin* as a council of gods), and its dialect (Aramaic vs. a local Canaanite/"Gileadite" form) is debated; it is an independent regional text, not an Israelite one, and is not connected to any modern people, nation, or place.

The relevance to Matthew 2 is **comparative, not a line of dependence**: Matthew's *magoi* are non-Israelite seers "from the East" who read a celestial sign — broadly the *type* that the eastern seer Balaam represents in the Hebrew Bible. The Deir 'Alla text simply confirms that such a Balaam-seer figure was a real, anciently-rooted Transjordanian tradition; it says nothing about Matthew or about a star. (How Balaam's *star oracle* in Numbers 24:17 was later read messianically is a separate reception strand, not attested here.)

**Source:** J. Hoftijzer & G. van der Kooij, *Aramaic Texts from Deir 'Alla* (Brill, 1976) [*editio princeps*]; Baruch A. Levine, "The Deir 'Alla Plaster Inscriptions," *Journal of the American Oriental Society* 101.2 (1981): 195–205 [PEER-REVIEWED]; inscription = KAI 312.

## F. Later Reception in Other Traditions

### F1. Flight to Egypt in Coptic tradition
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The Coptic Orthodox Church preserves an elaborate tradition of the Holy Family's journey through Egypt. The *Maymar* (Homily) attributed to Theophilus of Alexandria (late 4th century) identifies specific sites where the family rested, including Hermopolis, Ashmunein, and Mount Qussqam (where the Muharraq Monastery stands). The Coptic liturgical calendar celebrates the "Entry of the Lord into Egypt" on June 1 (Bashans 24).

These traditions developed over centuries and reflect local Egyptian Christian devotion. They cannot be verified historically but are important as evidence of how later communities received and expanded the narrative. The Matthew text provides no geographic details beyond "Mitsrayim (Egypt)."

**Source:** Gabra, G., "The Holy Family in Egypt," in *Christianity and Monasticism in Upper Egypt*, ed. Gabra and Takla, 2008.

### F2. "Natsri" etymology debate in later tradition
**[LATER RECEPTION — DOCUMENTED]**

The designation *Nazōraios* / *Natsri* generated early debate:
- **Jerome** (4th-5th century): connected it to *netser* ("branch," Isa 11:1) in his commentary on Matthew.
- **Eusebius** (*Onomasticon*): identified Natseret (Nazareth) geographically.
- **Epiphanius** (*Panarion*): described a pre-Christian Jewish sect called "Nasaraioi/Nazoraioi," though the reliability of this report is debated.
- In **rabbinic literature**, *notsri* (נוצרי) became the standard Hebrew term for "Christian," derived from Nazareth. In modern Hebrew, *Natsrut* = Christianity.

The question of where the word comes from remains open among scholars. No single solution has achieved consensus.

**Source:** France, R.T., *The Gospel of Matthew*, NICNT, 2007, pp. 93-95. Schaeder, H.H., "Ναζαρηνός, Ναζωραῖος," in *TDNT*, vol. 4, pp. 874-879.

## G. Curiosities and Open Questions

### G1. No biblical source for "He shall be called a Natsri"
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

This is the most distinctive feature of Matthew 2:23. Every other fulfillment formula in Matthew cites a known OT passage (often modified or combined, but identifiable). The "Natsri" quotation has no clear single source. The plural "the prophets" may be Matthew's signal that this is a thematic summary rather than a direct quotation. The mystery has generated centuries of scholarly discussion without resolution.

### G2. The star — narrated without explanation
**[TEXTUAL — VERIFIED]**

The star "goes before them" and "stands over" the place where the child is (v.9). The text narrates this behavior without explanation or astronomical comment. Various identifications have been proposed in modern discussion:
- A conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn in Pisces (7 BCE) — calculated by Kepler in 1604
- A comet — Halley's comet appeared in 12 BCE (too early for most chronologies)
- A supernova
- A non-natural phenomenon narrated within a theological framework

The text shows no interest in the mechanism. The star functions narratively as divine guidance for the *magoi*. The TT translates what the text says without scientific rationalization.

### G3. "Two years and under" — timeline implications
**[STRONG INFERENCE — PROBABLE]**

Herodes (Herod)' order to kill children "from two years old and under, according to the time which he had determined from the *magoi*" (v.16) implies that the star appeared up to two years before the *magoi* arrive in Yerushalayim (Jerusalem). This suggests an interval between the star's first appearance and the *magoi*'s arrival. Combined with the term *paidion* ("child," not "baby") at v.11, and the setting in a "house" (not a manger), the text places the *magoi*'s visit some time after the birth, possibly months or even a year or more.

### SCENARIO A — Pre-70 CE (Temple still standing)

#### IA-1. Political — Herod's paranoia and the mechanics of client-king violence
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Matthew 2 is set in the final years of Herod the Great (died c. 4 BCE), a period Josephus documents as marked by intense family violence and political suspicion. Herod executed his wife Mariamne I, her mother Alexandra, and three of his sons (Alexander, Aristobulus, Antipater) on suspicion of conspiracy (*Antiquities* 15–17; *War* 1). His secret order at the end of his life — that notable men be arrested and executed at his death so there would be mourning — illustrates a mind for whom control extended beyond death (*Antiquities* 17.6.5). The massacre of male children in Bethlehem is not mentioned by Josephus, but given Bethlehem's small population (perhaps 300–1,000 inhabitants), the number of male children under two would have been very small (10–20 at most). A local atrocity of this scale might not register in Josephus's broader account. The narrative is consistent with Herod's documented character; its historical occurrence cannot be confirmed or denied from external sources.

**Source:** Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities* 14–17, Loeb Classical Library; Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 2.4.11; Brown, R.E., *The Birth of the Messiah*, updated ed., Doubleday, 1993.

#### IA-2. Neighboring Peoples — Magi, Babylonian astronomy, and Persian religious specialists
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The *magoi* of 2:1–12 correspond to a known figure in the ancient world: Eastern religious specialists, primarily associated with Persian and Babylonian traditions. Herodotus identifies the *magoi* as a Median priestly tribe specializing in dream interpretation and ritual sacrifice (*Histories* 1.101, 1.132). By the Hellenistic and Roman periods, *magos* covered astrologers, astronomers, and diviners from anywhere in the East. Babylonian astronomy — the most sophisticated in the ancient world — had been tracking planetary movements, conjunctions, and stellar phenomena for centuries. Jupiter-Saturn conjunctions (occurring in Pisces in 7 BCE) and other celestial events have been proposed as candidates for the "star." The *magoi*'s association with star observation connects to the Babylonian tradition of reading celestial signs as omens for earthly events. Their Gentile identity (they are neither Jewish nor Roman) makes their recognition of a Jewish king's birth globally significant in Matthew's framing: the nations acknowledge what Israel's own ruler (Herod) fears.

**Source:** Herodotus, *Histories* 1.101, 1.132, trans. A.D. Godley, Loeb Classical Library, 1920; Hunger, H. and Pingree, D., *Astral Sciences in Mesopotamia*, Brill, 1999.

#### IA-3. Political — Beyt-Lechem as David's city and the messianic geography
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

Beyt-Lechem (Bethlehem) carried enormous symbolic weight as David's hometown (1 Sam 16:1–13, 17:12) and the setting of the book of Ruth. Micah 5:1 [5:2] identified it as the origin of a future ruler "whose origin is from of old, from ancient days," making it the prophetically designated birthplace of the messianic king. The chief priests and scribes cite this verse when Herod asks where the anointed one is to be born (2:5–6) — a scene that reveals the irony: the experts in Scripture know the answer; the one positioned to welcome the king uses the information to try to destroy him. Bethlehem's smallness is theologically important: the significant ruler emerges from insignificance (Micah's reversal — "too small to be among the clans" becomes "by no means least among the rulers" in Matthew's modified citation, 2:6). Pre-70 readers would feel the irony fully: the living city of David is an agricultural backwater; the true heir of David grows up in Galilee.

**Source:** Finkelstein, I. and Magen, Y. (eds.), *Archaeological Survey of the Hill Country of Benjamin*, Israel Antiquities Authority, 1993; Brown, R.E., *The Birth of the Messiah*, updated ed., Doubleday, 1993.

#### IA-4. Daily Life — Refugee flight and the Egypt route
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — VERIFIED]**

The flight to Egypt (2:13–15) follows a well-traveled ancient route: the coastal road from Gaza through the Sinai peninsula to the Nile Delta, approximately 400 km on foot, achievable in about two weeks. Jewish communities in Egypt were long-established and extensive — Alexandria alone may have held 150,000–200,000 Jews by the 1st century CE, with their own synagogues, communal courts, and a Temple at Leontopolis (founded c. 160 BCE). A Jewish family arriving from Judea would have found established networks of support. The precedent of flight to Egypt runs through the Hebrew Bible: Abraham (Gen 12:10), Jacob's family (Gen 46), and refugees after Jerusalem's fall (Jer 43–44). When Matthew's narrator says "out of Egypt I called my son" (2:15, citing Hos 11:1), the Egypt-departure pattern is being recognized as a repeated form in Israel's story, now applied to Jesus.

**Source:** Modrzejewski, J.M., *The Jews of Egypt: From Rameses II to Emperor Hadrian*, Princeton University Press, 1995; Tcherikover, V., *Hellenistic Civilization and the Jews*, Jewish Publication Society, 1959.

### SCENARIO B — Post-70 CE (Temple destroyed)

#### IB-1. Political — Herod's violence in the memory of post-70 Jewish experience
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Post-70 readers of Matthew 2 had lived through — or had parents who had lived through — violence that dwarfed Herod's local paranoia. The Roman destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE killed tens of thousands and enslaved or scattered the Judean population. The Arch of Titus in Rome commemorated the looting of the Temple's sacred vessels. Reading Herod's massacre of Bethlehem's male children in this context brought a different weight: it was a type or anticipation of the larger Roman massacre, with Yeshua (Jesus)'s survival figuring the hope for what continues after catastrophe. The Rachel-mourning citation (2:18, Jer 31:15) would have resonated differently for communities that had experienced mass death and forced exile in living memory. Jeremiah 31 itself moves from Rachel's weeping to the promise of restoration (31:16–17) — a trajectory that post-70 communities needed as much as the original exiles.

**Source:** Goodman, M., *Rome and Jerusalem: The Clash of Ancient Civilizations*, Allen Lane, 2007; Boyarin, D., *The Jewish Gospels*, New Press, 2012.

#### IB-2. Neighboring Peoples — Diaspora Judaism and the Gentile mission after 70 CE
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

The Magi's visit in Matthew 2 — Gentiles from the East who recognize and worship the king of the Yehudim — functions as an anticipation of the Great Commission (28:19: "make disciples of all the nations"). For post-70 readers, the Gentile mission was not future prospect but present reality. Jewish-Christian communities in the Diaspora were increasingly Gentile in composition; the question of how Gentiles related to the Torah and the promises of Israel had been central to Paul's letters (c. 50–60 CE) and continued to be debated. The *magoi* as Gentile truth-seekers who find their way to the Jewish messiah before Israel's own authorities is a narrative of the Gentile mission in miniature. The geography — East to Judea to Egypt and back — also maps the Diaspora world in which Matthew's Gospel likely circulated. **[PROBABLE that the Magi story functioned as a legitimation of Gentile inclusion for post-70 communities.]**

**Source:** Barclay, J.M.G., *Jews in the Mediterranean Diaspora*, T&T Clark, 1996; Overman, J.A., *Matthew's Gospel and Formative Judaism*, Fortress Press, 1990.

#### IB-3. Daily Life — The flight to Egypt as refugee experience after 70 CE
**[HISTORICAL / ARCHAEOLOGICAL — PROBABLE]**

Post-70 Jewish refugees would read the flight to Egypt narrative with first-hand understanding. The Jewish War of 66–70 CE displaced enormous numbers of Judean Jews: some fled to Galilee, Syria, Egypt, and the eastern Diaspora; others were sold into slavery or worked to death building the Colosseum and other Roman monuments. Egypt remained a destination for Jewish refugees — the Jewish community there had roots going back centuries. The Leontopolis Temple continued to operate until 73 CE, when it was closed by Vespasian. A family fleeing Herodian violence to Egypt is a narrative immediately comprehensible to communities that had themselves fled or absorbed refugees from Judean violence. Matthew's typological use of the Egypt-and-return pattern (Hos 11:1, "Out of Egypt I called my son") speaks to communities who understood displacement not as theological abstraction but as lived geography.

**Source:** Tcherikover, V., *Hellenistic Civilization and the Jews*, Jewish Publication Society, 1959; Josephus, *Jewish War* 7.10.1 (on the closing of the Leontopolis temple).
