## Micah 5:1 + 2 Samuel 5:2 — Bethlehem birthplace (Matt 2:5–6)

**Verse:** Matthew 2:5–6
**Text says:** "In Bethlehem of Judah, for thus it has been written through the prophet: 'And you, Bethlehem, land of Judah, are by no means least among the rulers of Judah; for out of you will come a ruler who will shepherd my people Yisra'el.'"
**Context:** Herod has gathered the chief priests and scribes to ask where the anointed one is born. They reply by citing a composite quotation that blends Mic 5:1 (the Bethlehem birthplace) with 2 Sam 5:2 (the shepherding-Israel formula). The citation answers Herod's question and motivates the magi's journey to Bethlehem.
**Subject:** The future ruler from Bethlehem — Matthew applies the composite citation to Yeshua. Mic 5:1 in its original setting describes a Davidic ruler emerging from Bethlehem (David's birthplace) during the Assyrian crisis of the 8th c. BCE; 2 Sam 5:2 describes YHWH's commission to David himself.
**Fulfillment status:** PARTIAL
**Fulfillment notes:** Per Entry M-001 §Composite, Matthew blends two distinct OT sources: Mic 5:1 (the geographic claim) and 2 Sam 5:2 (the shepherd-of-Israel formula). The Micah text is also modified — the Hebrew "least of the clans of Judah" is reversed to "by no means least," producing a positive rather than negative-by-contrast statement about Bethlehem. The Davidic-descent claim implicit in this composite (Yeshua born in Bethlehem, descended from David) is independently supported by the Matt 1 genealogy and is the operative force of the citation. The PARTIAL fulfillment label reflects: Matthew's specific application is CLAIMED; the underlying Davidic-from-Bethlehem expectation is broader than Yeshua alone (other Davidic claimants could in principle satisfy the same prophecy). → See `docs/editorial-log/matthew.md` Entry M-001 §Composite for the layer-by-layer textual analysis (certain modification + probable 2 Sam echo + possible interpretive adjustment).
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Mic 5:1 is read as a prophecy of a Davidic ruler emerging from Bethlehem during a specific historical crisis (Assyrian threat, 8th c. BCE). Most Jewish interpreters do not read it as messianic in the Christian sense; some rabbinic and later Jewish messianic traditions do anticipate a Davidic figure from Bethlehem, but the identification with Yeshua is rejected. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The composite citation has been read since the second century as decisive proof of Yeshua's messianic identity. The geographical specificity (Bethlehem, not Nazareth where Yeshua was raised) is treated as evidence that the gospel narrative aligns with prophetic expectation. The Davidic-descent claim is reinforced by the Matt 1 genealogy. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: The Quran identifies Isa (Jesus) as born in a specific place (Quran 19:22–23 describes the location only as where Maryam gave birth, traditionally identified by some Islamic scholars with Bethlehem but not so named in the text). Islamic tradition does not engage Matthew's specific composite citation. [DOCUMENTED]

## Hosea 11:1 — "Out of Egypt I called my son" (Matt 2:15)

**Verse:** Matthew 2:15
**Text says:** "And was there until the death of Herod, so that what was spoken by the Lord through the prophet might be fulfilled, saying, 'Out of Egypt I called my son.'"
**Context:** After the magi depart, an angelic dream warns Yosef (Joseph) to flee to Egypt with Miryam and the child. They stay there until Herod's death. The narrator inserts the fulfillment formula at the moment of return from Egypt — not at the flight to Egypt — applying Hos 11:1 retrospectively to the family's return.
**Subject:** The "son" called out of Egypt — Hosea's original referent is Israel as a corporate "son" of YHWH (Hos 11:1 reads as historical narration of the Exodus, not future-tense prophecy); Matthew applies typologically to Yeshua as the new "son" who repeats Israel's pattern.
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED
**Fulfillment notes:** Per Entry M-001 §Typological, Hosea 11:1 in its original context is a historical statement about Israel's exodus, not a future-tense prophecy. The Hebrew reads "When Israel was a youth, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son" — a backward-looking declaration about the historical Exodus from Mitsrayim. Matthew's reapplication is typological: the new "son" (Yeshua) repeats the pattern of the original "son" (Israel). This typological reading is attested in Second Temple Jewish interpretation as a hermeneutical method, even when its specific applications differ. The CLAIMED label reflects: Matthew claims the typological connection explicitly via the fulfillment formula; the connection is one of pattern, not direct future-tense prophecy. → See `docs/editorial-log/matthew.md` Entry M-001 §Typological for the full Hosea-original-context vs. Matthew-application analysis.
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Hosea 11:1 is read straightforwardly as historical narration — YHWH brought Israel out of Egypt as his "son" (corporate Israel). No future-tense prophetic dimension is read in the original text. Matthew's typological reapplication to Yeshua is rejected; the text describes the past, not the future. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The typological reading is foundational to Christian use of the Hebrew Bible. The pattern — Israel as God's "son" called out of Egypt, Yeshua as the new "son" repeating the pattern — is read as evidence of God's consistent narrative purpose across both Testaments. Origen, Eusebius, and later Christian interpreters elaborated the typology. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: The Quran affirms Isa's flight or refuge with Maryam (Quran 23:50 describes them being given refuge on "high ground with water and shade") but does not engage Hosea 11:1 or Matthew's typological framework. [DOCUMENTED]

## Jeremiah 31:15 — Rachel weeping for her children (Matt 2:17–18)

**Verse:** Matthew 2:17–18
**Text says:** "Then was fulfilled what was spoken through Yirmeyahu the prophet, saying: 'A voice in Ramah was heard, weeping and much lamentation — Rachel weeping for her children, and she would not be comforted, because they are not.'"
**Context:** Herod, outwitted by the magi, orders the massacre of male children in Bethlehem and its vicinity. The narrator inserts a distinctive fulfillment formula — τότε ἐπληρώθη ("then was fulfilled") rather than the purpose-clause ἵνα πληρωθῇ — and cites Jeremiah 31:15. The formula choice is theologically significant: the massacre is not presented as divinely intended to fulfill prophecy; it is presented as an event that, having occurred, corresponds to the prophetic lament.
**Subject:** Rachel weeping for her children — Jeremiah's original context is the Babylonian exile, with Rachel (the matriarch buried at Ramah, between Bethel and Bethlehem per Gen 35:19) symbolically weeping as her descendants are deported. Matthew applies the lament typologically to the mothers of Bethlehem grieving their massacred infants.
**Fulfillment status:** CLAIMED
**Fulfillment notes:** Per Entry M-001 §Temporal-resultive, Matthew's fulfillment formula here is distinctive: τότε ἐπληρώθη ("then was fulfilled") rather than ἵνα πληρωθῇ ("so that it might be fulfilled"). The distinction is theologically significant: the purpose-clause formulas (1:22, 2:15, 2:23, 3:3) present the events as divinely intended to fulfill prophecy; the temporal-resultive formula at 2:17 presents the massacre as an event that, having occurred, corresponds to the prophetic lament — not as something God willed in order to fulfill the prophecy. Jeremiah 31:15's original context is the Babylonian exile (c. 587 BCE); Rachel, buried at Ramah (Gen 35:19), is the symbolic mother weeping for her exiled descendants. Matthew's reapplication is typological lament rather than predictive prophecy. → See `docs/editorial-log/matthew.md` Entry M-001 §Temporal-resultive for the formula-distinction analysis.
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: Jeremiah 31:15 is read in its exilic context — Rachel symbolically grieving for the deported descendants of Israel (the Northern Kingdom in 722 BCE; the Babylonian exile in 587 BCE). The verse is part of a larger oracle (Jer 31) that promises return and restoration; the lament is followed by hope (Jer 31:16–17). Matthew's application to the Bethlehem massacre is read as a typological appropriation, not original prophetic intent. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The temporal-resultive formula is widely recognized as Matthew's most restrained fulfillment claim. Patristic and later Christian interpreters have generally read this as Matthew acknowledging that the massacre was an evil act that corresponds to a previous lament pattern — not as God willing the deaths to fulfill prophecy. The reading respects the Jewish original-context concern while affirming Matthew's typological framework. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: The Quran does not engage Jeremiah 31:15 or Matthew 2:16–18 directly. The Bethlehem massacre is not narrated in the Quran; Islamic tradition holds that the infant Isa was protected from harm without engaging this specific Matthean episode. [DOCUMENTED]

## Matt 2:23 — "He shall be called a Natsri" (unresolved source)

**Verse:** Matthew 2:23
**Text says:** "And coming, he settled in a city called Natseret (Nazareth), so that what was spoken through the prophets might be fulfilled: 'He shall be called a Natsri.'"
**Context:** After Yosef returns from Egypt and learns that Archelaos reigns in Judah, he is warned in a dream and withdraws to Galilee, settling in Natseret (Nazareth). The narrator inserts a fulfillment formula whose source is genuinely unidentified — no extant Hebrew Bible or Second Temple text matches the citation as Matthew presents it.
**Subject:** The "Natsri" — the term itself is contested. Three main proposals: (a) connection to *netzer* ("branch") in Isa 11:1, applied messianically; (b) connection to the Nazirite vow (Judg 13:5/7), though Yeshua is never narratively presented as a Nazirite; (c) reference to a lost prophetic text or oral tradition no longer extant. Matthew's plural "prophets" may signal that the citation is a composite or summary rather than a single source.
**Fulfillment status:** DEBATED
**Fulfillment notes:** Per Entry M-001 §Unresolved, no known single OT source matches Matthew's citation. The plural "prophets" (διὰ τῶν προφητῶν) is unusual — Matthew's other fulfillment formulas typically cite a single named prophet. Three proposed sources, each with confidence labels per Rule 13: Isa 11:1 *netzer* ("branch") with messianic application — POSSIBLE; Judg 13:5/7 Nazirite vow — UNCERTAIN (Yeshua is not narratively presented as a Nazirite, undermining the connection); a lost prophetic text or summary tradition — POSSIBLE, since the plural "prophets" suggests Matthew may be summarizing rather than quoting verbatim. The TT does not resolve the source per the Prime Directive (don't resolve what the text leaves uncertain); the DEBATED label reflects that the source itself is genuinely unidentified, not that Matthew's claim is contested. → See `docs/editorial-log/matthew.md` Entry M-001 §Unresolved for the three-proposal analysis with confidence labels.
**Readings:**
- **Jewish**: The Natsri citation is read as evidence that Matthew is working with a tradition not present in the canonical Hebrew Bible. The plural "prophets" is read as either a composite from multiple sources or as a summary of a tradition no longer extant. The Isa 11:1 *netzer* connection is acknowledged as a possible play on the consonants but is not read as a fulfillment claim Jewish interpreters accept. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Christian**: The most influential Christian reading connects Natsri to Isa 11:1's *netzer* (Hebrew "branch"), reading the citation as a messianic title meaning "the branch" — a Davidic descendant. Other patristic and modern interpreters have proposed the Nazirite connection or a lost source. The general pattern (Yeshua's settlement in Natseret fulfilling some scriptural anticipation) is affirmed; the specific source remains debated. [DOCUMENTED]
- **Islamic**: The Quran does not engage Matthew 2:23 or the Natsri citation. Isa's location of upbringing is not a Quranic concern; the Islamic narrative focuses on his prophetic identity rather than geographic typology. [DOCUMENTED]
