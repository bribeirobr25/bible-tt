# How to Read the Transparent Translation

A short guide for readers and collaborators.

---

## What this is

The Transparent Translation (TT) is a translation of the Hebrew Bible (from the Masoretic Text) and the Greek Scriptures (from the Greek New Testament) into English, Brazilian Portuguese, German, and Spanish. It is governed by 30 rules designed to show you what the source text says, what it may mean, and where it remains open.

It is not a devotional Bible. It is not a replacement for scholarly editions. It is not a commentary. It is a disciplined attempt at transparency.

---

## What makes it different

Most translations make choices silently — smoothing ambiguity, adding words for readability, choosing one meaning where several exist, importing later theology into earlier text. The TT makes all of this visible.

---

## How to read the notation

The TT translates two source texts: the **Hebrew Bible** (Hebrew, with some Aramaic) and the **Greek Scriptures** (the New Testament, in Greek). "The source" below means whichever of the two a book is translated from.

Four markers appear in the verse text. They are the only styling the verse text uses.

| Marker | Looks like | Means | Rule |
|--------|-----------|-------|------|
| *Italics* | grammatical-blue italic | a word **added for grammar** — it is not in the source | 11 |
| Transliterated term | **teal, bold** | a source word kept in its original form | 4 |
| Slash (wind/spirit) | **bold**, body colour | two valid meanings kept open | 2 |
| Divine speech | red, not bold | God (or, in the Greek Scriptures, Jesus) speaking directly | 30 |

### *Italics* — added words

Words in italics were **added for grammar** — they are not in the source text.

> "And God saw that ***it was*** good"

Hebrew says "and-saw God that good." The words *it was* were added so the English sentence works. Italics mark every such addition. Where a language fuses an added article into a contraction (Portuguese *na*, German *im*, Spanish *del*), the whole contraction is italic.

### Slashes (wind/spirit) — preserved ambiguity

When a source word has two or more valid meanings and context does not force one, both are shown with a slash, in bold.

> "**wind/spirit** of God was hovering"

Hebrew **רוּחַ** (*ruach*) means wind, spirit, and breath; Greek **πνεῦμα** (*pneuma*) has the same range. The TT does not choose for you.

### Teal terms — transliterations (raqia, nephilim, tebah)

Some source words are kept in their original form because every translation would import false meaning. They are shown in teal and bold. **Raqia** is not "firmament" (too medieval) or "expanse" (too modern). It is *raqia* — explained in the notes. In the Greek Scriptures this is rare (for example *Hakeldama*, "Field of Blood", Acts 1:19).

### Red text — God speaking

When the text presents God speaking directly — and, in the Greek Scriptures, Jesus speaking, or the Spirit where the text gives it speech — the quoted words are shown in red. Narration *about* God is not marked, and neither are God's words quoted by someone else (a prophet, an apostle). Where the speaker's identity is open (the "messenger of YHWH"), the text is not marked. The colour shows who is speaking, not what the words mean.

### YHWH

In the Hebrew Bible the divine name is rendered consonantally — **YHWH** in English, Portuguese, and Spanish, **JHWH** in German. Not "the LORD." Not "God." The four Hebrew consonants, because that is what the text has. The original pronunciation is lost.

### "The Lord" in the Greek Scriptures

The Greek text never contains YHWH; it has **κύριος** (*kyrios*, "lord/master"). The TT translates what the Greek says: "the Lord" (o Senhor, der Herr, el Señor). When the Greek quotes a Hebrew Bible passage that had YHWH, a note says so. When *kyrios* refers to Jesus or to a human master, the note says which; where the referent is debated, the note says that too.

### The Yehudim

In the Greek Scriptures, **οἱ Ἰουδαῖοι** (*hoi Ioudaioi*) is rendered **the Yehudim** (os Yehudim, los Yehudim; German **die Jehudim**). "The Jews" carries a long and harmful history of misuse, especially in John; "the Judeans" narrows the word too far. A note at each occurrence says which sense is active: the Jerusalem authorities, the Jewish people more broadly, or the people of Judea.

## Three doors to each chapter

Every chapter has three pages, each with its own address. Switch between them with the Read · Notes · Deeper bar at the top of the chapter.

### Read

`/{language}/{book}/chapter/{n}`

Continuous prose with superscript verse numbers. Clean page. No notes. Read it like a story. The text flows from beginning to end without interruption.

This is the closest to how the source narrative reads — one continuous stream, not isolated verses.

### Notes

`/{language}/{book}/chapter/{n}/notes`

Verse by verse. Each verse card shows the main text and, below it, collapsible notes organized by type:

- **Critical** — major textual or translation decisions (what we chose and why)
- **Lexical** — word meanings, roots, glossary connections
- **Grammatical** — source structure, verb forms, syntax preserved or adapted
- **Theological** — interpretive options, ambiguity preservation, what the text leaves open

Below the verses: the chapter glossary and supplementary analysis (patterns across the chapter). Each verse has its own link (`#v{n}`), so you can point someone to a single verse.

### Deeper

`/{language}/{book}/chapter/{n}/deeper` — only for chapters that have background material.

The contextual study companion, with a **Prophecies** tab where a chapter has one, and a link to the book's **People and Genealogy** page. The companion covers, as available:

- **Source Text Features** — what the transparent rendering makes visible
- **Ancient Parallels** — texts from neighboring cultures (for the Hebrew Bible: Mesopotamia, Egypt, Ugarit; for the Greek Scriptures: Second Temple Jewish and Greco-Roman sources)
- **Historical & Archaeological** — material culture, dating, ancient settings
- **Linguistic Deep Dives** — rare words, cognates, semantic fields
- **Scientific Correspondence** — what the text presents vs. what modern science describes (neither proving nor disproving)
- **Later Reception** — how Jewish, Christian, and Islamic traditions have read these passages (clearly labeled as post-biblical)
- **Curiosities** — text-linked observations and open questions
- **The World at the Time** — politics, trade, daily life and ideas when the text was written or set

Every entry carries two labels: **what kind of claim** (Textual, Comparative Parallel, Later Reception, etc.) and **how certain** (Verified, Probable, Possible, Uncertain, Speculative).

The companion draws no conclusions. You do.

### Book pages

Each book also has a landing page with an at-a-glance card, the full **Introduction**, **People and Genealogy**, and **Background** (themes that run across chapters). Start at `/{language}/start` for a suggested reading order, or `/{language}/books` for all books. The 30 rules are at `/{language}/rules`.

## What the TT intentionally refuses to do

- **Add "the" where the source has none.** "In beginning" — not "In the beginning." Hebrew *bereshit* has no article.
- **Resolve ambiguity.** If the source supports two readings and context doesn't force one, both stay. The slash is not laziness — it is honesty.
- **Import later theology.** The serpent in Genesis 3 is a *nachash* (serpent), not Satan. There is no "Fall" in the Hebrew text. The word "sin" does not appear until Genesis 4:7.
- **Smooth what sounds strange.** "Dying you shall die" — not "you shall surely die." "Seeding seed" — not "seed-bearing." The awkwardness mirrors the Hebrew.
- **Pretend certainty.** When the meaning of a word is genuinely unknown (like *ed* at Gen 2:6 or *shuf* at Gen 3:15), the note says so. Probable, Possible, Uncertain — the label is part of the honesty.

## What the strange bits mean

If a phrase sounds odd, it is probably preserving a Hebrew or Greek structure that other translations smooth over. The examples below are from Genesis.

| You read | Why it sounds strange | What it preserves |
|----------|----------------------|-------------------|
| "In beginning" | Missing "the" | Hebrew has no article here |
| "Shall be light" | Unusual English | Hebrew jussive (command-form) |
| "Seeding seed" | Repetitive | Hebrew root-doubling (poetic device) |
| "That good" | Incomplete sentence | Hebrew compression (no "it was") |
| "wind/spirit" | Unresolved | Hebrew word means both |
| "side/rib" | Two options | Hebrew word used architecturally elsewhere |
| "dying you shall die" | Emphatic doubling | Hebrew infinitive absolute (maximum force) |

If you're reading the Transparent Edition and something feels wrong — check the note. The strangeness is usually the point.

## For collaborators

If you're contributing to the project:

- **Start with** `docs/guides/AUTHORING-PLAYBOOK.md` — the runbook for a new chapter or book
- **Read** `docs/rules/RULES-CORE.md` + `RULES-HB.md` (Hebrew Bible) + `RULES-GS.md` (Greek Scriptures) (v3.4.1) — the 30-rule governance system
- **Read** `docs/design/TT-DESIGN-SYSTEM.md` — UI/UX standards
- **Read** `docs/architecture/STANDARDS.md` — code and architecture standards
- **Log decisions** in the book's own editorial log, `docs/editorial-log/{book}.md` (e.g. `genesis.md`, `john.md`, `1-peter.md`), before drafting
- **New glossary terms** go in the locked glossary of RULES-HB.md (Hebrew Bible) or RULES-GS.md (Greek Scriptures) before use
- **EN first,** then PT-BR, DE, and ES follow
- **Test:** run the gate in `CONTRIBUTING.md` (`pnpm test`, `pnpm lint`, `pnpm typecheck`, `pnpm content:lint`, `pnpm build`) after every change

## Languages

The TT is available in English, Brazilian Portuguese, German, and Spanish. Switch languages on any page with the language switcher at the top right. The page you are on is kept.

Each language follows the same rules, the same glossary, and the same ambiguity-preservation policies. Where one language must diverge from others due to grammar (e.g., German requires an article where the source and English don't), the divergence is documented.
